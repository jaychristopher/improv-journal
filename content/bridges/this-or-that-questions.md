# This or That Questions: 300 for Warming Up Any Room

This or that is the cheapest question format that exists. One word answers it. There is nothing to compose, nothing to prepare, and no way to give a bad answer — which makes it the right tool for exactly one job, and the wrong tool for most others.

## What It Is Actually For

The job is **lowering the cost of the first answer**.

A cold room is not short of things to say. It is unwilling to be the first to say them, because the first person to speak sets a length and a level that everybody else now has to match, and nobody wants that responsibility. So the room waits.

This or that removes the responsibility entirely. The answer is one word, everyone gives the same shape of answer, and nobody can be judged for it. Ten of these and a group that could not start has now spoken twenty times each, which is a completely different room from the one you walked into.

`safety-in-the-room` on this site is the precondition for anybody offering anything real. It is built cheaply, in small increments that go unpunished — and a one-word answer is the smallest increment available.

## This or That Is Not Would You Rather

They look identical and do opposite jobs. Worth being precise, because using the wrong one is the most common way this falls flat.

**Would you rather is a dilemma.** Both options cost something, the trade-off is real, and the point is the argument afterwards. It needs a warm room, because defending a choice in front of strangers is exposure.

**This or that is a preference.** Nothing is at stake, no defence is required, and the point is momentum rather than insight. Coffee or tea reveals almost nothing and that is the feature.

So: this or that first, [would you rather](/would-you-rather-questions) later — and if the room is already warm, skip straight past this page. Running twenty preference pairs at a group that was ready to talk properly is a way of wasting a good evening.

## The Rules That Keep It Moving

**No explaining, first time round.** The whole value is speed. Ask for reasons and the round slows to a crawl and starts feeling like a survey.

**Go back for the interesting ones.** Two or three pairs will produce a genuine split or a surprising answer. Note them, finish the round, then return to those and ask why. That is where the actual conversation is.

**Do not let anybody say both, or neither.** `commitment` — choosing without full information and backing it — is the small skill this trains. "Both" is a refusal, and one person doing it gives everybody permission.

**Ten to fifteen is a round.** Forty is an ordeal. The format has a short half-life and the second half is always weaker than the first.

## The Part You Can Practise

The reason this format works is that it removes the gap between being asked and answering, and that gap is where most self-consciousness lives.

`obvious-choice` says your first answer is connected to the question and your second is connected to how you want to appear. This or that is the only format that reliably gets the first one, because there is no time to produce a better-sounding alternative.

If you notice yourself hesitating even on coffee or tea — deciding what your answer says about you — that is the planning habit, and it is trainable. `quieting-the-planning-mind` is built for exactly that gap.
