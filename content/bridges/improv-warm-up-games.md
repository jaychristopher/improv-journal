# Improv Warm-Up Games: The Order Matters More Than the Game

Almost everybody asks the wrong question about warm-ups. The question is not which game to run. It is what order to run three of them in.

A room of people who have just arrived cannot do improv yet, and the reason is specific: they are still in the mode that got them through the day, which is a mode built on planning ahead, monitoring how they are coming across, and not looking stupid in front of colleagues. Every one of those habits is fatal on stage. A warm-up is the twenty minutes in which you dismantle them, and dismantling them has an order.

## Why a Random Game Often Does Nothing

Pick a game off a list, run it for ten minutes, and you will frequently get a room that is louder but no more able to play than when it walked in.

That happens because most warm-up lists are sorted by what the game *is* — circle games, name games, energy games — rather than by what it *does to the people playing it*. A group that is nervous does not need a game requiring invention. A group that is already loud and scattered does not need more energy. Running the wrong stage first produces a room that has been entertained rather than prepared.

The three stages below are not a rigid formula, and plenty of good teachers vary them. But they are ordered by what each one costs the player, from cheapest to most exposing, and that ordering is the part worth keeping.

## Stage One: Move Before You Ask Anybody to Think

**Four to six minutes. No ideas required.**

The first game should be impossible to be bad at and should demand nothing verbal. This is not about raising heart rates, though that happens. It is about getting people out of their heads by giving their attention somewhere physical to go before you ask them to invent anything.

- **[Pass the Clap](/practice/exercises/pass-the-clap)** — a clap travels round the circle on eye contact. The best opening game there is, because it needs no words and no ideas, only attention to one person at a time.
- **[Zip Zap Zop](/practice/exercises/zip-zap-zop)** — three words, passed with a pointed hand. Small enough to remember, fast enough that nobody is standing around.
- **[Bippity Bippity Bop](/practice/exercises/bippity-bippity-bop)** — a caller in the middle sets a race against a phrase. It fails people constantly and cheerfully, which is exactly what you want in the first five minutes.
- **[Big Booty](/practice/exercises/big-booty)** — a rhythm game that loads attention until deliberation becomes impossible. Best with a group that already knows each other, because it is unforgiving of hesitation.

The failure to watch for here is a group playing carefully. If people are getting the pattern right every time, the game is too easy to be doing anything — speed it up until the mistakes start.

## Stage Two: Put Their Attention on Each Other

**Five to eight minutes. Still no invention, but now it is shared.**

Stage one gets an individual out of their own head. Stage two is what turns a set of individuals into something that can play together — the thing this site calls `ensemble`, and it does not arrive by being asked for.

- **[Sound Ball](/practice/exercises/sound-ball)** — throw an imaginary ball with a sound, catch it with the same sound. The whole discipline is returning exactly what you were given rather than improving it.
- **[Group mind cultivation](/practice/exercises/group-mind-cultivation)** — counting to twenty, one voice at a time, restarting whenever two people speak at once. No system and no turn-taking is allowed, so the only way through is collective listening.
- **[Mirroring](/practice/exercises/mirroring)** — two players, slow movement, sustained eye contact, no speech. Spolin's core exercise and still the fastest way to make two people attend to each other completely.

The count to twenty is the diagnostic in this group. A room that gets to twenty on the second attempt is ready for anything. A room that keeps colliding at four has people in it who are waiting for a turn rather than listening, and that is worth naming out loud before you move on.

## Stage Three: Rehearse the Habit You Are About to Need

**Five to ten minutes. Now they invent, in the shape the session requires.**

This is the stage most warm-ups skip, and skipping it is why a group can finish a lively warm-up and then produce a first scene that dies. The last game before the work should be a small, safe version of the exact thing you are about to ask for.

- **[Yes, Let's](/practice/exercises/yes-lets)** — somebody proposes, everybody answers "Yes, let's!" in unison and does it. Four minutes, and it installs the physical habit of starting on somebody else's word rather than your own.
- **[One-Word Story](/practice/exercises/one-word-story)** — a story told one word at a time round the circle. Forgiving, because no individual is responsible for the story.
- **[Last Word Response](/practice/exercises/last-word-response)** — in a two-person scene, your first word must be your partner's last. It makes not-listening structurally impossible.

If the session is about accepting offers, finish on Yes, Let's. If it is about listening, finish on Last Word Response. If it is about narrative, finish on One-Word Story. The specific choice matters less than the fact that it points at the same skill the next hour is about.

## Matching the Warm-Up to the Room

The stages hold, but their weighting should change with who is in front of you.

**A group that has never met.** Spend longer in stage one than feels necessary and go easy on stage three. Strangers need failure to be visibly survivable before they will risk inventing anything, which is a matter of `safety-in-the-room` rather than of energy.

**A group that knows each other well.** You can compress stage one to a single game. What experienced players actually need is stage two — familiarity makes people confident, and confidence quietly erodes listening.

**A corporate or non-performer group.** Keep everything physical and rule-bound and be careful with anything that looks like being asked to be funny. The moment the task reads as *perform*, half the room decides they cannot win.

**A group about to go on stage.** This is a different job. You are not teaching, you are shifting state, so run games they already know well. A show warm-up is no place to introduce a rule.

## How Long, and How You Know It Worked

Fifteen to twenty minutes is the range that works for a class of two to three hours. Under ten and you have not moved anybody. Past twenty-five and you are spending the session's best attention on the preliminaries.

The test is not whether people are laughing. It is whether they are looking at each other. A warmed-up room makes eye contact without being told to, responds before it has composed a response, and has stopped checking who is watching. A room that is loud but still playing to the teacher has had a nice time and is not ready.

One reliable sign you have run it well: nobody asks what the next game is. They are still in the last one.

## The Argument for Skipping Warm-Ups Altogether

Worth stating, because it comes from serious performers rather than from lazy ones.

Plenty of experienced improvisers do not run structured warm-ups. They arrive, talk to each other, and go on. The claim underneath that practice is that what a warm-up produces — attention, mutual trust, a shared baseline — is available directly from familiarity, and that an ensemble which has played together for years does not need a game to manufacture something it already has.

That is largely right about ensembles and largely wrong about classes, which is the distinction that resolves it. A long-standing group genuinely may not need the ritual. A room of people who met twenty minutes ago has nothing else to build the baseline out of.

Spolin's position cuts deeper than either, though, and is the reason the games survive: the games are not preliminary to the work, they *are* the methodology. Each one sets a point of concentration that occupies the analytical mind and leaves the spontaneous one free. On that account a warm-up is not a runway. It is a short version of the thing itself.

## Questions About Improv Warm-Ups

### How long should a warm-up be?

Fifteen to twenty minutes for a class of two to three hours, and closer to ten for a show. The useful check is proportion rather than clock time: much past a fifth of your total session and the warm-up has become the session.

### What if the group is too self-conscious to play?

Stay in stage one longer and pick games that fail people constantly. Self-consciousness does not respond to reassurance; it responds to watching everybody else get it wrong first and nothing bad happening. Bippity Bippity Bop earns its place here for precisely that reason.

### Can you warm up with only two or three people?

Yes, but the circle games do not survive the shrink. Mirroring and Last Word Response work with two and are arguably better in a pair, since neither has anywhere to hide. Sound Ball works with three.

### Should the warm-up relate to what you are teaching?

The last game should; the first two do not need to. That is the whole argument for the three-stage order — the opening games are about state, and only the closing one is about content.

### Do these need a large space?

No. Everything in stage one and stage three works in a circle in a classroom with the tables pushed back. Only Mirroring really wants room to move, and even that can be done standing still.
