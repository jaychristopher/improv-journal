# Questions to Get to Know Someone: 100 That Start Small

Almost every list of questions to get to know someone is a list of *deep* questions, on the theory that a better question produces a closer conversation. With somebody you have just met, that gets it exactly backwards.

`trust` on this site is an accumulated estimate — built from many small exchanges — of whether it is safe to be exposed around a particular person. With a stranger the estimate is at zero. You cannot draw on it, because there is nothing there. All you can do is make deposits, and a deposit is small by definition.

So the goal in a first conversation is not depth. It is **a series of increments that go unpunished** — each one slightly more revealing than the last, each one met without judgement, each one nudging the estimate up. Do that six times and you are somewhere real. Open with "what is your greatest fear" and you are somewhere polite for the rest of the evening.

## Ask for a Story, Not a Fact

The second thing that separates a question that works from one that dies.

"What do you do?" requests a fact. The honest answer is one noun and there is nowhere to go afterwards — which is why that conversation always needs rescuing about nine seconds later.

"How did you end up doing that?" requests a story. It has a beginning and some decisions in it, and stories are the only format in which people accidentally say something true about themselves.

This is `obvious-choice` applied to conversation: the plain question gets the real answer, because it is easy to answer honestly. The interesting question gets a performance, because the other person can tell it is a test and starts composing.

And every question is an `offers` — a thing you hand somebody to build with. Judge yours by what they can do with it, not by how it sounds when you ask it.

## Opening Questions

The first ten minutes. Specific, easy, and impossible to get wrong.

- What brought you here tonight?
- How do you know the people here?
- Where did you come from to get here?
- Is this the sort of thing you usually come to?
- What does the rest of your week look like?
- Where do you live, and how did you end up there?
- What is the best thing about that part of town?
- How has your day been up to now?
- What is the last thing you ate that you would recommend?
- Are you a morning person, honestly?
- What is your commute?
- What do you do with a free Saturday?
- Have you got anything coming up you are looking forward to?
- What is the last thing you watched that you would tell someone about?
- What are you reading, if anything?
- What is the last thing you said yes to without thinking about it?
- What is something you did recently for the first time?
- What is the weather doing where you grew up?
- What is your usual order somewhere?
- What is on in the background when you are at home?

## Questions That Move Past Small Talk

The transition is one move: pick something they mentioned in passing and ask about *that* rather than the topic they offered.

- What made you pick that?
- What is the part of that you actually enjoy?
- What did you expect to dislike and end up enjoying?
- What is the bit people always get wrong about it?
- What would you be doing if that had not worked out?
- What is something you have changed your mind about recently?
- What are you working on at the moment that is not work?
- What is something you are trying to get better at?
- What is a small thing you are unreasonably good at?
- What is something you used to care about a lot and no longer do?
- What is taking up most of your head at the moment?
- What is the last thing that genuinely surprised you?
- What would you do with a year off?
- What do you do with an evening that suddenly has nothing in it?
- What is a rule you have for yourself?
- What is something you have been putting off?
- What is something you keep going back and forth on?
- What is the last thing you made?
- What is something you have got wrong about yourself?
- What do you do that makes you lose track of time?

## Questions About How They Got Here

People are far more forthcoming about the past than the present, and the past is where the explanation for the present lives.

- Where did you grow up, and what was that like?
- What did you assume adult life would involve that it does not?
- What is the first thing you remember being trusted with?
- What did you study, and would you do it again?
- How did you end up in this line of work?
- What was the first big decision you made on your own?
- Who influenced you before you noticed it was happening?
- What is a place you have lived that you think about?
- What made you move here?
- What did your family do?
- What was the first thing you bought with your own money?
- What is something you were obsessed with as a kid?
- What did you do after school before anyone got home?
- What is a job you had that you would not do again?
- What is the longest you have stayed anywhere?
- What was the last thing you learned properly rather than dabbled in?
- What did you nearly become?
- Who did you look up to that you would not now?
- What is a risk you took that worked?
- What is one that did not?

## Questions That Find What They Care About

By this point the estimate has moved. These probe values without asking for a summary of them.

- What is something you would defend that most people would not?
- What do you find it hard to stay quiet about?
- What is a small injustice that irritates you disproportionately?
- What do you think most people are wrong about?
- What is something you have quietly stopped participating in?
- What would you do more of if there were more hours?
- Who do you admire that nobody has heard of?
- What is worth spending money on?
- What is a thing you are always trying to convince people of?
- What do you want more of in your life at the moment?
- What is enough, for you?
- What is something you keep going back to?
- What is the best thing you have been part of?
- What do you hope is still true about you in twenty years?
- What do you think you are for?
- What is a piece of advice you would give that you have not taken?
- What do you notice that other people do not?
- What would you fix first if you could fix one thing?
- What is something you have changed your mind about that mattered?
- What do you hope somebody asks you?

## Questions for a Group or a Party

Different constraint: several people, short attention, nobody wants to be interviewed. These are answerable in a sentence and everybody can join in.

- What is the hardest work you have ever done?
- What is something everyone here has done that nobody would admit to?
- What is the best thing you have eaten this year?
- What was everyone doing at twenty?
- What is a film everyone else loves and you do not?
- What is the last thing you all argued about?
- What is something you can do that never comes up?
- What is something you will not switch away from?
- What is something small that always works on a bad day?
- What job did you not know existed until you met someone doing it?
- What do you always have with you?
- What is a thing you would happily never do again?
- What has taken you longest to learn?
- Which place do you keep meaning to go back to?
- What is something everyone here learned the hard way?
- What is a hill you would die on?
- What is the last photo on your phone, described not shown?
- What is something you learned this year?
- What is the best thing about where you live?
- What does an ordinary Sunday look like for you?

## Questions That Do Not Work on a Stranger

Each of these fails specifically because there is no trust yet.

- **"What's your greatest fear?"** — asks for a withdrawal from an account with nothing in it. You get the socially acceptable fear.
- **"Tell me about yourself."** — enormous, unanchored, and the answer is always a CV.
- **"What do you do?"** — not banned, but it requests a fact and has nowhere to go. Follow it immediately with how they got there.
- **"What's your biggest weakness?"** — an interview question. So is anything shaped like one.
- **"Are you seeing anyone?"** — asks somebody to account for their private life to a person they met eleven minutes ago.
- **"How much do you earn?"** and anything adjacent to it — including where they went on holiday and where their children go to school.
- **"What's your Myers-Briggs / star sign / love language?"** — asks for a self-classification. Categories end conversations; specifics open them.
- **"Why?"** as a bare follow-up. It reads as a challenge. "What was that like?" gets the same information with none of the edge.
- **"Can I ask you something personal?"** — announces the withdrawal before making it, and the announcement is what makes people tense.
- **Rapid-fire anything.** Three questions with no reaction in between is an interrogation, however friendly the tone.

## How to Ask Them

**Answer your own question, briefly, before or after.** A question asked without any reciprocity is an interview. `safety-in-the-room` is the condition under which people risk being seen without armour, and with a stranger you are the only one who can create it, because you are the only variable they can read.

**Follow up on the detail, not the topic.** Somebody says "we moved here after my dad got ill." The topic is moving house. The detail is the dad. Nine people out of ten ask about the house.

**Receive, do not assess.** `be-thankful` here means treating what arrives as usable rather than judging it. Reacting to how surprising or heavy an answer was — rather than to what was in it — is how people learn where your limits are and stay inside them.

**Let a silence sit for one beat longer than is comfortable.** Most people fill it with the next question. The other person will often fill it with the second half of their answer, which is the half worth having.

**Stop before they want you to.** Ending a first conversation while it is still going well is the single most reliable way to get a second one.

## Practise the Following-Up Part

**Building from their last words.** `last-word-response` forces every reply to start from your partner's final words, which makes changing the subject structurally impossible and turns following up into a reflex rather than a decision.
