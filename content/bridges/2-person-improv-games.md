# 2 Person Improv Games: What Two People Can Actually Do

Almost every improv game published assumes a circle of eight to sixteen people, and nobody says so. You find a list, pick something promising, and discover halfway through the rules that it needs somebody to tag out, somebody to freeze the scene, or six other people to pass a clap to.

A pair is a completely workable unit. It is just a different one, and the games that survive the shrink are not the popular ones.

## Why Most Games Need a Group

Three structural reasons, and knowing them lets you filter a list yourself rather than discovering the problem mid-game.

**Rotation.** Circle games run on turn-taking — the fun is in the queue, the near-misses, the person who fumbles. Two people have no queue.

**Replacement.** Freeze tag, tag-outs and sweep edits all work by somebody coming in from outside. With nobody outside, the mechanism has nothing to do.

**Anonymity.** Group games let people hide, briefly, in the middle of a round. That hiding place is what makes them safe for beginners — and in a pair it does not exist, which is both the difficulty and the point.

## What a Pair Has Instead

The duo is not a reduced version of a cast, which is worth establishing before the list.

TJ and Dave have built the most admired act in modern improv out of two people, no suggestion and no edits, and nobody watching concludes something is missing. `interdependence` is fully present with two — meaning still requires a receiver, and there is exactly one.

What a pair actually gains is **density**. In a class of twelve you might play four minutes in two hours. In a pair you are in every scene, so an hour produces more repetitions than a term of standing in a circle. Nothing is more useful for improving quickly, and nothing is more tiring.

## The Games That Work With Two

- **[Mirroring](/practice/exercises/mirroring)** — one leads, one follows, slowly, no talking. The oldest exercise there is and the one that most obviously does not need a group. Swap the lead without announcing it and the exercise gets considerably harder.
- **[Last word response](/practice/exercises/last-word-response)** — your first word must be your partner's last. It makes preparing a line structurally impossible, which is the single most common problem in a pair who know each other well.
- **[One-word scene](/practice/exercises/one-word-scene)** — a scene built one word at a time, alternating. Nobody can steer it, so nobody can hide behind having a plan.
- **[Gift giving](/practice/exercises/gift-giving)** — you are handed an unspecified something and you name what it is. Trains the habit of making the other person's offer work rather than assessing it.
- **[Emotional honesty scene](/practice/exercises/emotional-honesty-scene)** — a scene where the only rule is that neither of you may hide behind a joke. Uncomfortable and the fastest thing on this list.
- **[First line drill](/practice/exercises/first-line-drill)** — one of you gives an opening line, the other starts immediately, and you stop after thirty seconds. Twenty of these beats one long scene for the specific problem of starting.
- **[No-backspace scene](/practice/exercises/no-backspace-scene)** — no self-correction allowed, so a sentence finishes in the direction it began. Most people find out how much of their speech is backing out of an opening.
- **[Status transfer](/practice/exercises/status-transfer)** — run the same short scene high, then low, then shift it mid-way. Two is the ideal number for this; a group makes it muddier.

## Running a Session for Two

Forty-five minutes is the right length and the structure barely varies.

Five minutes of mirroring, in silence, to stop the session being a continuation of the conversation you were already having. Ten minutes of a listening constraint — last word response or one-word scene — which is where the actual training happens. Twenty minutes of ordinary scenes with one rule applied, changing the rule every few scenes. Then ten minutes of scenes with no rule at all, which is the part that tells you whether any of it transferred.

Do not give each other notes between every scene. A pair that stops to analyse after each one will spend forty of the forty-five minutes talking, and talking about improv is a different and much easier activity than doing it.

## What Two People Cannot Train

Worth being straight about, because the honest list is short but real.

Group mind, in the sense of a whole ensemble arriving at the same beat, needs an ensemble. Edits that depend on someone entering — sweeps, tag-outs, walk-ons — have no mechanism. Group scenes obviously cannot happen, and neither can the specific skill of being one voice among seven and knowing when not to speak, which is harder than it sounds and is only learnable in a crowd.

If your goal is performing in an ensemble show, a pair will take you a long way and will not take you all the way. If your goal is to be better in conversations, meetings and rooms, the pair is arguably the better training unit, because those are two-person situations too.

## Questions People Ask

### Can two people actually improve without a class?

Yes, faster than most people expect, with one condition: you need a constraint on every scene. Two people playing free scenes for an hour will reliably drift into the thing they are already good at, because there is no teacher to notice and nothing forcing a different move. The rule is doing the job the teacher would do, which is why the exercises above matter more in a pair than in a class.

### What if one of us is much more experienced?

Use it deliberately rather than pretending it is not true. The more experienced person should take the constraint that is hardest for them and let the other pick the scenes, which balances the room without anybody having to coach. The failure to avoid is the experienced one giving notes after every scene — that converts a practice into a lesson, and the beginner stops taking risks about ten minutes in.

### How often is worth it?

Forty-five minutes weekly beats three hours monthly by a wide margin, for the same reason spaced repetition beats cramming anywhere else. Two people who meet every week for a month will notice a difference; two people who do one long session will mostly notice that they were tired by the end.
