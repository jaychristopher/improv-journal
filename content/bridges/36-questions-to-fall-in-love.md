# 36 Questions to Fall in Love: The List and the Mechanism

The 36 questions come from a 1997 psychology paper, and almost everything popularly attached to them was added afterwards. The list below is the real one. The explanation is the part most pages skip, and it is the part that tells you whether this will work for you.

## What the Study Actually Was

Arthur Aron and colleagues published *The Experimental Generation of Interpersonal Closeness: A Procedure and Some Preliminary Findings* in **Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin** in 1997.

The goal was not romance. It was **a tool**. Researchers studying closeness had a practical problem — closeness normally takes months to form, and you cannot run an experiment on something that slow. So Aron's team built a procedure that reliably produces the *feeling* of closeness between two strangers in about forty-five minutes, so that it could be studied in a laboratory.

That is what the 36 questions are: a closeness generator built for research convenience. The name it now travels under came much later, from Mandy Len Catron's 2015 essay in the *New York Times* Modern Love column, which described using the procedure on a date. The essay was excellent and enormously popular, and it is the reason a lab instrument is now a Valentine's Day activity.

## Why It Works

The mechanism is not mysterious, and it is the same one this whole site is built on.

**Closeness is produced by escalating reciprocal self-disclosure.** Each person reveals something slightly more exposing than the last thing revealed, the other person meets it without judgement and matches it, and an estimate updates. `trust` here is exactly that accumulated estimate — not a feeling you decide to have, but a running total of how many times being open has gone unpunished.

Ordinary conversation almost never escalates. It finds a comfortable level in the first few minutes and stays there, sometimes for years, because raising the level requires somebody to go first and risk being the only one who did. The 36 questions remove that risk in three ways:

- **The escalation is pre-agreed.** Both people know the questions get harder. Nobody is choosing to push, so nobody can be blamed for pushing.
- **Reciprocity is structural.** You both answer everything. `vulnerability` cannot be requested one-way here, because the format makes an interview impossible.
- **The exposure is bounded.** Thirty-six questions and forty-five minutes. `safety-in-the-room` depends heavily on knowing where the far edge is, and people will go considerably further when they can see it.

Strip those three properties out and you have an ordinary conversation, which is why sending somebody the list and asking a few over text does approximately nothing.

## The 36 Questions

The full set, as published in Aron et al. (1997) and reproduced by Berkeley's Greater Good Science Center. Three sets of twelve, roughly fifteen minutes each. Both people answer every question, taking turns.

### Set I

1. Given the choice of anyone in the world, whom would you want as a dinner guest?
2. Would you like to be famous? In what way?
3. Before making a telephone call, do you ever rehearse what you are going to say? Why?
4. What would constitute a "perfect" day for you?
5. When did you last sing to yourself? To someone else?
6. If you were able to live to the age of 90 and retain either the mind or body of a 30-year-old for the last 60 years of your life, which would you want?
7. Do you have a secret hunch about how you will die?
8. Name three things you and your partner appear to have in common.
9. For what in your life do you feel most grateful?
10. If you could change anything about the way you were raised, what would it be?
11. Take four minutes and tell your partner your life story in as much detail as possible.
12. If you could wake up tomorrow having gained any one quality or ability, what would it be?

### Set II

13. If a crystal ball could tell you the truth about yourself, your life, the future, or anything else, what would you want to know?
14. Is there something that you've dreamed of doing for a long time? Why haven't you done it?
15. What is the greatest accomplishment of your life?
16. What do you value most in a friendship?
17. What is your most treasured memory?
18. What is your most terrible memory?
19. If you knew that in one year you would die suddenly, would you change anything about the way you are now living? Why?
20. What does friendship mean to you?
21. What roles do love and affection play in your life?
22. Alternate sharing something you consider a positive characteristic of your partner. Share a total of five items.
23. How close and warm is your family? Do you feel your childhood was happier than most other people's?
24. How do you feel about your relationship with your mother?

### Set III

25. Make three true "we" statements each. For instance, "We are both in this room feeling…"
26. Complete this sentence: "I wish I had someone with whom I could share…"
27. If you were going to become a close friend with your partner, please share what would be important for them to know.
28. Tell your partner what you like about them; be very honest this time, saying things that you might not say to someone you've just met.
29. Share with your partner an embarrassing moment in your life.
30. When did you last cry in front of another person? By yourself?
31. Tell your partner something that you like about them.
32. What, if anything, is too serious to be joked about?
33. If you were to die this evening with no opportunity to communicate with anyone, what would you most regret not having told someone? Why haven't you told them yet?
34. Your house, containing everything you own, catches fire. After saving your loved ones and pets, you have time to safely make a final dash to save any one item. What would it be? Why?
35. Of all the people in your family, whose death would you find most disturbing? Why?
36. Share a personal problem and ask your partner's advice on how they might handle it. Also, ask your partner to reflect back to you how you seem to be feeling about the problem you have chosen.

## The Structure Is the Interesting Part

Read the three sets next to each other and the design becomes obvious.

**Set I is almost entirely hypothetical.** Dinner guests, perfect days, being famous. Nothing here costs anything, and that is deliberate — the first twelve exist to establish that answering is safe, not to reveal anything.

**Set II turns to the actual life.** Treasured memory, terrible memory, greatest accomplishment, your mother. Still answerable at whatever depth you choose, but now about you rather than about a hypothetical.

**Set III is directed at the other person.** Notice how many of the last twelve are not questions at all — tell your partner what you like about them, make three "we" statements, share a problem and ask their advice. The final set stops being disclosure and becomes **address**. You are no longer describing yourself to a researcher; you are speaking to a specific person about the two of you.

That progression — hypothetical, then personal, then relational — is the whole invention. The individual questions are ordinary. Several appear on any list of get-to-know-you questions. The sequence is what does the work.

## The Four Minutes of Eye Contact

Widely attached to this and **not one of the 36**. The staring step comes from the popular version of the procedure rather than from the numbered list above, and it is worth separating the two, because plenty of people abandon the whole thing at the idea of four minutes of silent eye contact with somebody they have just met.

What does it do? Sustained mutual gaze is uncomfortable for a reason — in ordinary interaction, eye contact is rationed, and holding it past a couple of seconds is normally reserved for intimacy, confrontation or performance. Four minutes puts two people well outside the ration with no task to hide behind, and the usual reported experience is a first minute of acute self-consciousness followed by it subsiding into something calmer. That arc is most of the effect: you are not learning anything about the other person, you are discovering that being looked at did not turn out to cost anything.

Which is the same mechanism as the questions, in a different medium — exposure that goes unpunished, so the estimate updates. It is optional. Doing it badly, self-consciously, while both of you laugh, works about as well as doing it solemnly.

You can do the questions without it entirely. The escalation is where the effect lives.

## Does It Actually Work?

Two honest qualifications, neither of which is a reason not to do it.

**It generates closeness, not love.** The study measured how close two people felt afterwards, and the procedure reliably raises that. Feeling close to somebody is a precondition for a great deal, and it is not the same thing as falling for them. A page promising the second is selling the first.

**It works on willing strangers.** Both people knowing what they signed up for is part of the mechanism, not an artefact of the lab. Sprung on somebody unprepared, question 18 is an ambush rather than an escalation.

What it is genuinely good for: two people who want to skip the eighteen months that ordinary acquaintance takes, and are both prepared to say so out loud first.

## How to Do It Properly

**Agree to it explicitly.** The pre-agreement is doing real work. "Shall we do the 36 questions" is a better opening than sliding into question one and hoping.

**Both answer everything, alternating who goes first.** Whoever answers first sets the depth for that question, so rotating it stops one person calibrating the whole thing.

**Keep to roughly fifteen minutes a set.** The instinct is to linger in Set I where it is comfortable, then rush Set III. That inverts the design.

**Do not skip ahead to the interesting ones.** Question 35 without the preceding 34 is just a strange thing to ask somebody.

**Say when you would rather not.** Declining one question does not break it. Answering carefully and evasively for twenty minutes does.

## When Not to Use It

On a first date where either person is unsure — the format is intense and consent to intensity has to be genuine. With a colleague, where the power dynamics make declining expensive. And with anybody who has not been told what it is, which turns a structure into a manoeuvre.

## The Part You Can Practise

The procedure supplies the escalation. It cannot supply the receiving, and the receiving is what decides whether an answer is followed by another one.

`active-listening` is the whole of it: attending to what was actually said rather than preparing your own answer while they speak. `last-word-response` trains it directly, by making each reply depend on the last thing your partner said — which is impossible to do while drafting.
