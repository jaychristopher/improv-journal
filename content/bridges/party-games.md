# Party Games: 40 That Need Nothing but People

Most party game lists are shopping lists. They assume you have a box, or cards, or an hour to explain rules to eleven people who are holding drinks.

Everything below needs **nothing**. No equipment, no setup, no teams, and no explaining beyond a sentence. Several can be started without announcing that a game is happening at all, which is the single most useful property a party game can have.

## What Makes a Party Game Work

Four tests, and almost every failure is a failure of one of them.

**Nobody can lose.** A game with a loser creates a person who now has to be a good sport in public, which is work. The best party games have no scoring at all, or scoring so obviously arbitrary that nobody minds.

**Nobody has to perform.** Anything that requires being funny on demand excludes most of the room — the confident three play, everybody else watches and quietly resents it. `safety-in-the-room` on this site is the precondition for anybody joining in, and it is destroyed fastest by a game where a bad turn is visible.

**It survives being joined late.** Parties are not synchronised. People arrive, drift to the kitchen, come back. A game that requires everyone to have heard the setup is a game that dies the first time somebody gets a drink.

**It can be stopped without ceremony.** If ending it requires a winner or a conclusion, it will overstay. The good ones just stop, and nobody notices they have.

## The Ten That Always Work

If you want ten and nothing else, take these. Each is described properly below; this is the shortlist for somebody standing in a kitchen with fifteen minutes to fill and no plan.

1. **Would You Rather** — two options, both costing something. The defending is the game.
2. **This or That** — the same shape with the stakes removed. The one to use when the room is still cold.
3. **Most Likely To** — everybody points on three. Warmest of the lot when the labels are kind.
4. **Stand Up If** — no turns at all, so a group of thirty can play it. Fastest way to change a room.
5. **Two Truths and a Lie** — everybody reveals two true things while apparently just guessing.
6. **Categories** — name a category, go round, no repeats. The category is where the fun is.
7. **The Phone Game** — everybody reveals the same thing at once. High risk, high reward.
8. **Group Rock Paper Scissors** — losers become supporters. Ends in forty people shouting. Four minutes.
9. **Wink Murder** — needs nothing, runs itself, and works while people are still arriving.
10. **Best and Worst** — for the last six people at midnight, when the loud games are over.

The two you will actually reach for most are numbers 1 and 4, for opposite reasons: one gets a warm room arguing, the other gets a cold room speaking without anybody having to take a turn.

## Games That Need Nothing at All

These are the ones to reach for first. Each is a rule, not an activity — you can start any of them mid-conversation.

**1. Would You Rather.** Two options, both costing something, everyone picks. The pleasure is not the choice, it is being made to defend it. Works from four people to thirty. There are [164 pairs here](/would-you-rather-questions), sorted by how hard they are.

**2. This or That.** The same shape with the stakes removed — one word, no defending. Faster, and the right one for a room that has not warmed up yet. [300 pairs](/this-or-that-questions).

**3. Most Likely To.** Everyone points at once on a count of three. The only game here where the answer is about somebody else, which makes it the warmest when it works and the sharpest when it does not. [182 of them](/most-likely-to-questions), plus the rules that keep it kind.

**4. 21 Questions.** Two people, twenty-one questions, taken in turns. The number is doing the work — it removes the polite exit that ends most conversations at the fourth exchange. [The full game](/21-questions-game).

**5. Two Truths and a Lie.** Each person gives three statements, the room guesses the lie. The reason it endures is that everyone reveals two true things while apparently just playing.

**6. Fishbowl, without the fishbowl.** Everyone thinks of a person, place or thing. Take turns describing without saying the word. No paper needed if you simply trust people not to change their answer.

**7. The Question Chain.** Only questions allowed. Any statement, hesitation or repeat and you are out, cheerfully. Lasts four minutes and produces genuine laughs from people who insist they are not funny.

**8. Who Am I.** One person leaves, the room picks somebody famous, they return and ask yes-or-no questions. Ancient, still excellent, needs nothing.

**9. Story, One Word Each.** Round the room, one word each, building a single story. Nobody can steer it, which is exactly why it works — and it is an improv exercise in disguise.

**10. The Alphabet Game.** Pick a category. Go round naming things in it, starting with A, then B. Ordinary, absorbing, and impossible to be bad at.

**11. Categories.** Name a category, go round the circle, no repeats, no hesitating. The category is where the fun is: things in a shed, excuses for being late, disappointing biscuits.

**12. Never Have I Ever, Clean Version.** Hold up five fingers, drop one each time something applies. Keep the prompts to the mundane — never have I ever been abroad, never have I ever broken a bone — and it is a genuinely good way to find out about a room.

## Games With One Object

Everything here needs a single thing somebody in the room already has.

**13. The Phone Game.** Somebody names a category — last photo, most-played song, first contact alphabetically. Everyone reveals at once. High risk, very high reward.

**14. Werewolf, with playing cards.** One deck, a moderator, and a village that has to work out who is lying. The best large-group game there is, and the only one on this page worth the setup time.

**15. Hat Charades.** Everyone writes three names on scraps and folds them in. Three rounds, same names each time: describe, one word only, mime. It gets funnier each round because the room already knows the answers.

**16. The Pen Game.** Pass a pen with a rule nobody explains. Others work out the rule by watching. Infuriating and excellent.

**17. Cup Pyramid Race.** Any stack of cups, two teams, build and unbuild. Sounds childish, absorbs a kitchen for twenty minutes.

**18. The Coin Question.** Flip a coin. Heads, you answer a question from the room. Tails, you ask one. The randomness removes the awkwardness of choosing.

## Games for a Large Group

Twelve or more, where anything requiring turns will collapse.

**19. Stand Up If.** Somebody calls a statement. Anyone it applies to stands. No turns, no waiting, and the room learns a surprising amount in three minutes.

**20. Human Bingo.** Find somebody who has done X. Works without cards if you simply call out five things and let people find each other.

**21. Group Rock Paper Scissors.** Lose and you become the winner's supporter, cheering them on. Ends with two people and forty spectators screaming. Takes four minutes.

**22. The Line-Up.** Order yourselves by birthday, distance travelled, years in the city — without speaking. Chaotic and oddly bonding.

**23. Wink Murder.** One murderer, chosen silently, kills by winking. Everyone else works out who. Requires nothing and runs itself.

**24. Splitting the Room.** Call out two options and have people physically move to a side. It is [this or that](/this-or-that-questions) with legs, and it works far better than asking a large group to answer out loud.

## Games for a Small Gathering

Four to eight, where you can afford to go slower and deeper.

**25. Best and Worst.** Everyone gives the best and worst thing about their week. Nothing more. Reliably becomes the conversation of the night.

**26. The Hot Seat.** One person takes questions for three minutes. Volunteer only, and the room must be warm first.

**27. Interview the Newcomer.** If somebody does not know anybody, the room asks them ten questions. Sounds brutal, is actually the fastest way to make somebody feel included.

**28. Unpopular Opinions.** Everyone offers one. No debating for the first round — just collect them, then go back to the interesting ones.

**29. The Photo Round.** Everyone shows a photo from exactly one year ago today. Instant nostalgia and total randomness.

## Games for Children and Family

**30. Sleeping Lions.** All children lie still. Last one to move wins. Invented by exhausted adults and the finest game ever devised.

**31. Keep It Up.** One balloon, no hands allowed after the first round, no letting it touch the floor. Twenty minutes, no equipment beyond the balloon.

**32. The Story Ball.** Throw a soft object round the circle, each catcher adds a sentence to a shared story. The younger version of the one-word game, and children are better at it than adults.

## Which Game for Which Moment

The commonest mistake is not picking a bad game. It is picking a good one at the wrong point in the evening, which makes it look like a bad one.

**The room has just arrived and nobody has settled.** Nothing with turns. Use something everybody answers simultaneously — Stand Up If, Splitting the Room, or a fast run of this-or-that. The goal is only that everybody has spoken once, which changes a room more than anything else you can do.

**It is warm but the conversation has split into twos.** This is the moment for would-you-rather or most likely to. Both pull separate conversations back into one, because both produce a disagreement the whole room wants to weigh in on.

**Somebody has arrived late and knows nobody.** Interview the Newcomer, or anything with no accumulated state. Avoid anything where the room shares a running joke they would have to explain.

**It is late and down to six people.** Best and Worst, Unpopular Opinions, 21 Questions. The room can now afford slower turns and will remember these rather than the loud games earlier.

**There are children present.** Sleeping Lions, Keep It Up, the Story Ball. Also worth knowing that children play every game on this page better than adults, because they have not yet learned to work out how an answer will look.

**It is a work event.** Careful — the power dynamics change everything and a game that flattens status in a friendship group can expose people at work. That has [its own page](/icebreaker-questions-for-work).

## More Games Worth Knowing

**33. Fortunately, Unfortunately.** Round the circle, alternating: fortunately the train arrived, unfortunately it was on fire. Builds a ridiculous story and requires nothing but the two words.

**34. The Expert.** One person is declared a world expert on something absurd — cutlery, weather in fictional countries — and takes questions from the room, answering with total confidence. Volunteer only.

**35. Guess the Song, Hummed.** No devices. Someone hums, the room guesses. Consistently funnier than it has any right to be.

**36. Count to Twenty.** The group counts to twenty out loud. Anyone may speak, nobody may plan, and if two people speak at once you start again. A genuine improv exercise, and a room that manages it feels absurdly pleased with itself.

**37. Backwards Charades.** The whole team acts, one person guesses. Reverses the usual pressure and is far less exposing than standard charades.

**38. Yes, And.** Two people build a scene where nothing can be rejected. Everything the other says is true and you add to it. The foundational improv game and the origin of half this page.

**39. What Are You Doing?** One person mimes an action, the next asks what they are doing, and the answer must be something else entirely, which the asker then mimes. Fast, silly, impossible to be bad at.

**40. Convergence.** Two people say a word each. Both then simultaneously say a word connecting them. Repeat until they say the same word. Weirdly compelling, and it takes two minutes.

## Why Most Party Games Fail

**They need explaining.** If the rules take longer than ninety seconds, half the room has stopped listening and will spend the game asking what is happening.

**They put one person on the spot.** Games built around a performer work brilliantly for the performer. Everyone else is doing a turn they did not want.

**They cannot be joined late.** Anything with fixed teams or accumulated scores excludes whoever walks in at nine.

**They go on.** The most common failure is not a bad game, it is a good game played for twice as long as it deserved. `commitment` matters at the start of a game and matters just as much at the end: decide it is finished while people still want more.

## How to Start One Without Announcing It

The word *game* is the problem. Announcing one puts everybody on notice that participation is about to be required, and half the room begins working out how to avoid it.

So do not announce it. Ask two people a would-you-rather in an ordinary voice and let the circle grow. `offers` on this site is the idea that everything you say is something the other person can build on — a question asked casually is an offer, and if it is a good one somebody will pick it up without anybody having to declare that a game has begun.

If it takes, it takes. If it does not, nothing has failed, because nothing was ever formally started.

## The Part You Can Practise

Every game on this page rewards the same thing: producing something without checking first whether it is any good.

`one-word-story` is the shortest way to train it — a story built one word at a time, where planning is structurally impossible because you cannot know what word arrives before yours. `spontaneity` on this site is treated as a trainable skill rather than a personality trait, and this is the exercise that does the training.
