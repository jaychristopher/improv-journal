# Fun Questions to Ask Friends: 116 Worth Actually Answering

Fun is the vaguest word in this whole category, which is why lists of fun questions to ask friends tend to be one of two things: party trivia nobody cares about, or deep questions with an exclamation mark on the end.

It is worth pinning down, because there is a real distinction underneath and it predicts which questions work.

## Fun Is Not Funny, and It Is Not Shallow

Funny is a property of the question. Fun is a property of the answering.

A funny question gets a laugh on the way in and then leaves the person holding it with nothing to do. A fun question might raise no smile at all when asked, and then produces four minutes of everyone talking over each other. Those are different objects, and most lists mix them together.

Shallow is a separate axis again. "What would you do with a year you were not allowed to work?" is enormous and also enormous fun. "What is your favourite colour?" is shallow and no fun whatsoever. Depth and fun turn out to be close to unrelated, so sorting questions by how deep they sound gets you nowhere.

## The Actual Test: Are They Inventing or Retrieving?

Here is the line that separates the two piles.

**A boring question asks you to look something up. A fun question asks you to make something up.**

"What is your favourite film?" is a retrieval task. The answer already exists, finished, somewhere in their memory, and the only job is to fetch it and read it out. There is nothing to enjoy in that, which is why the room goes quiet and somebody changes the subject.

"What film would you make everyone watch before they were allowed to have an opinion about anything?" is an invention task. That answer does not exist yet. They have to build it, in front of you, and building something in front of people is — genuinely, mechanically — the part that is fun.

This is the whole premise of improv. `spontaneity` on this site is the capacity to make something without a plan, and it is treated as a skill because it feels good to use. A question that gives somebody a reason to use it is doing them a favour, and they will feel that even if they could not tell you why.

The portable version, if you remember nothing else: **if a question could be answered accurately from a form they once filled in, it is retrieval.** Change it until it could not be.

## Fun Questions Are Offers, and They Have to Be Catchable

The other half of it is difficulty.

A question is an `offers` — something handed over for the other person to build on. An offer that is impossible to build on is not generous, it is a demand. "Describe yourself" is technically an invitation and practically a wall.

`accepting-the-offer` is the skill of taking what you are given and doing something with it, and it gets much easier when the thing you are given has edges. Constraints make invention easier, not harder. "You may bring three objects" beats "what would you bring", because the number gives the mind something to push against.

Nearly every question below has a constraint welded into it on purpose. That is not decoration. It is the part that makes them work.

## Hypotheticals With a Floor Under Them

Big enough to be interesting, specific enough to be answerable.

- You have to teach a one-hour class tomorrow on any subject. What are you teaching?
- You get to add one rule that everybody on earth has to follow. What is it?
- You can be world-class at one thing, but you start from zero on Monday. What?
- A stranger has to guess your job from your kitchen. What do they guess?
- You have a year off and no need to earn. What does the first month look like, day by day?
- You can send one sentence to every phone on the planet. What does it say?
- You get to permanently delete one invention. Which one goes?
- You have to live inside a film's world for a year. Which film, and where in it?
- Somebody is making a documentary about you but only has the last week of footage. What is it about?
- You keep one skill you currently have and lose all the others. Which do you keep?
- You have to name a colour that has just been discovered. What is it called?
- You get a shop on your street, rent-free, for one year. What is in it?
- Everybody must wear a badge stating one true fact about them. What is on yours?
- You can put a plaque on any building in the world. Where, and what does it say?

## Would You Rather, But Actually Interesting

The format is fine. The usual examples are the problem — either the answer is obvious, or the two options are not connected by anything.

A good one has a genuine trade-off, so people have to explain themselves. The explaining is where the fun lives.

- Always know when someone is lying, or always be believed when you tell the truth?
- Speak every language badly, or one extra language perfectly?
- Unlimited time and no money, or unlimited money and no free time?
- Remember every book you have read, or every conversation you have had?
- Be famous in one country you have never visited, or faintly known everywhere?
- Never feel embarrassed again, or never be bored again?
- Be the best in the world at something nobody values, or middling at something everyone does?
- Live one brilliant year on repeat, or forty unremarkable ones in a row?
- Pause time for an hour a day, or rewind ten seconds once a week?
- Always be twenty minutes early, or always have exactly the right change?
- Perfect memory of your childhood, or perfect knowledge of next year?
- Never need to sleep, or never need to eat?
- Be trusted instantly by strangers, or forgiven instantly by everyone who knows you?
- Know the day you die, or know the thing you are best at?

## Questions That Start a Good Argument

Ones where reasonable people split, which turns a conversation into a group activity. Nobody has a stake in the outcome, so it stays warm.

- Is a hot dog a sandwich, and does your answer say something about you?
- Which food is overrated and defended purely out of habit?
- What is the correct number of times to let a phone ring before answering?
- Is it worse to be an hour late or forty minutes early?
- What is the most annoying sound that is not technically a bad sound?
- Which decade had the best-looking cars, and be honest about why?
- Is there any excuse for replying to a voice note with a voice note?
- What is the least defensible thing you are completely certain about?
- At what age do you stop being allowed to blame your upbringing?
- Can you leave a film at the halfway point and still hold opinions about it?
- What is the appropriate punishment for somebody who spoils an ending?
- Which everyday task do people get wrong at a rate of about ninety per cent?

## Favourites, Reframed So They Are Worth Asking

Any favourites question converts from retrieval to invention with one move: attach a job to it.

- What song would you use to wake an entire building?
- What would you cook for somebody you wanted to impress but did not fancy?
- What book would you hand a sixteen-year-old who says they hate reading?
- What is the correct film for a bad Sunday?
- What is your best cheap thing — under a tenner, slightly changed your life?
- What would be playing in a lift you had designed?
- Which single item of clothing would you rescue from a fire?
- Which meal have you eaten most times, and are you proud of that?
- What is a smell that puts you somewhere specific immediately?
- What is the single best texture in food?
- What would your last meal be, given you would be too nervous to eat it?
- Where have you been that you would not recommend but would return to?

## Absurd Premises

These look like nonsense and reliably produce the best material of the night, because nobody can prepare and nobody can be wrong.

- You are now legally required to have a catchphrase. What is it?
- Your life has a laugh track. When does it fire most inappropriately?
- You must fight one animal forever, scaled to your size. Which?
- A song plays every time you enter a room. What is it, and does it wear thin?
- Replace one of your five senses with an entirely new one. What does it detect?
- Aliens land and can only be shown one human activity. Which do you pick?
- You are assigned a personal narrator. Whose voice, and are they on your side?
- Your thoughts become audible at low volume. What is the first problem this causes?
- You have to hide something enormous in your house. What is the plan?
- Everybody must adopt one conspiracy theory. Which is least damaging?
- You may only communicate in questions for a week. What breaks first?
- A statue of you goes up. What are you doing, and what is at your feet?
- You must run a country of two hundred people. What is the first law?
- One letter of the alphabet is taken from you permanently. Which, and how bad is it?

## Questions About the Group Itself

Fun generated by the specific people in the room. These get quoted back for years.

- Who here lasts longest with no phone, and who goes first?
- If one of us had to negotiate on behalf of humanity, who?
- What would each of us most plausibly be arrested for?
- Who has the best-founded confidence, and who has the most confidence?
- If this group started a business tomorrow, what is it and who runs it?
- Which of us ends up quietly famous in fifteen years?
- What is the thing everyone here is too polite to say about the group?
- Who do you want with you during a crisis, and who afterwards?
- If we swapped lives for a week, which pairing is the funniest disaster?
- What nickname should this group have had all along?
- Which of us has changed most and noticed least?
- If somebody filmed this group, what genre is it?

## Questions for Waiting Around

Long drives, delayed trains, the hour before food arrives. Low effort to ask, and they expand to fill whatever time there is.

- What is the most useless skill you have, and how did you acquire it?
- What was the last thing you did for the first time?
- What is something in your home that guests always comment on?
- What is the strangest job you can prove somebody actually does?
- What have you never done that everybody assumes you have?
- What is the closest you have come to being on television?
- What do you own that you would struggle to replace?
- What is the most you have spent on something you used once?
- What do you do that would look mad on camera without context?
- What are you unreasonably good at?
- What is the worst advice you have ever actually followed?
- What is your most-repeated story, and are you sick of it?
- What were you certain about at twenty that embarrasses you now?
- What is the longest you have gone without telling anybody something?

## Questions With a Rule Attached

The constraint is the fun. Say the rule out loud before you ask.

- Describe your last year without mentioning work.
- Recommend something, and you have to be genuinely enthusiastic about it.
- Tell us about somebody you admire without using the word inspiring.
- Explain your job as though we had to do it tomorrow.
- Describe your ideal day with every hour accounted for.
- Tell us something true about yourself that sounds like a lie.
- Give us an opinion you would defend for two full minutes.
- Sum up five years in five words, one per year.
- Describe a place using only what you could hear there.
- Pitch your hometown as though you were being paid to.
- Say something kind about somebody here without using an adjective.
- Describe the last thing you watched as badly as you can.

## Questions That Are Fun to Answer Badly

The ones where a wrong answer beats a right one. This is where the least confident person in the room usually wins, because there is nothing to get wrong.

- What is happening inside a microwave, and do not look it up?
- Explain how a plane stays up, confidently.
- Where does the sea end, technically?
- What is the difference between a lake and a very large pond?
- Explain rain to somebody who has only ever seen taps.
- What does the moon smell like?
- Who decides what a new species gets called?
- How does a fridge know?
- What happens to a cloud after it has rained?
- Why is the sky that particular blue, in your own words?
- What is electricity, one sentence, no cheating?
- How do bees decide anything?

## When Somebody Cannot Think of Anything

The argument on this page cuts both ways, and it is worth saying so rather than leaving it for somebody to discover at their own expense.

If the pleasure of a good question is invention, then **invention is also the cost**. Ask a table of five to make something up and one of them will be visibly slower — not less imaginative, just slower, because invention speed varies enormously and the quick ones set the pace without meaning to. That person now has a choice between saying something they are not happy with and holding everybody up, and neither is fun, which is the only thing this page claims to be delivering.

It is fixable, and none of the fixes is a different question.

**Pass first, yourself.** Somewhere early, say you have not got one and move on. A pass that nobody has modelled reads as a confession; a pass the asker took two minutes ago reads as a rule.

**Answer your own first when you can see somebody stuck.** It buys them the ten seconds they need and shows the level, which is usually the actual problem — people freeze less often on the question than on not knowing how big an answer is wanted.

**Take the flat answer without rescuing it.** "Oh come on, you can do better than that" is meant warmly and lands as a mark. Accept the short version, respond to it as though it were the full one, and the next round costs them less.

**Change the format rather than the question.** If somebody is struggling, stop asking for invention. [This or that](/this-or-that-questions) needs a one-word preference and no composition at all, and it will get the same person talking within about thirty seconds. [Would you rather](/would-you-rather-questions) is a middle setting — a choice to defend rather than a thing to conceive.

**Ask the quiet one something they already know the answer to.** Not to put them on the spot — the opposite. A question about their actual life is retrieval, and retrieval is free.

The principle underneath all of it: **a fun question is only fun while answering it is optional.** The moment invention becomes compulsory, you are running a performance review with snacks.

## The Ones That Kill It

Worth knowing what to avoid, because two or three of these will flatten a good run.

**Questions with a correct answer.** Anything the group can be graded on stops being play and becomes a test. "What is the capital of Chile" produces one winner and six people waiting.

**Questions that require a confession.** A question is not fun if answering honestly costs something. That is a different and often worthwhile conversation, but it is not this one, and dropping it into a light evening kills the mood in a single move.

**Questions you have not answered yourself.** If you would stall on it, so will they. `discovery` is what you are hoping for here — the answer neither of you saw coming — and it does not arrive while one person is conducting an interview.

**Questions that are really a complaint.** "Why does nobody ever organise anything?" is not a question, and everyone can tell.

## How to Keep It Going After the Answer

The questions are the easy part. What decides whether an evening of these is fun or exhausting is the ten seconds after somebody answers.

`yes-and` is the habit of taking what you were given and adding to it, and it is the entire difference between a list of questions and a conversation. The failure mode is the round-robin: everyone answers in turn, nobody responds to anybody, and it quietly becomes admin.

Two things fix it. **Follow the interesting part, not the list.** If an answer contains something better than what you asked, abandon the list on the spot — it has already done its job.

**Answer your own questions too.** Not necessarily first, but at some point, at the same level of effort. A question you are unwilling to answer is a question you are administering.

## The Part You Can Practise

All of this is one skill: making something up in front of people without checking first whether it is any good.

If these are fun to answer but you freeze on your own turn, that is not a personality trait. It is the planning habit, and it is trainable. `one-word-story` is the shortest way in — you contribute a single word at a time to a story nobody is steering, which makes planning impossible and invention automatic.
