# Del Close: The Ideas That Still Run Improv

**Del Close (1934–1999) was the American improv teacher and director who invented the Harold, co-founded ImprovOlympic with Charna Halpern, and trained a large share of two generations of American comedy — among them John Belushi, Bill Murray, Chris Farley, Mike Myers, Tina Fey, Amy Poehler and Stephen Colbert.** His argument, unusual at the time and now close to orthodoxy, was that improvisation is a theatrical art form in its own right rather than a way of generating sketch material.

Almost everything written about him is either hagiography or a list of famous students. Neither is much use if what you want to know is what he actually taught, so this page is mostly that — the ideas, where they came from, which have held up, and which parts of the legend are worth resisting.

## The Career in Brief

Close came out of Kansas into the first generation of American improvisational theatre. He performed with the **Compass Players** in the late 1950s, the St. Louis and Chicago company that grew out of the University of Chicago and produced Mike Nichols and Elaine May, then joined **The Second City** shortly after its founding.

The formative detour was **The Committee** in San Francisco in the mid-1960s — a politically sharper company working in a city then at the centre of the American counterculture, with which Close was closely involved. It was there, in 1967, that the form later called the `harold` was first attempted: a long, unscripted piece built from a single audience suggestion, treated as one work rather than a string of sketches.

He returned to Chicago and spent the 1970s and early 1980s as resident director at Second City, the period that put him in a room with most of the era's future stars. He also spent time in the mid-1980s as a consultant to *Saturday Night Live*.

The final and most consequential phase began in 1984, when he joined **Charna Halpern** at what became **ImprovOlympic** — later iO. Halpern had the theatre and the organisation; Close had the form and the teaching. Their collaboration turned the Harold from an experiment into a curriculum, and the curriculum is the thing that spread. *Truth in Comedy* (1994), written with Halpern and Kim "Howard" Johnson, is its written form — see `ref-truth-in-comedy`.

He died in Chicago in March 1999, days before his sixty-fifth birthday.

## What He Actually Taught

Four ideas do most of the work, and they are more connected than they first appear.

**Improv is theatre, not a shortcut to comedy.** This is the root claim and the one everything else depends on. Close treated an improvised piece as a work to be judged as a work — with structure, weight, and an ending it had earned. The immediate corollary is that chasing laughs is a failure of nerve, because a laugh is available at any moment and always cheaper than the alternative.

**The truth is funny.** Not a claim about humour so much as an instruction about where to look. Invented material is thin because the person inventing it is guessing; observed material is dense because reality is more specific than anybody's guess. `be-honest` is the working form of this.

**Work at the top of your intelligence.** Close's objection to most improv was that performers played dumber than they were, on the theory that stupidity is funnier. His position was that an audience will follow anything if the people on stage genuinely believe it, and that condescension is legible from the back row.

**The ensemble is the star.** The most quoted line from *Truth in Comedy*, and the one that most directly opposed how comedy worked then and largely still works. The practical content is that a performer's job is to make the others look good, which is not politeness — a scene is a shared object, and anybody protecting their own position is degrading it.

Out of these he built the two ideas the site treats as load-bearing: `group-mind`, the ensemble behaving as one intelligence, and `connections`, the practice of carrying material across scenes until a long piece turns out to have been about something. The `opening` — the ensemble's first collective move on a suggestion — is where both are trained.

## Follow the Fear

His best-known instruction, and the most misused.

What it meant in the room was narrow and technical. At any moment a scene offers several available moves, and performers reliably select the safest — the joke instead of the admission, the new topic instead of the uncomfortable one, the exit instead of staying. The instruction was to notice that flinch and take the other option, on the grounds that the reluctance is information: you are avoiding it because it matters.

That is a claim about *choice-making inside a scene*. It is not a claim that discomfort is good for people, and the distance between those two readings is where a great deal of harm has been done by people quoting him.

## The Mythology Problem

Close is the closest thing improv has to a saint, and the hagiography has costs that are worth naming plainly.

**The genius-teacher model.** He was, by many accounts including admiring ones, frequently cruel — contemptuous of students he judged lazy, and capable of humiliating people in front of a class. The standard telling files this under uncompromising, and treats the results as vindication. But the results are not evidence: the students who thrived are the ones who are known, and nobody counts the people who left. An anecdote about a brilliant teacher destroying somebody who deserved it has been used to license a great deal of ordinary bullying by people with none of his ability.

**The drugs.** His heavy use is generally told as colour, occasionally as a source of insight. It cost him jobs and years, and the romantic framing has not helped the many improvisers who inherited the idea that self-destruction and creative seriousness are related.

**Mysticism as a defence.** Close spoke about the Harold as a spiritual endeavour and about interconnectedness as literal rather than metaphorical. `group-mind` is a real, observable phenomenon with identifiable conditions, and describing it in terms that cannot be examined made it harder to teach and easier to gatekeep. If the thing you are training is ineffable, the person who decides whether you have it is whoever holds the room.

None of this touches the quality of the ideas. It bears on how they are transmitted, which is why it belongs on a page about the ideas.

## What Has Held Up, and What Has Not

**Held up.** The core claim — that improvisation is a form rather than a feeder system — is now the default assumption of every serious training programme, which is a substantial thing for one person to have moved. Ensemble-first, honesty over cleverness, and playing at full intelligence have survived every subsequent school, including the ones that define themselves against him.

**Revised.** The UCB generation, taught largely by his students, made comedy the explicit goal again and put *game* — the identified pattern a scene runs on — at the centre. That is a genuine correction rather than a betrayal: Close's suspicion of laughs protected against hackery but left performers without a usable account of why a scene was working.

**Superseded in practice.** The Harold's structural cost is now widely acknowledged even by its advocates, and many strong teams use its lessons without its scaffolding. The `harold` page covers that argument, including Will Hines's objection that the form simply contains too many scenes.

**Quietly abandoned.** The teaching culture. Contemporary training has largely concluded that the room has to be safe for the work to be honest, which is close to the opposite of the environment Close is celebrated for running — and is arguably a better route to the thing he actually wanted.

## The Skull

The story usually told last: that Close willed his skull to the Goodman Theatre so that he could go on playing Yorick in *Hamlet*.

It is a good story and it appears to be, at least in the particulars, untrue. Halpern later acknowledged that she had been unable to obtain his skull after his death and had supplied one from another source. The Goodman received a skull; it was probably not his.

It is a fitting coda for a teacher whose subject was the difference between an appealing invention and an honest observation, and improv has mostly gone on telling the better version anyway.

## Where to Go Next

*Truth in Comedy* is the primary text and is short — see `ref-truth-in-comedy` for what it establishes. Kim "Howard" Johnson's *The Funniest One in the Room* (2008) is the full biography and is franker than the legend. Halpern's *Art by Committee* (2006) covers how the Harold changed after him.

For the form itself, start with `harold`. For the two ideas that outlived the form, `group-mind` and `connections`.

If you arrived here without much improv background, [what is improv](/what-is-improv) covers the form and where Close sits in its history. The games and drills that came out of the lineage are collected in [improv games](/improv-games).
