# Questions to Ask at the End of an Interview, and Before

Your questions are read more carefully than your answers. Answers are prepared and everybody knows it. Questions are the one part of an interview that reveals how you actually think — what you notice, what you consider important, and whether you have understood the job well enough to ask something specific about it.

They also do something structural, which is what most lists of interview questions miss entirely.

## The Short Answer

If you want three questions to ask in an interview and nothing else, ask these.

**"What is the problem you are hoping this hire solves?"** Job descriptions describe a role; this asks for the brief. The answer is usually more specific than anything written down, and it tells you what you would actually be judged on.

**"Was this role created, or is it a replacement — and what happened to the person before?"** The highest information per word available to a candidate. A new role means undefined expectations. A replacement means a story, and how readily the interviewer tells it is itself the answer.

**"What would make you say, a year from now, that this hire went unusually well?"** Success criteria, stated aloud, by the person who will apply them. If they cannot answer it, nobody has decided, and you would be walking into a job whose definition is still open.

Ask any of them of more than one interviewer. Asking the same question of the recruiter, the hiring manager and a future peer is the single most informative move available during an interview, and almost nobody does it — the differences between the three answers are worth more than any individual answer.

Everything below is the fuller set, grouped by what you are trying to find out rather than by when in the process you ask it. Most of these work at any point: the questions people save for the end are usually just the ones they were nervous about asking earlier.

## The Status Mechanic

`status` is complementary: when one person's position rises, the other's falls. An interview starts with that gap wide open. You want something, they decide, and everything you say is being assessed. That is not a mood — it is the structure of the room, and it does not go away because everyone is being friendly.

Your questions are the only move available that changes it. But only some of them do.

**Questions that ask for reassurance confirm the gap.** "Do you think I'd be a good fit?" "Is there anything about my background that concerns you?" "How did I do?" Each one asks the interviewer to evaluate you a second time, which is the lowered position doing exactly what the lowered position does. They also invite an answer neither of you wants to give in the room.

**Questions that probe the business close it.** Not because they are aggressive, but because they reposition you as someone deciding whether this is worth your time. That is the actual truth of the situation — a hire is a mutual bet — and asking as though it were true is what makes it true.

There is a second mechanic underneath. `initiation` is the craft of making a choice that implies the world rather than asking someone else to supply it, and the test is whether your line asserts a reality or defers to theirs. "What's the culture like?" defers, and you will get the brochure answer, because you have given them nothing to be specific about. "What do people find hardest about their first six months here?" asserts that something is hard, which is obviously true, and gives them permission to say what it is.

Every question below is built that way.

## Questions About the Role Itself

- What does the first ninety days look like for whoever takes this?
- What is the problem you are hoping this hire solves?
- Was this role created, or is it a replacement — and what happened to the person before?
- What does a good week in this role look like, concretely?
- Which parts of this job are most people surprised by?
- What proportion of the work is planned versus reactive?
- What is the hardest part of this job that would not show up in the description?
- What would someone need to get right in the first month to make everything after it easier?
- Who will I be working with most, and on what?
- What decisions will I be able to make on my own?
- Which ones will I need to escalate?
- What is currently being done badly that this role would take over?
- What is the piece of this job nobody enjoys?
- How has this role changed in the last two years?
- What would make you say, a year from now, that this hire went unusually well?
- What would have to happen for it to have gone badly?

## Questions About the Team

- How does the team make decisions when people disagree?
- Who has been here longest, and what keeps them?
- What has the team lost recently — a person, a project, a way of working?
- How do people here find out things they need to know?
- What is the team currently arguing about?
- What is something the team is proud of that an outsider would not notice?
- How much of the work is done together versus alone?
- What does a bad day look like here?
- Who on the team would I learn the most from?
- What does the team do that you would change if it were up to you?
- How do new people usually get up to speed — and how does that actually happen, rather than how it is meant to?
- What is the team's relationship with the rest of the company?
- Where does work come into the team from?
- What is the last thing the team pushed back on?

## Questions About the Manager

If you are speaking to your future manager, these are the highest-value questions in the interview. If you are not, ask to.

- How do you like to be kept informed?
- What does a good one-to-one look like to you?
- How do you give feedback — and when did you last give someone difficult feedback?
- What do you want people to bring you, and what do you want them to just handle?
- How do you know when someone on your team is struggling?
- What is something your team would say is hard about working for you?
- How many people report to you, and how much time do you have for them?
- How has your management changed since you started doing it?
- What is a decision you made recently that your team disagreed with?
- How do you decide what the team does not do?
- When you have had someone not work out, what usually went wrong?
- What do you do when you disagree with your own manager?
- How much autonomy do you actually have?
- What would you want me to tell you in my first month that you might not want to hear?

## Questions About How Success Is Measured

- How will my performance be judged in the first six months?
- Who decides that, and on what evidence?
- What does the review process actually look like here?
- When was the last promotion on this team, and what did it take?
- What is the difference between someone doing this job well and doing it exceptionally?
- Is there anything measured here that people quietly game?
- How is the team's performance judged as a unit?
- What targets is this team carrying this year?
- How confident are you about hitting them?
- What would have to change for someone in this role to be promoted?
- Has anyone in this role been promoted before?
- How do compensation reviews work, and when do they happen?

## Questions About the Company's Actual Position

Asked plainly and without needling, these mark you as someone who thinks about the business. Asked as a challenge, they read as arrogance — the difference is tone, not content.

- What is the biggest constraint on this company growing right now?
- Who is your most serious competitor, and what do they do better?
- What has changed in the strategy in the last year?
- What is the company betting on that might not work?
- How does this team's work connect to how the company makes money?
- What is the runway or funding position, as much as you can say?
- What is something the company believed two years ago that turned out to be wrong?
- Where does the company lose customers, and why?
- What is the thing you find hardest to explain to candidates?
- What kind of person does well here — and what kind of person does not?
- What has been the biggest surprise for you working here?
- Is there anything about the company right now that you would want to know if you were me?

## Questions to Ask at the End

**"Do you have any questions for us?" is the only part of an interview you can prepare exactly**, and it is the last thing in the room before they start comparing you to somebody else. Recency is doing real work here: whatever you ask is disproportionately what they remember when they talk about you afterwards.

Three things worth knowing before the list.

**Saying "no, I think you've covered everything" is read as a signal, not as efficiency.** It rarely reads as rudeness, but it does read as low investment — and against a candidate who asked something specific, it is the difference in a close decision. Have two prepared regardless of how thorough the conversation was.

**Two is usually right; four is too many.** They are almost always running to time and often have another candidate after you. Asking one signals politeness, two signals interest, and past three you have moved from interested to auditing them, which is a different and worse impression.

**If it genuinely has all been covered, say so and then ask anyway.** "You've answered most of what I had — can I ask the one thing I'd still want to know?" acknowledges the conversation and buys the question. This is far stronger than either inventing something or declining.

When there is time for one or two, these earn their place.

- What is your biggest hesitation about me, and can I address it now?
- What has not come up that you would want me to know?
- Is there anything in my background you would like more detail on?
- Who else would I meet if this goes forward?
- What is the timeline, and what happens between now and then?
- What would you want me to think about before we speak again?
- Is there anything you would want from me in the meantime?
- How many people are you speaking to at this stage?
- What has the process been like from your side?
- Is there anything about the way I have answered today that would help you to hear differently?
- What would make this an easy yes for you?
- Would it be useful for me to speak to anyone on the team?

Two of those need a note. **"What is your biggest hesitation about me"** is the one exception to the rule about not asking for reassurance, and it works because it does not ask for reassurance — it asks for an objection so that you can answer it. It is a high-status move and it takes nerve, which is why `be-brave` is the relevant thing rather than technique. Ask it only if you can hear the answer without flinching.

## Questions for a Final Round or a Senior Leader

Different room, different question. Someone two levels up cannot tell you what your Tuesdays look like, and asking them wastes the meeting.

- What are you personally spending most of your attention on this year?
- What would have to be true in three years for you to be pleased with this decision?
- Where is the organisation strongest, and where is it thin?
- What is the hardest trade-off you have had to make recently?
- What do you wish more people here challenged you on?
- What would you change about the company if it cost you nothing?
- How do you decide what gets funded?
- What is the thing you keep having to repeat?
- What is the risk that keeps you up?
- What do you look for in someone before you back them?

## Questions That Cost You the Offer

Each of these fails in a way worth understanding, and most of them fail on status.

- **"Do you think I'd be a good fit?"** Asks them to evaluate you again, in front of you. The honest answer is not sayable, so you get a polite non-answer and you have spent a question confirming the gap.
- **"How did I do?"** The same move with the mask off.
- **"What does the company do?"** Ends the interview quietly. It says you did not spend twenty minutes on the website.
- **"What's the culture like?"** Not fatal, but wasted — you will get the brochure, because you asked a question with no foothold in it. Ask what people find hard instead.
- **"Is there a lot of overtime?"** A reasonable thing to want to know, asked in the way most likely to be heard as reluctance. "What does the team's workload look like in a normal week, and in a bad one?" gets the same information.
- **"How quickly can I be promoted?"** Reads as wanting a different job to the one being offered.
- **"Can I work from home?"** in a first interview. The information matters; the timing signals it matters more than the work. Ask it once there is an offer to negotiate against, or ask it factually of the recruiter.
- **"Nothing, I think you've covered everything."** The most common answer and it is close to a no-show. If you genuinely have nothing, use the end questions above.
- **Anything you could have looked up.** Headcount, founding date, funding rounds. It spends your credibility on a fact.
- **A question that is actually a complaint.** "Why does the process have so many stages?" You may be right. It will still be heard as `blocking` — a move that refuses what was offered rather than building on it.

## How to Ask Them

**Ask the same question of three different people.** This is the single most informative thing available to a candidate and almost nobody does it. Ask the recruiter, the manager and a peer what the hardest part of the job is. Consistent answers mean the organisation knows itself. Answers that contradict each other tell you more than any single answer could.

**Write them down and take notes on the replies.** It looks like seriousness because it is seriousness, and it changes the register of the conversation. Nobody has ever thought worse of a candidate for having prepared.

**Ask fewer than you brought.** Three good questions asked properly, with a follow-up on the answer, beats eight fired off in sequence. The follow-up is where the real answer lives — the first reply is the version they give everybody.

**Listen to what they avoid.** A question that gets a fast, general, cheerful answer when you asked something specific has usually been dodged, and that is data. `safety-in-the-room` cuts both ways: if an interviewer cannot say anything difficult about their own team, that tells you what the team is like to be in.

**Do not perform interest you do not have.** Every interviewer has sat opposite someone asking a question from a list. The questions above only work if you want the answer.

## Practise the Status Part

**Playing both ends.** `status-transfer` runs the same scene high, then low, then shifts mid-scene. It is the fastest way to feel the difference between asking a question from a lowered position and asking the same question from a level one — which, in an interview, is most of what separates a good question from an identical one that lands badly.
