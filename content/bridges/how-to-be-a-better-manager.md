# How to Be a Better Leader: Coach Without Taking Over

Most people are promoted for being good at the work and then discover the job is not the work. What nobody explains is what the new job actually is, so they do the old one more loudly.

Improv directing has an unusually clear answer, because a director who takes over a scene destroys it visibly and immediately. The constraint forces a precise definition of the role, and it transfers.

## Your Job Is to Make Their Work Land

`be-supportive` is glossed on this site as: make your partner's choices look brilliant, and optimise for the ensemble rather than for yourself. For a performer that is a discipline. For a manager it is the entire job description.

It means your best idea is usually not the one that should be executed, because an idea you supplied and they execute is worse than a slightly weaker idea they own — the second one gets improved by the person doing it, and the first one gets delivered to specification.

The test is whether your team's output is better than your output would have been. Not equal to it. Better, because it is several people rather than one, and it is only several people if their choices are the ones being made.

## Side-Coaching: Adjust Without Stopping

Viola Spolin's core teaching method is `side-coaching` — calling adjustments *during* the exercise rather than stopping to explain. She described it as the most essential element in her games, and the defining rule is that players act on the note at the moment it is needed rather than stepping outside to consider it.

Her examples are instructive because of what they are not. "Use your where." "Help your fellow player." "Show, don't tell." Short, directional, and about attention rather than instruction. Nobody is told what to do.

Most management feedback inverts this. It is delivered afterwards, at length, in a scheduled meeting, and it explains rather than redirects. By then the work is finished, the moment is unrecoverable, and the note lands as assessment instead of help.

The transferable version:

- **Adjust while the work is live**, in a sentence, in the room. A redirect during is worth ten reviews after.
- **Point at attention, not action.** "What does the customer see here?" moves where someone is looking. "Change the button to blue" moves your hand onto their work.
- **Do not stop the thing to explain the thing.** Pulling someone out to intellectualise a note is how the note stops working — the same failure Spolin describes on stage.

## Your Status Is Permanently Raised

`status` is complementary: when one person raises, the other feels pressure to lower. A manager holds a raised position by default, in every exchange, whether or not they want it — which distorts more than most managers notice.

Your idle speculation is heard as a decision. Your question is heard as a criticism. Your silence in a meeting is read as disapproval by at least one person in it. None of this is paranoia on their part; it is the structure working normally.

The practical consequences:

**Speak last on anything you want honest input about.** Once you have stated a view, you are no longer receiving opinions — you are receiving positions relative to yours.

**Lower deliberately and often.** Saying "I don't know", "I got that wrong", or "what am I missing?" costs you nothing you actually need and is the fastest available way to make the room usable. `status-dynamics` calls the skill fluency; a manager who can only play high has one move.

**Assume agreement is not agreement.** A team that agrees with you quickly is usually a team that has learned agreeing is cheaper.

## Safety Is a Precondition, Not a Perk

`safety-in-the-room` is the condition under which people risk being visibly wrong. Without it you do not get worse ideas — you get the same ideas, later, after someone has checked they are acceptable.

The single most effective thing a manager does here is be visibly imperfect first. It is also the cheapest. A manager who says "here's a draft, it's rough" has done more for the team's willingness to share unfinished work than any statement about psychological safety will.

## One-to-Ones

The most common failure of a one-to-one is that it becomes a status update, which is information you could get from a document and which fills the time with the safest available content.

Three adjustments, all drawn from the sections above:

- **Let them go first, on their agenda.** Whoever sets the agenda sets the status, and if you open, the rest is a response to you.
- **Ask what they are stuck on, not how it is going.** "How's it going" has one socially correct answer, the same problem as "how are you" in small talk.
- **Say one thing you got wrong.** It converts the meeting from assessment into something two people are doing.

## Manager or Leader, and Why the Distinction Is Mostly Sold to You

The standard version — managers do things right, leaders do the right things — is a flattering way of saying that administration is beneath you, and it has produced a great many people who want to set direction and will not do a rota.

There is one real difference underneath it, and it is worth having.

**A manager's authority is granted. A leader's is conceded.** One arrives with the job title and works whether or not anybody rates you; the other is given by the group, provisionally, and can be withdrawn without anyone announcing it. Most people in charge of a team have the first and assume it supplies the second.

Improv is unusually useful here precisely because nobody on a stage has the granted kind. A director in rehearsal has some. A performer running a scene has none at all — no authority to instruct anybody, and yet groups visibly follow one person's read of what the scene is about. What they are responding to is not rank. It is that the person is demonstrably tracking what is happening and their contributions keep turning out to be useful.

That is the whole of the conceded kind, and it is trainable in a way charisma is not.

## The Decisions Only You Can Make

The instinct once you know all this is to stop deciding things, which is its own failure and a more annoying one than the first.

Three things do not get delegated, and pretending otherwise reads as evasion rather than empowerment.

**Direction, when the group genuinely cannot see the whole.** If you have information they do not — the budget, the reorganisation, what the client actually said — asking them to arrive at your conclusion by discussion is a performance of consultation. Say the thing.

**The call, when a decision has stopped improving.** Groups converge slowly and then stall, and past the stall the discussion is no longer surfacing new information. Ending it is your job. Waiting for consensus that will not arrive is not patience; it is leaving people in an argument you had the power to close.

**Anything with a consequence you carry alone.** If it goes wrong and the accountability lands on you rather than the group, the decision was always yours and involving people in it was borrowing cover.

The test for everything else is whether you would accept a different answer than your own. If you would not, you are not delegating a decision, you are running an audition for your own idea — and the room can tell, which costs you more than deciding openly would have.

## Where This Runs Out

Two limits, and they are the reason most leadership advice fails quietly.

**Technique cannot substitute for conditions.** Every method here assumes you can actually give people work worth doing, defend them, and change something when it is wrong. A team that is under-resourced, or whose leader has no authority upward, does not have a leadership-skills problem. Running better one-to-ones inside that is a way of being seen to do something, and people generally know the difference.

**You cannot make a room safe while you are the risk.** `safety-in-the-room` is set by the highest-status person present, which is you. If disagreement has ever cost somebody something — even mildly, even only visibly annoying you once — no amount of inviting challenge undoes it, because everybody is reading your reaction rather than your invitation and they are right to. The repair is slower and more specific than a technique: change what happens the next three times somebody disagrees, and let people notice on their own schedule rather than being told the culture has improved.

For the mechanics of the conversations this implies, [how to have difficult conversations](/how-to-have-difficult-conversations) covers the harder end and [how to give feedback](/how-to-give-feedback) the routine one.

## How to Practise It

**Directing without taking over.** `directed-scene` puts you outside a scene, calling adjustments while it runs. It is side-coaching with the stakes removed, and it exposes very quickly whether your instinct is to redirect attention or to specify the outcome.

**Supporting rather than contributing.** `yes-and-chain` requires accepting what came before and adding to it, with no reframes. Managers find this harder than they expect.

**Playing both ends of status.** `status-transfer` runs the same scene high, then low, then shifts. If your default is permanently raised — which it now is, structurally — this is where you find out.

For the group-level version of the same problem, see [team dynamics](/team-dynamics) and [collaboration skills](/collaboration-skills).
