# How to Be a Good Friend: The Support Most People Miss

Be there. Listen. Be loyal. Show up when it matters. All true, and none of it tells you what to do differently on Thursday — which is a problem, because the people who need this advice are rarely people who do not care.

The more useful question is what specifically goes wrong when someone who genuinely cares is still not much good at this. Improv has an unusually precise answer, because it spends its whole time on the mechanics of receiving what another person gives you.

## Most Bad Friending Is Well-Intentioned Blocking

`blocking` is the improv term for any move that refuses, deflects or undermines what your partner offered. On stage it is obvious. In friendship it is almost invisible, because every common form of it looks like helping.

**Solving.** Someone describes a difficulty and you supply a fix. This is `steering` — accepting the offer on the surface while redirecting toward your own predetermined outcome, which here is the conversation ending with the problem resolved. Most people share a problem to be accompanied in it, not to have it closed, and the fix arrives as a door shutting.

**Redirecting.** "That happened to me too —" and then your version. It feels like empathy and functions as `bulldozing`: attention leaves their offer and lands on yours. The tell is that they never get back to what they were saying.

**Reassuring.** "You're being too hard on yourself." "It'll be fine." Contradicting someone's account of their own experience is negation, however kindly meant. It tells them the thing they said was not acceptable as stated.

**Silver-lining.** "At least you learned something." This resolves your discomfort at their expense, which is the common thread in all four.

None of these come from indifference. They come from wanting to help and reaching for the fastest available way to do it.

## What Support Actually Is

`be-supportive` on this site is glossed as: make your partner's choices look brilliant, and optimise for the ensemble rather than for yourself. That is a stranger instruction than it looks, because it means your job is not to contribute your best material. It is to make theirs work.

In practice that is smaller than it sounds:

- **Receive before you do anything else.** Take in what was actually said, including the tone and the part they nearly did not say.
- **Extend rather than replace.** Your next line should come out of theirs. "What was the worst part?" keeps the thread. "Same thing happened to me" starts a new one.
- **Let it be unresolved.** Sitting with something unfixed is a real contribution and it feels like doing nothing, which is why people reach for advice instead.

`be-thankful` supplies the other half: treat what arrives as usable rather than evaluating it. A friend telling you something awkward, inconvenient or unflattering is handing you information about their actual life. Reacting to the awkwardness rather than the content is how people learn what not to tell you.

## The Advice Trap

Advice is not banned, and it is worth understanding why it goes wrong so often.

Giving advice raises your status and lowers theirs — `status` is complementary, so the person receiving it is momentarily positioned as the one who could not work it out. When they asked, that trade is fine. When they did not, it costs something, and the cost is usually paid later in what they stop bringing you.

The workable rule is to ask. "Do you want thoughts, or do you want to be annoyed about it for a bit?" is not a technique so much as a way of finding out which conversation you are in. Most people, asked directly, know.

## Repair Is the Actual Skill

Friendships are not distinguished by an absence of rupture. Every long friendship contains several, and the ones that last are the ones where somebody repaired them.

The improv version is `fracture-recovery` — what to do when two people are in different scenes and neither can work out why it feels off. The move is not to relitigate who was right. It is to name the state plainly and rebuild the shared reality from there.

Two things make repair possible:

**Go first, without a ledger.** Waiting for the other person to apologise first is the position that ends friendships, because both people can hold it indefinitely and both feel justified.

**Do not defend before you have understood.** Defensiveness is a block on an offer that happens to be critical. You can receive the complaint fully and still disagree with it afterwards — but doing it in that order is what makes the disagreement survivable. Reversing the order is what turns a small rupture into a permanent one.

## The Boring Part That Does Most of the Work

`trust` is an accumulated estimate built from many small exchanges, which means reliability beats intensity by a wide margin. Turning up predictably, remembering the thing they mentioned last month, answering the message — these are unglamorous and they are most of it.

The dramatic version of friendship, where you appear at the crisis and are magnificent, is the part people plan for and the part that matters least. By the time the crisis arrives, whether you are someone they call has already been decided by two years of small exchanges.

## How to Practise It

**Receiving without evaluating.** `gift-giving` hands over an undefined object that the receiver names and values specifically. It is the whole of `be-thankful` in two minutes.

**Extending rather than replacing.** `last-word-response` forces your reply to build from your partner's final words, which makes redirecting structurally impossible.

**Supporting rather than performing.** `yes-and-chain` requires accepting the previous statement and adding to it, with no reframes and no "yes, but".
