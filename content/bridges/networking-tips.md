# Networking Tips That Work If You Hate Networking

Most networking advice is technique: how to open, how to exit, how to remember names, how many cards to collect. It treats the discomfort as a skills problem.

It is not a skills problem. **Networking feels bad because it is framed as extraction** — you are there to get something, the other person is there to get something, and both of you can tell. No opening line fixes a conversation whose actual subject is what each party wants out of the other.

Everything below follows from changing that one thing.

## The Reframe

`offers` on this site is anything you hand someone that they can build with. It is the unit improv runs on, and it is a surprisingly exact description of what makes some people effortless in a room and others exhausting.

`be-supportive` is glossed as making your partner's choices look brilliant and optimising for the ensemble rather than yourself. On stage that is a discipline. At an event it is the entire difference between someone people are glad to see and someone people manage.

So the reframe is not "be more confident" or "have a better pitch". It is: **arrive with something to give, and give it without invoicing.** Introductions, a useful name, an answer to a problem they mentioned, the thing you read last week that is exactly their situation. Most of it costs nothing.

This is also the only version that works long term, because `trust` is an accumulated estimate built from many small exchanges. One event is one exchange. Whatever you were hoping to extract from it was never going to arrive that night anyway.

## Before You Go

- **Decide what you are useful for.** One sentence. Not your job title — what you can actually help someone with. Everything else follows from having an answer.
- **Have three things you are genuinely curious about.** Not questions to ask; things you want to know. Curiosity is not fakeable and its absence is obvious.
- **Find out who is going.** Two names you want to meet beats a room full of nobody in particular.
- **Set a leaving time before you arrive.** Knowing you leave at nine makes the first hour better.
- **Eat first.** Half of what people call social anxiety at these things is holding a drink on an empty stomach.
- **Aim for three conversations, not thirty.** Nobody has ever benefited from a room full of ninety-second exchanges.

## Joining a Conversation

The bit nobody teaches, and the bit that causes the most standing-around.

- **Look for an open triangle.** Two people angled slightly apart have left a gap; two squared off have not. This is `reading-the-room` at its most literal.
- **Approach the side, not the back.** Enter where somebody can see you arriving.
- **Wait for a beat, then say the obvious thing.** "Mind if I join?" works. So does "you look like you are having a better time than the rest of the room."
- **Listen for thirty seconds before contributing.** Joining a conversation by redirecting it is the single fastest way to be resented.
- **Ask about what they were already discussing.** Not a fresh topic. The one they chose.
- **If it is a closed pair, leave them.** Two people in a real conversation are the last people who want a third.

## What to Actually Say

The openers that work at an event are the ones that are easy to answer and go somewhere. Same rule as anywhere.

- What brought you to this?
- How do you know the organisers?
- What is the most interesting problem on your desk right now?
- What is taking up most of your time at the moment?
- What does your job involve that the title does not suggest?
- How did you end up doing this?
- What would make tonight worth it for you?
- Who here would you want me to meet?
- What is the last thing you changed your mind about in this industry?
- What is everyone in your world worried about that nobody outside it knows?
- What is something you would happily talk about for an hour?
- What has surprised you this year?

Two to avoid. **"What do you do?"** requests one noun and has nowhere to go — ask how they ended up doing it instead. And **"so what are you looking for tonight?"** names the transaction out loud, which is the thing everyone was politely pretending was not the point.

For the general version of this, see [questions to get to know someone](/questions-to-get-to-know-someone) and [conversation starters](/conversation-starters).

## The Status Problem

`status` is complementary: when one position rises the other falls. Approaching someone as a supplicant — wanting a job, an introduction, a favour — puts you in the lower position and makes the exchange transactional before anybody speaks.

That is not fixed by faking confidence. It is fixed by having something to offer, because then the exchange is actually level and both of you can feel it.

The specific moves:

- **Do not open with the ask.** If there is one, it comes at the end or in the follow-up, never in the first two minutes.
- **Do not apologise for existing.** "Sorry, I know you're busy, I'll be quick" lowers you and makes the other person manage your discomfort as well as their own.
- **Ask them something only they can answer.** Expertise questions raise the other person honestly, which is the one form of flattery nobody minds.
- **Be willing to disagree, lightly.** Total agreement reads as deference and is boring. `safety-in-the-room` cuts both ways — you are also deciding whether they are safe to be real around.

## Leaving Well

Leaving badly is what makes people dread these events, because it is the part that feels rude.

- **"I'm going to go and find a drink — it was really good to meet you."** Complete, kind, and nobody has ever been offended by it.
- **Introduce them to somebody before you go.** The single highest-value thing you can do at an event, and it makes the exit gracious rather than abrupt.
- **Leave while it is still good.** Same rule as everything else on this site.
- **Do not exit the room having spoken to nobody properly.** Three real conversations is a good night. Zero plus forty cards is not.

## The Follow-Up

This is where networking actually happens, and it is why the event felt pointless — the event is not the thing.

- **Within two days, or not at all.** After that you are a stranger with a reminder.
- **Reference the specific thing.** Not "great to meet you" — "you mentioned the hiring problem, this is the piece I was thinking of."
- **Send the thing you promised.** More relationships are built by the person who actually sent the link than by anyone's charisma.
- **Ask for nothing the first time.** The estimate has one exchange in it. Withdrawing now empties it.
- **Follow up again in three months with something useful and no ask.** This is the entire technique. Almost nobody does it.
- **Keep a note of what they are working on.** Not a CRM — three words in your phone. It is the difference between remembering someone and remembering their job title.

## If You Are an Introvert

The standard advice is to push through, which misreads the problem. The cost is not fear, it is depletion — and depletion is a bandwidth problem rather than a courage one.

- **Go early.** Small rooms are easier than full ones, and the people who arrive early are the ones who want to talk.
- **Get a job.** Help at the desk, pour drinks, mind the door. A role removes the question of why you are standing there and gives everyone a reason to talk to you.
- **One-to-ones over rooms.** Suggest coffee with one person you met rather than attending three more events.
- **Leave when you are done.** Staying past your capacity produces the worst conversations of the night and the memory that networking is awful.

## What Does Not Work

- **Working the room.** Thirty ninety-second conversations produce nothing anybody remembers, including you.
- **The elevator pitch.** It is a monologue, and it signals you were waiting rather than listening.
- **Business cards as a scoreboard.** A card is not a relationship; it is a note that a relationship did not happen.
- **Connecting without a message.** A bare request is asking for something while giving nothing, in a single click.
- **"Let me know if I can ever help."** Generous-sounding and it transfers the work. Offer something specific or nothing.
- **Only talking to people who are useful to you.** Everyone can tell, including the people you skip, and industries are much smaller than they look.

## Practise the Giving Part

**Handing something over without controlling it.** `gift-giving` has one person offer an undefined object and the other name it and find its value. It trains both halves of what makes someone good in a room: offering without steering, and treating what arrives as worth having.
