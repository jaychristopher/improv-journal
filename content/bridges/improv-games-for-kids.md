# Improv Games for Kids: What Works at Each Age

Most improv games fail with children for one reason, and it has nothing to do with the games. They were designed for adults who chose to be in the room.

An adult improv class is full of volunteers who have already decided they want to do this and are willing to be uncomfortable to get it. A Year 5 classroom is thirty people who were told to stand up. Every game on this page works; what changes is how you introduce it, how long you run it, and what you do about the four children who want to be watched and the four who would rather die.

## The Mistake That Wrecks Most Sessions

Telling them it is a comedy game.

The moment a child believes the task is *be funny*, the room splits into performers and hiders, and both halves have stopped doing improv. The performers start reaching for the biggest thing they can think of, which is `performing-cleverness` arriving decades early. The hiders correctly conclude that they cannot win and go quiet.

Every game below works when it is framed as a task with a rule rather than an opportunity to be entertaining. Nobody has to be funny to pass a clap around a circle. The laughter arrives anyway, and it arrives at things nobody was aiming for, which is the whole point and is worth not explaining.

## Ages 5 to 8: Rules You Can Hold in One Hand

At this age the constraint is working memory, not confidence — small children are frequently the least self-conscious people in any building. What they cannot do is hold a three-part rule while also inventing something.

So: one rule, physical, circular, and no waiting.

- **[Pass the Clap](/practice/exercises/pass-the-clap)** — a clap travels round a circle on eye contact. It is the single best first game at this age because it needs no words and no ideas, only attention to one person at a time.
- **[Zip Zap Zop](/practice/exercises/zip-zap-zop)** — three words, passed with a pointed hand. The rule is small enough to remember and fast enough that nobody is waiting.
- **[Sound Ball](/practice/exercises/sound-ball)** — throw an imaginary ball with a sound; catch it with the same sound. Children are better at this than adults, because they have not yet learned to improve what they were given.
- **[The Machine](/practice/exercises/the-machine)** — one child starts a repeating movement and sound, the others join one at a time until the class is a single machine. It is the earliest possible experience of `ensemble` and it needs no verbal invention at all.
- **[What Are You Doing?](/practice/exercises/what-are-you-doing)** — a mime game with a simple verbal trap. Works from about seven, once the contradiction is funny rather than confusing.

Run each for three or four minutes. A game that ends while they still want more is the one they will be willing to play next week.

### What to avoid before nine

Anything requiring a scene with two characters and a situation. It is not that they cannot do it — it is that a scene has no rules to fall back on, so the child who does not know what to do has nowhere to stand.

## Ages 9 to 12: Where Scenes Become Possible

This is the age where improv starts working properly, and it is also where self-consciousness arrives. Both at once.

The useful adjustment is that games now need a visible structure, so that a child who is nervous can succeed by following the rule rather than by being interesting.

- **[Freeze Tag](/practice/formats/freeze-tag)** — two play, anybody can call freeze and take over a position. The structure does the work: you always know what you are supposed to do next, which is take the shape you were given and justify it.
- **[One-Word Story](/practice/exercises/one-word-story)** — a story told one word at a time round the circle. Enormously forgiving, because no individual is responsible for the story.
- **[Gift Giving](/practice/exercises/gift-giving)** — mime handing somebody a present; they decide what it is and react. The receiver defines the gift, which quietly teaches that accepting is an active job.
- **[Questions Only](/practice/exercises/questions-only)** — a conversation entirely in questions. This one they will play at break.
- **[World's Worst](/practice/formats/worlds-worst)** — a category, then one line each stepping forward from a line. Best used sparingly at this age, because it is the closest to a joke competition on this page.

**On splitting the room.** Thirty is too many for one circle past about age nine — the wait between turns is longer than their attention lasts, and the far end will start a private conversation, which is a rational response. Two circles of fifteen with a teaching assistant on the second, or one circle running and one group watching with a job, works considerably better than one circle of thirty.

## Teenagers: The Only Real Problem Is Exposure

Teenagers can do everything on this page and most of them will refuse to, for a completely reasonable reason: the social cost of looking stupid in front of this specific group of people is, at fifteen, genuinely high.

Nothing about the games solves that. What solves it is removing the audience.

- **Run things simultaneously.** Everybody in pairs doing [Mirroring](/practice/exercises/mirroring) at once means nobody is being watched. Fifteen pairs is fifteen times the practice and none of the exposure.
- **Make declining cheap and public.** Say at the start that anybody can pass, and mean it. A group that has watched somebody pass without consequence will volunteer far more than a group that has not.
- **Use games where being wrong is the mechanic.** [Zip Zap Zop](/practice/exercises/zip-zap-zop) played fast means everybody fails constantly and visibly, which is the fastest way to make failure boring.
- **Then let them play [Bus Stop](/practice/formats/bus-stop) or [Party Quirks](/practice/formats/party-quirks)** once the room has warmed. Both give a strong character to hide behind, which is exactly what a self-conscious sixteen-year-old needs.

`hesitation` at this age is not a skill problem and should not be coached as one. It is a social calculation, and it changes when the calculation changes.

## Running It With a Class You Do Not Know

Four things matter more than the game list, and one thing matters before them: what you open with. The ordering principle holds at every age, and [improv warm-up games](/improv-warm-up-games) sets out the three-stage sequence in full.

- **Side-coach, do not stop.** `side-coaching` — calling adjustments while the game runs — is how Spolin taught, and it is the difference between a class that improves and one that plays. "Louder." "Look at them." "Next one faster." Stopping to explain kills the energy you spent ten minutes building.
- **Never make one child perform alone in front of the rest.** The single most reliable way to lose a room. Everything on this page can be run so that nobody is the only person moving.
- **Reward the obvious.** Children reaching for something clever is the same instinct as adults doing it and produces the same thin work. `obvious-choice` is worth praising out loud — the plain answer given quickly is the correct answer.
- **Stop early.** Every session should end while the class still wants another go. This matters more with children than any other group, because the memory they carry to next week is the last ninety seconds.

## What It Is Actually Teaching

Not comedy, and it is worth being clear with a head of department who asks.

The transferable part is that these games train paying attention to another person under mild pressure and responding to what they actually did rather than to what you had planned. That is the same skill a child needs to join a conversation, work in a group, or handle a moment they have not rehearsed. The comedy is a side effect, and treating it as the goal is the reliable way to lose the rest.
