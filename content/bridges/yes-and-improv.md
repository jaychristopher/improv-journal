# Yes And Improv: The Rule Everyone Gets Wrong

**"Yes, and" is improv's shorthand for accepting what your scene partner has established as true, then adding something to it. The "yes" is acceptance of the reality, not agreement with the character. The "and" is your contribution.** It is the most quoted idea in improvisation and the most consistently misdescribed, including by people teaching it.

The misdescription is specific and worth fixing first, because everything else follows from it.

## It Is Not About Agreeing

The popular version says: agree with everything, never say no, be relentlessly positive. That is not the rule, and a room run on it produces scenes in which nothing happens pleasantly.

The distinction that resolves it is between the **fiction** and the **people making it**. Two characters can disagree, argue, lie to each other, refuse each other and walk out. All of that is available. What cannot happen is one *performer* declining what the other *performer* has contributed.

If your partner says "we're in a hospital", you are not required to say "yes, and I love hospitals!" You are required to be in a hospital. You can hate it, want to leave, be furious about being brought there. What you cannot do is say "no, we're in a restaurant", because there is now no shared world to build in.

So the test is not whether you are being agreeable. It is whether the thing your partner added is still true one line later.

## What the "Yes" Actually Is

Acceptance is a perceptual act before it is a cooperative one. You cannot accept an offer you did not notice, and most failures to yes-and are failures of attention rather than of generosity — the person was composing their next line while the world was being built.

`offers` are much wider than dialogue. A sigh, a way of standing, a hesitation before answering, where somebody chose to stand in relation to you: all of it adds to the shared reality, and all of it can be accepted or ignored. The word "yes" is doing a lot of work for something that is mostly listening.

## What the "And" Actually Is

The "and" is the half that gets dropped, and dropping it is the more common failure among people who have learned the phrase.

Accepting without contributing produces a scene of pleasant nothing — two people confirming each other's statements. "We're in a hospital." "We certainly are." "It's very clean." "It is." Nothing has been refused and nothing is happening.

The "and" is where you are actually required to bring something: a reason, a feeling about it, a piece of information nobody asked for. `be-positive` is the underlying disposition; the phrase is the name for what it produces.

## What It Sounds Like

Both halves fail quietly, and they are easier to recognise in dialogue than in description. One offer, four replies.

**"I have been standing out here for twenty minutes."**

- **"No you haven't, you just got here."** — a block. The reality is denied, and the scene becomes an argument about whose account is true. `blocking` is the whole failure in one line.
- **"Have you? Why were you waiting?"** — accepts the fact and hands the work straight back. Nothing has been denied and nothing has been supplied.
- **"Twenty minutes. Wow."** — yes with no and. Agreeable, warm, and the scene is exactly where it was before anybody spoke.
- **"I know. I watched you park, walk past the door twice, and come back."** — accepts the twenty minutes and adds who these two are to each other. It is now a scene about somebody avoiding going in.

Only the last reply leaves the other person more to work with than they started with, and notice how little it cost: one fact accepted, one fact added.

### The version people find harder

If the principle were about agreement, this would not be a yes-and:

**"We should ship it Friday."** — **"Friday is not going to work, and I think the reason is the migration nobody has scoped."**

What is accepted is that a date has been proposed and a decision is now live. What follows is a flat refusal of the date and a genuine addition: a reason, and a piece of information the other person did not have. Set it beside "Friday doesn't work", which accepts and adds nothing and leaves the group where it was — and beside "Sure, Friday", which is agreement, and is the thing this principle is constantly mistaken for.

The test is not whether you said yes. It is whether the person who spoke before you now has more to work with.

## The Quieter Opposite

Most people answer `blocking` — flat contradiction. That is the textbook answer and the least useful one, since anybody doing it plainly gets told within a fortnight of starting.

What actually happens instead is `negation`, and its distinguishing feature is that it is polite. The words are received warmly and the substance is swapped out underneath them. "Yes, but." "Totally — although what if instead." Nobody has refused anything, so there is no moment anyone can object to.

Which gives you a diagnostic that works as well in a meeting as on a stage, and needs no vocabulary at all: **look at what happened to the last thing somebody offered.** If it was genuinely built on, the idea moved forward carrying their contribution. If it was negated, the conversation is now somewhere else and the person who offered has gone quiet.

## Where the Phrase Came From

Briefly, because the lineage is not what most people are looking for and is covered in full on `yes-and`.

It is not in Spolin and not in Johnstone. It was published in *Truth in Comedy* in 1994 by Halpern, Close and Johnson, having been in use as teaching shorthand around Chicago before that, and it reached the general public largely through Tina Fey's *Bossypants* in 2011. Its brevity is why it spread and also why it is so often mangled — four words cannot carry the distinction between the fiction and the performers, so the distinction fell off in transit.

## The Case Against Teaching It First

Not everyone in improv thinks it should be the first thing a beginner hears, and the objection is serious.

Mick Napier's argument is that "yes, and" as an opening instruction produces compliant, passionless scenes. A beginner given a rule will monitor themselves for compliance with it, and self-monitoring is exactly the state that makes people stiff and unspecific. They become careful rather than present. His position is that the phrase describes good scene work better than it teaches it — a lens for looking back at a scene that worked, not a directive to run in real time.

The practical version of this critique: **you can say the word "yes" while denying the reality entirely.** Yes-and lives in behaviour, not vocabulary, and a room can be scrupulously verbal about it while nobody is genuinely building anything. When it is actually working, nobody on stage is thinking about it.

## Using It Outside Improv

This is where most people meet the idea, and where it is worth being precise about what transfers.

**In meetings and brainstorming.** The mechanism that transfers is that an idea which gets built on becomes the group's idea, while an idea met with silence teaches everybody watching what speaking costs. Being the person who says what is useful about a suggestion before saying what is wrong with it changes what other people are willing to put forward — not because it is nice, but because the risk of contributing is calculated from what happened to the last person who did.

**In conversation.** The transferable part is small and specific: your reply should extend what was actually said rather than pivot to your own version of it. "That sounds exhausting — what was the worst part of it?" builds. "Oh, the same thing happened to me" replaces. Both feel like engagement to the person doing it, and only one feels like it to the person receiving it.

**In teaching and with children.** Accepting the contribution and adding to it is the whole of it. The failure mode is the adult who asks for ideas and then selects the correct one, which teaches the group that the question was a quiz.

## Where It Stops Working

Four places, and the first is the one nobody writing about this in a business context mentions.

**It can become an instrument of whoever has power.** In a room with a hierarchy, "we're a yes-and culture" can function as an instruction not to object to the senior person's idea. Improv's version assumes two performers with equal standing and a shared interest in the scene. A meeting is often neither. Where the power is uneven, the rule stops describing mutual construction and starts describing compliance, and the people who notice this first are the ones with least room to say so.

**Some things should be blocked.** Not every offer deserves to be built on. Work that is genuinely a bad idea, a proposal that will hurt somebody, a direction that is wrong — the correct response is to say so. Improv's version is bounded by a stage and a scene that ends in four minutes. Decisions are not.

**It is not a substitute for a decision.** Yes-and is a generative move, and generative moves are the wrong tool for converging. A group that yes-ands indefinitely produces an enormous pile of accepted ideas and no choice. The improv version has an editor and a running time; a project usually has neither unless someone provides them.

**Applied to a person who is not doing it, it is just absorption.** Two performers building is a system. One person accepting and extending while the other redirects everything is not a partnership with better manners — it is one person doing all the work, and the phrase gives that arrangement a flattering name.

None of these argue against the idea. They mark where the analogy runs out, which is a thing worth knowing about any principle borrowed from a stage.

## How to Practise It

`yes-and-chain` is the standard drill, and its value is that it is boring. With every other option removed, a group hears the failure the instant it happens — the chain simply stops accumulating, and everyone in the circle knows who and when. You cannot get that feedback from a discussion about whether people are being collaborative.

Two things worth doing outside a class. **Run one meeting where your first response to any proposal has to name something usable in it** before you say anything else; you will find out quickly how much of your normal contribution was redirection. And **watch one conversation you are not in**, tracking only whether offers come back. It is the fastest way to stop mistaking politeness for building.
