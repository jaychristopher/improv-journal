# How to Be More Articulate: It Is Not About Vocabulary

The advice is always the same. Read more. Build your vocabulary. Learn some rhetorical devices. Record yourself and count the filler words.

None of it addresses what is actually happening when somebody sounds articulate, which is this: **their sentences cost you almost nothing to process.** You are not admiring their word choice. You are noticing the absence of effort on your side.

That reframes the whole problem. Articulacy is not a supply of words. It is a discipline about the listener.

## Why You Struggle to Say What You Mean

Almost everybody who looks this up is carrying the same specific worry: that they sound less intelligent than they are, and that people are quietly grading them on it.

So it is worth answering the question directly. **You can be intelligent and inarticulate, and the two are close to independent.** Thought and speech run on different constraints. A thought arrives compressed and mostly wordless — a shape, a sense of how several things relate. Saying it out loud means converting that into a single line of words, in order, in real time, with no chance to revise. Being good at the first has almost no bearing on being good at the second, which is why the pattern is so common in people who read a great deal and say little.

That also explains the particular frustration of it. You are not failing to understand your own point. You are watching a good internal version come out as a worse external one, and the gap between the two is the thing that stings.

Four things cause most of it, and none is a shortage of words:

- **You are drafting and judging at the same time.** Composing the sentence while assessing how it is landing means two jobs competing for one channel, and the composing is what gets starved.
- **You started before the sentence had a destination.** Most stumbling is not a lost word. It is a sentence begun on the assumption that the end would arrive on its own, and then it does not.
- **You are trying to be complete rather than clear.** Fear of being misunderstood produces qualifications, and each one adds a clause. Past about two, the qualifications are what makes you misunderstood.
- **You have imagined the room as an examiner.** Speaking to people you have cast as judges makes every sentence a submission, and submissions get edited to death before they leave.

The useful part of all this: people who sound well spoken are not thinking faster than you. **They have fewer processes running.** That is a smaller and much more fixable difference than a gap in intelligence, and everything below is about closing it.

## Why More Vocabulary Makes It Worse

`cognitive-bandwidth` is the constraint. Attention is a limited pool, and a listener spends it on two things at once: understanding your words and thinking about what you mean. Every unusual word, nested clause, or piece of jargon shifts spend from the second to the first.

So the person reaching for the impressive word is not sounding clever. They are charging the listener for the privilege of decoding them, and the listener experiences that as harder work, not as intelligence.

`performing-cleverness` is the improv name for the failure — reaching for something impressive rather than something true. It kills scenes for exactly the same reason it kills sentences.

## The Actual Mechanism

Three things, and none is about words.

**One idea per sentence.** `overcomplication` is what happens when you try to carry three thoughts in one structure. The sentence acquires clauses, the clauses acquire qualifiers, and by the end neither of you knows what the subject was. Full stops are free. Use more of them.

**Say the point first.** Most unclear speech is a story told in the order it was discovered rather than the order it needs to be received. "So we looked at the numbers, and there was this thing in Q3, and it turned out..." — the listener is holding all of it open, waiting to learn what it is for. "We are going to miss the target. Here is why" costs nothing to hold.

**Stop when you have said it.** The most common articulacy failure is not stumbling, it is continuing. A finished point followed by three more sentences of hedging reads as uncertainty about the point.

## The Pause Is the Skill

People who sound articulate are not speaking faster. They are pausing in different places.

An inarticulate pause happens mid-clause, because the sentence was begun before it was resolved. An articulate pause happens *between* sentences, before starting, and it reads as consideration rather than as searching.

The habit that changes this is unglamorous: **finish the sentence in your head before starting it out loud.** Not the paragraph — the sentence. That is roughly a second of silence, and everyone else experiences it as composure.

This is where the usual advice about filler words gets it backwards. "Um" is not a verbal tic to be trained out. It is what a mouth does when it has started transmitting before there was anything to transmit. Remove the cause and the symptom goes.

## What to Do About Stumbling

- **Start over rather than repair.** A restarted sentence is clean; a repaired one carries the wreckage of the first attempt. "Let me say that again" costs less than three subordinate clauses of rescue.
- **Slow the first five words.** Almost all stumbling happens at the start, before the shape of the sentence exists. Get through the opening slowly and the rest arrives on its own.
- **Drop the run-up.** "So I guess what I was going to say is that basically..." is eleven words of throat-clearing while you find the thought. The thought arrives sooner without it.
- **Breathe out before speaking, not in.** Speaking on a held breath is what produces the tight, rushed delivery people mistake for nerves.
- **Let a question land before answering.** The two seconds after a question feel like an eternity to you and like thought to everybody else.

## The Words You Actually Need

`be-simple` on this site is glossed as preferring simple, obvious actions, because they create space for depth and are immediately usable by the other person. Applied to speech it is nearly a formula:

- **Concrete over abstract.** "It took three weeks" rather than "there were timeline implications."
- **Verbs over nominalisations.** "We decided" rather than "a decision was made." Nominalising hides who did what, which is often the point and always a cost.
- **The short word where it works.** Not because long words are bad, but because the short one is usually the one you mean. Reach only when the precise word is genuinely more precise.
- **Their vocabulary, not yours.** Jargon signals membership of a group the listener may not be in, and that registers before any reasoning does — see [the framing effect](/framing-effect).
- **Numbers and names.** Specifics are easier to hold than generalities, which is why "three people complained" sticks and "there has been some feedback" does not.

## Under Pressure

Being articulate in a prepared talk is a different skill from being articulate when someone asks you something unexpected in a meeting. The second is where people feel they fail, and it is the one improv actually trains.

The failure mode is `internal-computation` — attention going to monitoring your own performance rather than to the thing you are describing. You are simultaneously talking, listening to yourself talk, judging it, and revising. Four processes and one pool of attention.

What breaks the loop is not confidence. It is having somewhere else to put your attention: the actual content, the actual person, the actual question. `signal` is continuous, so you are already transmitting something — the choice is whether it is your point or your discomfort.

Two things that help immediately: **answer the question that was asked** rather than the one you prepared for, and **say the first true sentence** rather than the best one. `obvious-choice` holds here as everywhere — the first thought is connected to the question, the second is connected to how you want to appear.

## Practise It

**Compression under constraint.** `one-word-scene` gives each player a single word per turn. It sounds like a party game and it is the most direct training available in saying only the load-bearing thing, because there is room for nothing else.

**Building without reframing.** `yes-and-chain` requires accepting the previous statement and adding to it, with no restatement and no "yes, but". Most verbal clutter is restatement.
