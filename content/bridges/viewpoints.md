# Anne Bogart Viewpoints: The Nine Channels

**Viewpoints is a training method that splits physical awareness into nine named channels — tempo, duration, kinesthetic response, repetition, shape, gesture, architecture, spatial relationship and topography — and asks a performer to attend to one at a time rather than to everything at once.** Anne Bogart and Tina Landau set out the nine in *The Viewpoints Book* (2005), adapting a six-part framework that Mary Overlie had built out of postmodern dance three decades earlier.

The reason it endures is narrower than most accounts of it suggest. Actor training is full of instructions that describe a destination without naming a route: be present, fill the space, listen with your whole body, commit. A performer who could already do those things does not need to be told, and one who cannot is given nothing to act on. Viewpoints names nine things that can be observed from outside the body and adjusted on purpose. That is the entire trick, and it is why the method survives contact with people who dislike its vocabulary.

## The Nine Physical Viewpoints

Bogart and Landau sort the nine into two families. The division is not decorative — it maps onto two genuinely different kinds of noticing, one about when things happen and one about where.

### Viewpoints of Time

**Tempo.** The rate at which a movement happens. Not the mood of it, not the intention behind it — the speed. A performer crossing a room slowly and a performer crossing it quickly have made different choices before either has decided anything about character.

**Duration.** How long something goes on. Duration is the Viewpoint most often ignored by untrained performers, who end things at roughly the length that feels comfortable. Comfortable is short. Almost every piece of staging that reads as bold is something ordinary held past the point where the person doing it wanted to stop.

**Kinesthetic response.** The impulse to move triggered by movement outside you — someone else standing, a door closing, a sound. The discipline is to let the response happen at the speed of reflex rather than routing it through a decision. This is the Viewpoint that most resembles what improvisers already train as reacting before you have a plan.

**Repetition.** Repeating something in your own body, or picking up something from someone else's. Internal repetition is a gesture you return to. External repetition is the more interesting one, because it is how a group produces material nobody authored — a movement travels around a room, changing slightly, until it is the room's rather than anyone's.

### Viewpoints of Space

**Shape.** The contour the body makes. Bogart and Landau divide it further: lines, curves, and combinations of the two, made by the body alone, the body in relation to architecture, or the body in relation to other bodies.

**Gesture.** Split into two kinds, and the split is the useful part. A *behavioural* gesture belongs to the observable world — checking a watch, adjusting a collar, the things people actually do. An *expressive* gesture belongs to no realistic world and communicates an inner state or an abstraction. Trained performers can produce both on request and know which one they are doing, which is rarer than it sounds.

**Architecture.** The physical environment and what a body does about it. Walls, floors, doors, windows, the height of the ceiling, the mass of a pillar. Working in architecture means being genuinely affected by the room you are standing in rather than performing in front of it.

**Spatial relationship.** The distance between bodies, and how that distance changes. This is the Viewpoint that most reliably surprises people, because distance turns out to carry nearly everything an audience reads as relationship — long before anybody speaks, and often in contradiction to what they say.

**Topography.** The pattern traced on the floor. The landscape of movement through space: where the paths run, how wide they are, what shape they leave behind.

## The Vocal Viewpoints

Bogart and Landau extend the same logic to sound: **pitch, dynamic, acceleration and deceleration, silence, and timbre.** The claim is that a voice can be worked on with the same externality as a body — that you can adjust the duration of a silence without first deciding what the silence means.

This half is much less widely taught, and the honest reason is that it is harder to run in a group. Nine bodies moving in a room produce legible information; nine voices attending to timbre produce noise. Most training keeps the vocal work for smaller groups or pairs.

## Overlie's Six, and What Bogart Changed

Mary Overlie developed the Six Viewpoints — Space, Shape, Time, Emotion, Movement and Story, often abbreviated SSTEMS — in the 1970s, teaching at NYU's Experimental Theatre Wing, in the lineage of Judson Dance Theater.

The difference between her framework and the one that made the method famous is more than a count. Overlie's six are **materials of equal standing**, and the equality is the argument. Conventional theatre subordinates everything to story: movement serves the narrative, space serves the narrative, emotion serves the narrative. Overlie's refusal of that hierarchy is a deliberate position about what theatre is, borrowed from a dance world that had already stopped believing movement needed to mean something.

Bogart met the work at NYU and pulled it toward the rehearsal room, subdividing the physical material and appending a vocal set. The growth from six to nine is almost entirely a subdivision of Space: what Overlie treats as one material becomes shape, gesture, architecture, spatial relationship and topography. Dance could work with a broad category. Actors on a set, negotiating furniture and doors and each other, needed the finer grain.

Something is lost in the translation, and it is worth naming. Overlie's Emotion and Story do not survive into the nine. Bogart and Landau's version is more usable and less philosophically pointed — a training vocabulary rather than a claim about hierarchy. Overlie published her own account, *Standing in Space*, in 2016, in part because the version most people had learned was not hers.

## What a Session Actually Looks Like

**Open Viewpoints** is the standard format: an empty floor, a group, and one or more of the nine named aloud. There is no material to perform and no agreed outcome. A facilitator may name a channel to draw attention to it, then fall silent and leave the room to it.

Sessions run long — twenty minutes is short, an hour is normal — and the length is functional rather than indulgent. The first stretch is people making choices. Somewhere after that the choosing exhausts itself, and what follows is closer to responding.

The commonly reported effect is that a group begins agreeing on things it never discussed. The mechanism is unremarkable once stated: a named channel gives everybody the same thing to look at, and shared attention produces synchronised noticing. Ten people told to be aware of each other will each track something different. Ten people tracking tempo will register the same acceleration within a beat of one another, and act on it.

Narrower formats remove variables on purpose. **Lane work** confines everyone to parallel channels crossing the room, which eliminates floor pattern entirely and leaves the temporal channels carrying the whole session. **Grid work** permits only right-angle turns. Cutting the available choices leaves more attention for the ones that remain — the reasoning behind every constraint in the method.

## Composition: The Other Half

Viewpoints is usually taught alongside Composition, and the pairing is often dropped when the method travels — which is why so many people have done a Viewpoints session without ever finding out what it was for.

Composition is the practice of assembling short made pieces from assignments, often tight and strange ones: build a two-minute piece containing three entrances, one repeated gesture and a moment of unison. Viewpoints generates raw material and trains the perception; Composition is where that material gets selected, shaped and judged.

Without it, Viewpoints has no evaluative half, and a session can be enjoyable and go nowhere. The pairing is what makes it a rehearsal method rather than a warm-up.

## Where Viewpoints Falls Flat

The method is unusually easy to run badly, and the failures are consistent enough to be worth listing.

**It produces atmosphere and calls it work.** A room of people moving thoughtfully at varied tempos looks like something is happening. Often nothing is. The test is whether anybody could name what changed — a tempo that shifted, a gesture that got picked up — and if the answer is only that it felt good, the session was a mood.

**It rewards the already-comfortable.** Everything about the format — no words, no task, sustained attention to your own body in a room of people watching — is easy for performers who are physically confident and genuinely punishing for those who are not. Introduced too early, it teaches self-conscious people that they are bad at something they have not been taught yet. The nine channels are the remedy for that, not the cause, but only if they are named clearly and worked one at a time instead of left as an open floor.

**It has no built-in stopping condition.** Nothing in Open Viewpoints ends it, decides it worked, or distinguishes a strong session from a long one. That is what Composition is for, and groups that skip Composition tend to keep running sessions with no way to tell whether they are improving.

**The vocabulary can become the point.** Any specialised language produces people more interested in speaking it than using it. A performer who can categorise a gesture as behavioural but cannot make an interesting one has acquired the wrong half.

None of these are arguments against the method. They are what to watch for, and all four are visible from inside a session if anyone is looking.

## What Improvisers Get From It

Most improvisers meet Viewpoints as a workshop curiosity and never connect it to anything they do on stage. Four concrete connections are worth making, because they are where the method pays.

**Initiations.** The standard advice for starting a scene is to bring a strong choice, which in practice means arriving with an idea and announcing it. A Viewpoints-trained performer can start from tempo instead — walk in slowly, or fast, and let the pace be the offer. It gives a partner something to match or break before either of you has committed to a premise, which is exactly what a first line usually fails to do.

**Group scenes and walk-ons.** Six people on stage is where most teams fall apart, because everyone is deciding individually whether to enter. Repetition and kinesthetic response are the trained answers: enter on someone else's movement, echo a shape already in the space. Group scenes stop being six soloists and start being a room.

**Edits.** Deciding when to end a scene is usually taught as taste. Topography and spatial relationship make it observable — when the floor pattern has stopped changing and the distance between two people has been fixed for a while, the scene has finished moving even if the dialogue continues. That is a cue you can see from the back line.

**Two-handers that die standing up.** The talking-heads scene is a spatial-relationship failure before it is a content failure. Two performers at a fixed distance, facing out, have removed the one channel an audience reads most reliably. Moving is not the fix; *changing* the distance is.

For the technique's own place in the wider system, `viewpoints` covers what the channels are for. It is also worth holding `space-work` alongside it, since the two make opposite demands about what to treat as real.

## How to Start

You do not need a studio. Two useful entry points, both of which survive being done badly:

**Take one channel into an ordinary rehearsal.** Run a scene you already know, attending only to spatial relationship. Change nothing else. Most people find the scene changes anyway, because distance was doing work nobody had noticed.

**Run twenty minutes of open floor with a single Viewpoint named.** Tempo is the easiest to start with and the hardest to fake. Do not call anything else. Afterwards, ask the group what changed — if nobody can name something specific, run it again shorter.

The primary source is `ref-viewpoints-bogart-landau`. Read Overlie's *Standing in Space* after it, not before — it is the corrective, and it only lands once the nine have started to feel like the whole story.
