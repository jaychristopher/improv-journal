# Viola Spolin: The Woman Who Invented Theater Games

**Viola Spolin (1906–1994) invented the teaching method that all of modern improvisation is built on, and she did it before improvisation existed as a performance form.** Theater Games, the Point of Concentration, side-coaching, group evaluation — the apparatus every improv class in the world still runs on came out of her work, and almost none of it was designed for actors.

## She Came From Social Work, Not Theatre

This is the fact that reorganises everything else about her, and it is routinely left out.

Spolin trained under Neva Boyd at Hull House in Chicago in the 1920s, in a programme that used games, folk dance and storytelling as instruments of social development. The question there was not how to make better performers. It was how to get a room of immigrant and inner-city children — many with little English between them and the group — participating in something together.

She then spent years as drama supervisor for the WPA Recreation Project in Chicago, inventing games to teach dramatics to exactly that population. The constraint was severe and productive: whatever she designed had to work on people who had not volunteered, had no training, and in many cases did not share a language with the person next to them.

That is why the games still work on a corporate away-day, on a class of nine-year-olds, and on adults who have never performed. They were never adapted for non-performers. They were built for non-performers, and the theatre application came second.

## What She Actually Invented

Four things, and they are still the whole method.

**The Point of Concentration.** Every game gives the player one thing to attend to — the ball, the space object, the partner's exact words. It is narrow enough to occupy the part of you that would otherwise be monitoring your own performance, and the behaviour the teacher wanted arrives as a side effect of solving it. `be-present` on this site is a description of what a well-chosen Point of Concentration produces.

**Side-coaching.** `side-coaching` is calling adjustments while the game is running rather than stopping to explain. It keeps the player inside the experience, and it is the single largest practical difference between a class that improves and one that merely plays. Almost every improv teacher does this. Most do not know whose idea it was.

**Space work.** `space-work` and `environment` as the route into attention — handling an object that is not there requires you to decide where it is, how heavy it is and what it does, and that decision cannot be made from inside your own head. `physicality` before psychology is a Spolin position long before it was anybody else's.

**Evaluation as a group question about the problem.** Not notes about people. After the game, the group is asked whether the problem was solved — which removes the teacher as judge and makes the standard something everybody can see.

## The Credit Problem

Spolin is called the mother of improvisational theatre and is nowhere near as famous as the men who used her work.

Her son, Paul Sills, is the mechanism of both facts. He co-founded the Playwrights Theatre Club and then the Compass Players in 1955 — the first professional improvisational company in America — and then The Second City in 1959 with Bernie Sahlins and Howard Alk, and he ran his mother's games as the training method throughout. She came to Chicago to run workshops with Compass, and from 1960 ran improvisation workshops for the Second City cast directly.

So the line from her games to the entire American comedy industry is short, direct and familial. What travelled was the exercises; what mostly did not travel was her reason for them. Second City needed material for revues, and a method built to draw out non-performers became a method for generating sketches — which is a narrower use than she intended and the source of most of what later teachers spent decades arguing about.

## What Has Held Up, and What Has Not

Held up: essentially all of the mechanics. The Point of Concentration is a better account of how attention works under pressure than most of what has been written since, and it anticipates the cognitive-load argument this site makes by about twenty-five years.

Held up less well: the writing. *Improvisation for the Theater* is organised for retrieval by a teacher rather than for reading, and its prose is of its period. People who bounce off it are not missing something — they are using a handbook as a narrative, and [Improvisation for the Theater](/library/ref-spolin-improvisation-for-theater) sets out which half is worth your time.

The genuine dependency is the side-coach. Her games assume somebody outside the exercise calling adjustments while it runs, and without that they flatten into recreation — a room having a pleasant time and transferring nothing to their scenes. A great deal of bad corporate improv is Spolin games run with the coach removed.
