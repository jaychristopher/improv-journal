Two players stand together as one person — the world's leading authority on whatever the audience named — and answer questions one word at a time, alternating. Neither can finish a sentence alone and neither knows where it is going.

## What it trains

Genuinely shared authorship, which is much rarer than it sounds. In an ordinary scene you can have an idea and steer towards it over several lines. Here you get one word, and by the time the turn returns the sentence has moved somewhere you did not choose, so planning is not merely discouraged — it is mechanically impossible.

What that leaves is the plainest available next word, which is `obvious-choice` enforced by structure. It is also the clearest demonstration of `group-mind` in the shortform repertoire: the expert has opinions neither player holds, arrived at by nobody.

## Where it goes wrong

- **Steering.** The most common failure and the hardest to see in yourself. A player who keeps supplying words that push towards a joke they can see is playing solo with an accomplice, and the sentences stop being surprising.
- **Grammar disputes.** Somebody ends up trying to rescue a sentence that has become ungrammatical. Let it break. A broken sentence is funnier than a repaired one and the audience is not marking it.
- **Slowing down to be clever.** The pause before a word is always visible and never rewarded. Speed is what makes the form work.

## The honest objection

It is not really scene work, and the skill it isolates does not transfer as directly as its popularity suggests. Nobody improvises a scene one word at a time, and a performer can be excellent at this and still unable to build with a partner across a whole scene, because the constraint is doing the collaborating for them.

Its genuine use is diagnostic and it works on a specific person: the one who agrees pleasantly in every scene and is quietly running their own plan underneath. That habit is invisible in a normal scene and completely obvious here, to them and to everybody watching, within about thirty seconds.
