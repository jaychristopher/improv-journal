**Trains:** Be Present — attention that can be redirected instantly, and a group focus that lives outside anyone's head. The most widely played warm-up in improv, and the one most often played badly.

## Setup

Everyone stands in a circle. One player sends a "Zip" to someone else with a clap or a point and direct eye contact. That person immediately sends "Zap" to a third person. The third sends "Zop". The next sends "Zip" again, and the cycle continues. Word, gesture and eye contact all land at the same moment, aimed at one specific person.

Anyone who says the wrong word, hesitates, or sends without a clear target is out — or, in the version worth playing, simply acknowledges it and the game restarts. Elimination is a choice about what kind of room you want.

## Why it works

The game has almost no content. Three syllables in a fixed order is not a mental challenge, which is exactly the point — there is nothing to think about, so any hesitation is visibly not about difficulty.

What it actually trains is where attention lives. A player who is watching the whole circle catches their turn without effort. A player who is watching themselves — checking whether they are about to be caught out, rehearsing the next word — misses it, and everyone sees them miss it. `internal-computation` is invisible in a scene and unmissable here.

## Side-coaching

"Eyes first, then the word." "Send it to a person, not to the room." "Faster — you already know the word." "If you dropped it, drop it out loud and keep going." "Nobody is trying to catch you out."

## What to notice

The pace at which the circle stops being three separate signals and becomes one continuous one. Early on you can see each transaction: a send, a receive, a small delay, another send. When it is working the delay disappears and the group sounds like a single rhythm instrument. That is the smallest observable version of `group-mind` — and it arrives within about ninety seconds, which is why the game earns its place at the top of a session.

## Common failures

**Sending to nobody.** A vague point at the middle of the circle. The receiver has to work out whether they were meant, and by then the rhythm is gone. Specificity is the whole skill and it is the same skill as `initiation`.

**Speeding up to be impressive.** Rushing produces errors that look like energy. The useful speed is the fastest one at which everyone still lands their eye contact.

**Playing to survive.** In elimination form the incentive inverts: players start protecting themselves rather than serving the circle. If the room is new, drop elimination entirely — the mistake is the interesting part, and a warm-up that punishes it teaches the wrong reflex before the session has started.

## Variations

- **Add a fourth word** ("Boing" to reverse, "Zoom" to skip) once the base rhythm is stable. Adding load is how you find out whether the rhythm was real.
- **Silent version.** Gesture and eye contact only. Strips out the one thing players were relying on and shows how much of the game was already visual.
- **Whole-name version.** Send by saying the receiver's name instead of the syllable. Slower, less rhythmic, and far better on a first meeting.

## Lineage

A staple of the theatre-games tradition that runs from Viola Spolin's work into almost every improv curriculum taught since. Its exact origin is not documented — like most circle warm-ups it was passed between rooms rather than published — and the name varies ("Zip Zap Zap", "Zip Zap Boing"). What is consistent everywhere is the function: it is the game a teacher reaches for when a group needs to stop thinking and start looking at each other.
