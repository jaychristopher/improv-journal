**Trains:** Deep attention, body awareness, ensemble connection, yielding/leading as a spectrum. Viola Spolin's core exercise — taught at every school, in every first class.

## Setup

Two players face each other. Player A moves slowly; Player B mirrors A's movement as precisely as possible. Sustained eye contact. No speaking.

## The progression

1. **A leads, B follows.** B's job: match A so exactly that an observer can't tell who initiates. A's job: move slowly enough that B can follow. Speed is the enemy — the slower you go, the deeper the connection.
2. **Switch.** B leads, A follows. Same discipline.
3. **Neither leads.** This is the exercise's peak — "Who is the mirror?" becomes unanswerable. Both players follow. Movement emerges from the space between them rather than from either individual. Spolin called this the exercise reaching its ideal state. Contemporary teachers call it **"Follow the Follower."**

## Side-coaching

"Slower." "Stay with your partner's eyes." "Don't anticipate — respond." "Who is the mirror? Can you tell?" "Let the movement come from neither of you."

## What to notice

The qualitative shift between stages 1-2 (one leads, one follows) and stage 3 (neither leads). In stage 3, a kind of group mind emerges at the dyad level — two people moving as one organism. This is the smallest possible version of ensemble coherence. If you can do it with one person, you can learn to do it with seven.

It is also one of the few exercises that survives leaving the theatre intact. Mirroring needs no premise, no performance and no explaining, which is what qualifies it as [five-minute team building](/5-minute-team-building) rather than as an activity needing a facilitator.

## Common failures

Moving too fast (ego wants to be interesting). Anticipating instead of responding (planning leaks into the body). Breaking eye contact to check your own body (internal computation). Leading when you're supposed to follow (steering).

## The deeper lesson

Every improv scene is a mirror exercise with words. You're never just generating — you're always responding to what your partner gives you, and they're responding to what you give them. The mirror makes this visible. When it works, neither player leads. The scene leads.

## Variants worth knowing

Spolin published several, and they train different things.

- **Mirror with activity.** The leader performs a recognisable task — shaving, making tea — rather than abstract movement. Harder, because the follower has to track intention as well as position, and it is the variant closest to what a scene actually asks.
- **Mirror speech.** The follower speaks along with the leader in real time, a fraction behind, trying to close the gap. Deeply uncomfortable and the fastest way to demonstrate that anticipating and receiving are different activities.
- **Group mirror.** One leader, the whole room following. Useful for a large class and much weaker as training, because with twenty followers nobody is individually accountable and the leader gets no information back.
- **Three-person mirror.** A triangle with no designated leader. The step up from the dyad and the point at which `group-mind` stops being a metaphor.

## What the mimicry research does and does not show

There is a real phenomenon underneath this exercise, and it is worth being precise about its edges.

Chartrand and Bargh (1999), in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, documented what they called the chameleon effect: people unconsciously copy the postures and mannerisms of those they interact with. In their second experiment, confederates who deliberately mimicked participants produced smoother interactions and were liked more. In their third, people who scored higher on dispositional empathy mimicked more.

So mimicry and rapport genuinely travel together, and the more empathic people do more of it.

What the work does not establish is the thing an improv class usually implies. It describes mimicry that happens *without intention*, in ordinary conversation. It does not show that a deliberate mirroring drill produces that capacity, and no study makes that link. The exercise is defended by analogy to a real effect rather than by evidence that the drill causes it.

## The honest objection

Mirroring is the most-run exercise in improv and the transfer is assumed rather than demonstrated.

What it unambiguously trains is synchrony — two bodies tracking each other closely and continuously. What a scene mostly requires is `active-listening` for content: what was established, what it implies, what your partner needs next. Those are not the same skill, and a group can become genuinely excellent at the first while still blocking constantly in the second.

The strongest case for it is narrower and more defensible than the usual one. It puts a room into contact with no words, no ideas and nothing to be judged on, which makes it an unusually good opener for people who have not yet decided the room is safe — `trust` built at the cheapest available price. Treated as a warm-up that does that job, it is close to unimprovable. Treated as the thing that teaches listening, it is doing considerably less than its reputation suggests.

## Lineage

Spolin, *Improvisation for the Theater*, Ch. 3 ("Workshop Exercises") — the core "Mirror" exercise with variants (Mirror Speech, Mirror with Activity). The "Follow the Follower" stage represents the exercise's ideal endpoint — a concept developed across both Spolin's and Johnstone's pedagogies, though neither coined the phrase as a formal exercise name.

## Traditions diverge

Spolin frames mirroring as a concentration/awareness exercise (the POC is on reflecting the partner). Johnstone frames related exercises through status and control — his interest is in what spontaneity emerges from yielding. Same exercise, different lens.
