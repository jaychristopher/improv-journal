Wimping is accepting an offer and adding nothing to it. Johnstone's term for the politest way to stop a scene, and the hardest failure to see from inside it, because nothing was refused and everybody was agreeable throughout.

## Why it is harder to spot than blocking

A block is audible. Somebody says "we're in a hospital" and somebody says "no we're not", and the room knows immediately that something went wrong. Wimping produces no such moment. "We're in a hospital." "We certainly are." Both performers accepted, neither denied anything, and the scene is precisely where it was — except that a turn has been spent and the audience has quietly stopped expecting anything.

A wimping scene can run for four minutes on warmth alone. Everyone is pleasant, everyone agrees, and afterwards nobody can say what it was about. Performers usually diagnose it as low energy and try to fix it by being louder, which produces a loud scene about nothing.

## The tell

Agreement that carries no new information. "Yes." "Definitely." "I was just thinking that." "You're right." Each one is a complete acceptance and an empty turn, and a scene made of them is `yes-and` with the second half removed — the half that is actually required, and the one most people drop.

The test is not whether you agreed. It is whether the person who spoke before you now has more to work with than they did.

## Where Johnstone put it

Wimping sits inside a taxonomy of avoidance in *Impro for Storytellers* alongside cancelling — making an offer and immediately withdrawing it — along with bridging, hedging and the rest. The organising claim is that outright denial is the least common way improvisers refuse, and the more usual failure is a performer who technically accepts everything while committing to nothing, because commitment creates obligations they would rather not have.

That is why it is treated as a member of the `blocking` family rather than as a separate problem. The effect on the scene is the same as a denial. Only the manners differ.

The most-taught first exercise in improv drills this by accident. [Yes, Let's](/practice/exercises/yes-lets) asks a room to accept every proposal instantly and requires nobody to add anything, so a group that runs it a great deal produces students who are enthusiastic, agreeable and contributing nothing.

## The same move offstage

This is the one avoidance behaviour that transfers exactly. A meeting where every contribution is met with "good point" and no addition is a wimping scene with a budget, and the person doing it is usually being deliberately agreeable rather than lazy. [How to stop people pleasing](/how-to-stop-people-pleasing) covers the version that has become a habit, where the accepting is real and the adding has been trained out.

The fix is the same in both places and it is small. Add one thing that was not already true. It does not have to be clever, and it is the entire difference between a turn and a contribution.
