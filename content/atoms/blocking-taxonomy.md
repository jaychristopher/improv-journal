Johnstone's central observation about refusal is that almost nobody does it out loud. Flat denial — "no we're not" — is rare, easy to spot and easy to coach. The refusals that actually kill scenes are polite, cooperative-looking and nearly invisible, and he catalogued them because each one avoids a different obligation and therefore needs a different fix.

## The five

### Wimping

Accepting and adding nothing. "We're in a hospital." "We certainly are." No denial, no contribution, and the scene is exactly where it was. `wimping` has the full account.
*The obligation avoided:* having an opinion. *The fix:* add one thing that was not already true. It does not have to be good.

### Cancelling

Making an offer and withdrawing it in the same breath. "I've got a gun — no, I'm joking, it's a lighter." The performer felt the size of what they had done and took it back before anybody had to deal with it.
*The obligation avoided:* consequences. *The fix:* let the offer stand for thirty seconds before deciding it was too much. It usually was not.

### Bridging

Delaying action with preparation. Two people who need to rob the bank spend six minutes discussing how they will rob the bank. It looks productive and it is a stall — the planning is the avoidance.
*The obligation avoided:* the actual event. *The fix:* start after the decision. Enter already doing the thing.

### Hedging

Offers made so vague they cannot fail. "There's something weird about this place." "Something's happening to me." Unfalsifiable, uncommitted, and impossible to build on because nothing specific has been claimed.
*The obligation avoided:* being wrong. *The fix:* name it. The specific version is riskier and is the only one your partner can use.

### Pimping

Forcing your partner to do the creative work. "Why don't you show everyone your famous dance?" The speaker has made an offer that costs them nothing and handed the bill to somebody else. Many contemporary teachers have retired Johnstone's word for this while keeping the observation; the behaviour is usually taught now as putting your partner on the spot.
*The obligation avoided:* your own exposure. *The fix:* do it first, or do it with them.

## What they have in common

Every one is a bandwidth-preserving move. Each buys the performer a moment in which they are not yet committed to anything, which is exactly the state `internal-computation` describes and exactly the state a scene cannot be built from. That is why they cluster: they are not five unrelated errors but five exits from the same room.

It also explains why the standard note — "stop blocking" — so rarely works. A performer who is hedging is not refusing offers; they are making offers that cannot be caught. Telling them to accept more is answering a question they did not ask.

## Using it as a diagnostic

The practical value is in ordering the question. Watching a scene that is not working, ask what the performers are avoiding rather than what they are doing wrong. An opinion, a consequence, an event, being wrong, or their own exposure — the five answers point at five different notes, and giving the wrong one is how a performer comes out of a class having been corrected for something they were not doing.

`diagnosing-scene-failure` covers the wider version of this, where the fault is in the scene's structure rather than in a performer's avoidance.
