**Mick Napier. *Improvise: Scene from the Inside Out.* Heinemann Drama, 2004; second edition, Meriwether, 2015.**

The book that argues the rules are the problem. Napier founded the Annoyance Theatre in Chicago, and this is the most useful statement of what that school thinks improv teaching gets wrong.

For the lineage, see [the Annoyance tradition](/traditions/annoyance). This page is about the book.

## The argument it is making

Most improv instruction hands a beginner a list — do not deny, yes-and, do not ask questions, support your partner, stay in the moment — and then asks them to start a scene. Napier's claim is that the list itself is what produces the paralysis, because a performer running seven checks cannot also be present. `internal-computation` is the state the rules reliably create.

His replacement is short enough to use under pressure. Do something. Notice what you did. Commit to it. Everything else is downstream of having already acted, and a scene begun with a choice can be repaired, while a scene begun with nothing cannot.

The most quoted line inverts the ethic it was written against: **take care of yourself first**, and by doing so you take care of your partner. Read carelessly that sounds like selfishness. What it means is that arriving with a strong choice gives your partner something to play with, and that `be-supportive` practised as deference — waiting, agreeing, adding nothing — hands them a vacuum and calls it generosity.

## Who it rewards, and who it does not

Anybody stuck. Performers who have been taught carefully and have become cautious tend to find this book the thing that unsticks them, and it is the standard recommendation for a student whose scenes are technically correct and lifeless.

It is a corrective rather than a foundation, and read first it does damage. A beginner who takes "take care of yourself" without the context behind it becomes exactly the player everybody dreads, and Napier is arguing against an excess most beginners have not yet acquired. It works on people who already have the discipline it is telling them to loosen.

## How to read it

Second, and the order matters more here than for any other book in this library. Not because it is difficult — it is short, direct and frequently funny — but because it is an argument against something, and a reader who has not yet acquired the habit it attacks will take the conclusion without the correction.

The right moment is recognisable from inside. It is when your scenes have stopped being wrong and started being dull, and when you can feel yourself running a checklist while somebody is talking to you. Read then it works quickly, and a good deal of it will land as something already half-known.

## What it does not contain

Group forms, long-form architecture, or a curriculum. There is nothing on how a show is shaped, how beats connect, or how to teach any of it. The book is almost entirely about the first ten seconds of a scene and about the person having them.

![A scale running from ten seconds to a whole curriculum, with the book's coverage marked at the small end and everything larger left to other sources.](/images/where-the-book-stops.svg)

*A book can be right about its subject and useless about everything next to it, which is a property of scope rather than of quality.*

That narrowness is deliberate and it is why the book still works. Napier is not offering a system to replace the one he is dismantling — doing that would have reproduced the problem exactly — so the missing architecture is meant to come from somewhere else.

## The Paradigm Lost journal

The most unusual thing in the second edition and the reason to prefer it. Napier kept a journal while directing *Paradigm Lost* at Second City and reproduced it in full: not a tidied retrospective but the record as written, including the stretches where the show was not working and he did not yet know why.

Almost nothing else in the improv literature does this. These books are written from the far side of competence and describe what good practice looks like once you have it. This one describes the middle of a process that might have failed, by somebody who could not see the ending from where he was standing. For a director it is the most instructive part of the book, and it has very little to do with the argument the rest of it is making.

## Key contributions to the knowledge graph

The case that `commitment` matters more than the content of a choice — *"it is far more important that you DO something than WHAT it is you actually do"*; `hesitation` reframed as the actual failure mode rather than a symptom of one; entering a scene already in motion rather than neutral; and the argument underneath `obvious-choice`, that the search for a better idea is what costs you the scene.

## Editions, and which one to buy

The 2004 Heinemann edition is the one usually cited. The second edition, published by Meriwether in August 2015, is expanded to 248 pages with a foreword by Stephen Colbert and a full reproduction of Napier's journal from directing *Paradigm Lost* at Second City — that journal is the most candid account of a directing process in the improv literature and is worth the upgrade on its own.
