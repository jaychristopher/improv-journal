**Amy C. Edmondson. "Psychological Safety and Learning Behavior in Work Teams." *Administrative Science Quarterly* 44(2), 350–383, 1999.**

The paper that gave psychological safety both its definition and its measure, and the anchor beneath what this site claims about groups.

Edmondson studied 51 work teams in a manufacturing company and defined team psychological safety as a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking. The risk she means is specific and unglamorous: asking the obvious question, admitting the mistake, disagreeing with the senior person. Teams higher in it showed more learning behaviour, and learning behaviour mediated the relationship between safety and team performance.

## Key contributions to the knowledge graph

- Safety is a property of the group, not of the brave individual inside it — which is why `safety-in-the-room` is built rather than summoned
- The mechanism is voice: safety does not stop errors happening, it makes them sayable, and a sayable error is one the group can work with
- It is a *shared belief*, so it can be measured, and it can be absent in one team while present in the team next door under the same management

## The finding people invert

Edmondson's original data contained a result that reads as a paradox: higher-safety teams reported *more* errors, not fewer. The reporting rose because concealment fell. Anyone using this work to argue that safe teams make fewer mistakes has the causal arrow backwards, and the improv version of the same error is treating a quiet room as a comfortable one.

## Why it sits in an improv library

The construct arrived independently at what ensemble practice had been asserting without evidence — that the willingness to make an unpolished offer is a function of what the room does with unpolished offers. Where the two diverge is scope: Edmondson is describing conditions for candour in organisations that mostly execute known work, while improv asks a group to generate unknown work continuously, which is a heavier load on the same variable. `vulnerability` covers what the load feels like from inside.
