A category is announced — world's worst doctor, world's worst person to sit next to on a plane — and the cast take turns stepping forward to deliver one image of it before stepping back. Nothing carries over. The next person starts clean.

## What it trains that nothing else does

An offer with no consequences. Every other form asks you to live with what you said: the scene continues, your partner builds on it, and a weak choice stays in the room. Here the offer lasts three seconds and then the slate is wiped, which makes this the cheapest place in improv to be wrong.

That is precisely why it works on people who have frozen. Somebody who cannot start a scene can almost always step forward and say one line about a bad dentist, because the thing stopping them was never invention — it was `hesitation` about what the invention would commit them to. Remove the commitment and the supply turns out to have been there all along.

It is also repeated `initiation` with the follow-through deleted. `first-line-drill` isolates the same moment more deliberately; this one hides it inside a game, which is often the only way to get a nervous room to do it forty times in five minutes.

## Where it goes wrong

- **Waiting for a good one.** The rhythm is the game. A player holding out for something clever while three others step forward and back has misread what is being trained, and the pause costs more than a mediocre line would have.
- **Explaining instead of showing.** "World's worst wedding speech" delivered as a thirty-second monologue is a sketch, and the form is a snapshot. One image, one line, out.
- **Only the confident stepping forward.** With no rotation, the same four people take every turn. Running it in a fixed order costs nothing and is usually better, because the point is the reps rather than the highlights.

## The honest objection

This trains punchline production, not scene work, and the two pull against each other. Somebody who has become good at World's Worst has practised arriving with a finished joke, which is close to the opposite of what `game-of-the-scene` asks — noticing what is already happening and building from it. It is a warm-up, and treating it as a skill to develop tends to produce performers who enter scenes with material rather than attention.

Used for what it is — a way to get a cold or frightened room making offers quickly — there is very little that does the job better.
