**Trains:** working under enough load that deliberation becomes impossible. A rhythm game whose real function is to flood attention until players stop planning and start reacting.

## Setup

A circle. One position is Big Booty; the rest are numbered from one, clockwise. Everyone keeps a steady beat — usually thigh-slap, clap.

On the beat, a player says their own name and then someone else's: "Big Booty, number three." Number three answers on the next beat: "Number three, number six." And so on, without breaking rhythm.

A player who fumbles the beat, says a wrong number, or answers when they were not called goes to the end of the line — and **everyone between them and the end shifts up a number**. That renumbering is the entire game.

## Why it works

The rules are trivial. What makes it hard is that your own number keeps changing, so the answer you rehearsed is wrong by the time your turn arrives.

![One player's position across three rounds: as the line shifts, the slot they occupy moves left, so an answer prepared for the old slot is already wrong.](/images/your-number-keeps-moving.svg)

*The rhythm is what makes this a moving target rather than a puzzle.*

That is deliberate. `cognitive-bandwidth` says attention is a limited pool and that internal computation and external response compete for it. Most warm-ups ask players to stop planning. This one makes planning useless, which works considerably better than being told. Within a few rounds the only players still in are the ones who have given up tracking and are simply listening.

## Side-coaching

"Keep the beat, even when you get it wrong." "Don't count — listen." "Say your number as if you already know it." "Whoever just moved, look up."

## What to notice

The moment a player stops rehearsing. It is visible: the shoulders drop, they look at the circle instead of the floor, and their timing improves immediately after they stop trying to be ready. The game is a live demonstration that preparation is costing them the thing they were preparing for.

Also notice how the rhythm holds the room together. When the beat survives a mistake, the group is functioning as an ensemble. When a mistake stops the beat, it is still a collection of individuals taking turns.

## Common failures

**Letting the beat drop.** The rhythm is the constraint. Without it players take as long as they like, and the game stops doing anything.

**Playing to win.** Reaching the Big Booty position is not the point; being unable to plan is. Groups that treat it as a competition get slower, not faster.

**Cruelty on elimination.** Nobody is out, but the shift down the line is a small public failure repeated many times. In a room that has not built any trust yet, that is the wrong first experience — see `safety-in-the-room`. Play it in week three, not in the first ten minutes.

## On the name

The name is a liability in classrooms, corporate rooms and any group you have just met, and nothing in the game depends on it. "Big Bootie", "Big Fish" and "Zoom Boom" are all in circulation, and renaming it costs you nothing. Reading the room about this is itself the skill the game claims to teach.

## Variations

- **Names instead of numbers.** Slower and far more useful with a group who do not know each other.
- **Two beats between calls** for a new room, tightening to one as it settles.
- **No elimination.** Keep the numbering fixed and simply continue through errors. Loses the renumbering pressure but keeps the rhythm work, which is the half that transfers.
