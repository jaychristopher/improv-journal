One scene, held for the whole set. Same location, same people, time moving roughly as it moves in life. The monoscene removes the edit, and removing the edit changes everything about how the show has to be built.

## What the missing edit costs

Every other long form has an escape hatch. When a scene stops going anywhere, somebody sweeps it and the next one starts clean, and the audience forgives the weak scene because the show moved on. A monoscene has nowhere to move on to. If the scene stalls at minute nine, the stall is the show for as long as it takes to find the way out.

That constraint is the whole pedagogical point. Performers who rely on the edit never learn what to do at the moment a scene runs out of obvious material, because they have always been rescued before finding out. Held in one scene, you discover that the way out is almost never a bigger idea — it is a smaller one. Somebody notices what the other person is doing. Somebody finally says the thing they have been not-saying for six minutes.

## How new material gets in

Anything another format would deliver with a new scene has to arrive physically instead. The available moves are narrow and worth naming, because beginners run out of them and then start inventing absurdities.

- **Somebody arrives.** A walk-on brings a new relationship into a room that already has its own weather, and the existing pair now has to behave in front of a witness.
- **Somebody leaves.** Often stronger than an arrival. The two people left alone are different people than they were with an audience present.
- **News comes in.** A phone call, a knock, a thing seen through the window. This is the cheapest device and the easiest to overuse.
- **Time passes in the room.** The dinner arrives, the drink is finished, the wait ends. Real time is not literal time, and a monoscene can let twenty minutes pass in a sentence if both performers agree it has.

## What it trains that nothing else does

Patience, and a specific kind of attention. In a montage a performer can survive on strong initiations, because a fresh scene arrives every three minutes to reward them. In a monoscene the initiations are gone after the first minute, and everything afterwards is made from what is already in the room — the `relationship`, the `base-reality`, the objects handled through `space-work`, and whatever the other person did thirty seconds ago and does not know they did.

It is also the format where `rest-beat` stops being a technical term. A scene running twenty-five minutes has to breathe, and performers trained on shorter forms tend to fill every silence because silence has always meant the scene was dying. Here it usually means the opposite.

## Where it goes wrong

**Plot arrives to fill the gap.** The commonest failure. Nothing is happening, so somebody introduces a kidnapping. The energy lifts for ninety seconds and the scene is now unrecoverable, because a room with a kidnapping in it cannot go back to being two people doing the washing up.

**The location gets abandoned.** Somebody says "let's go to the car" and the form quietly becomes a two-person montage with extra steps.

**Everyone waits for the game.** In a form built on `game-of-the-scene` the pattern usually shows itself early. In a monoscene it can take ten minutes, and a cast that stops playing while it waits produces the stall it was afraid of.

## Specific sources

The UCB Comedy Improvisation Manual treats the monoscene as a form for advanced students precisely because it removes the supports beginners depend on. TJ & Dave's work is the best-known public example of the approach taken to its limit — one hour, one reality, no announced structure and no suggestion — though they are performing something closer to an organic two-person form than a taught monoscene. **Counter-position:** some teachers argue the monoscene is a diagnostic rather than a performance form, useful in a classroom for exposing what a pair cannot do and rarely the best choice in front of a paying audience. The honest version is that it asks more of an audience than most formats and repays them only when the cast is good enough to make the ordinary interesting.
