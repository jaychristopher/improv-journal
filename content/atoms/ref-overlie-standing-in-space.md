**Mary Overlie. *Standing in Space: The Six Viewpoints Theory & Practice.* Movement Publishing, 2016.**

Overlie's own account of the framework she built, published four decades after she began teaching it and eleven years after the book most people have read instead. That gap matters, because for most of that time the Six Viewpoints were known through other people's descriptions of them.

Her six are Space, Shape, Time, Emotion, Movement and Story, and the arrangement is the argument. Overlie's insistence is that the six are materials of equal standing, and that this is a refusal rather than an oversight: conventional theatre subordinates everything to story, and the Six Viewpoints deliberately demote story to one material among six. She came to that from postmodern dance and the Judson Dance Theater rather than from actor training, which is why the framework reads as a way of looking rather than as a set of exercises.

![The same six materials arranged twice: story raised above the other five, then story sitting among them on one line.](/images/where-story-sits.svg)

*The lower arrangement is a decision, not a simplification of the upper one.*


Read alongside [The Viewpoints Book](/library/ref-viewpoints-bogart-landau), the two make a genuine disagreement visible. Bogart and Landau expanded the physical elements into nine and organised them for rehearsal; Overlie regarded the expansion as a different project rather than an improvement on hers, and this book is where she says so in her own voice. Anyone using the nine-channel version is using an adaptation, and it is worth knowing what it was adapted from.

## Who it rewards

Anybody who met Viewpoints through Bogart and Landau and found the nine channels more prescriptive than the work seemed to need. Overlie's version is looser by design, and reading the original after the adaptation is a common route to understanding why the adaptation made the choices it did.

It also rewards dancers more than actors, which is not a criticism of either — it was built in postmodern dance and the book does not translate itself.

**The honest caveat.** This is a philosophical account rather than an instructional one, and it arrived four decades after she began teaching and eleven years after the book most people read instead. A teacher looking for a session to run will find far less here than in Bogart and Landau, and that is deliberate — systematising the six is precisely what Overlie declined to do.

The demotion of story is the other real cost. It is the argument's point, and a company working on narrative will find the framework unhelpful in exactly the way she intended.

**Key concepts originating here:**
- The Six Viewpoints — Space, Shape, Time, Emotion, Movement, Story — as materials of equal standing
- The deliberate demotion of story, and why that is the point rather than a side effect
- The postmodern dance lineage, and what transfers when the framework moves to actor training
- Attention as something trained by narrowing rather than by effort
