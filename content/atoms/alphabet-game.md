Two players run a scene in which every line starts with the next letter of the alphabet. The audience calls a letter, the scene begins there, and it ends when the sequence comes back round — twenty-six lines later, whether or not anybody has anything left to say.

## What it is actually for

It is a load test, and that is a different thing from a training exercise. The letter constraint occupies a chunk of `bandwidth` continuously, and what the game reveals is what a performer drops when capacity runs short.

That is genuinely useful information. Some people stop listening, and their lines stop connecting to anything their partner said. Some stop being anybody in particular and the characters evaporate. Some hold the scene and simply miss letters. Which one you lose first is a fair description of what your improv is actually built on, and most people are wrong about their own answer until they see it.

## Why it is a poor regular exercise

The constraint competes directly with the skill. `internal-computation` — searching your own head instead of attending to your partner — is the thing most improv training exists to reduce, and this game requires it: you cannot start a line with Q without going and looking for a Q.

So running it often trains the habit it exposes. A group that plays it weekly gets better at the alphabet rather than at scene work, and the smoothness they gain is the smoothness of people who have pre-loaded a list of words.

Play it to find something out, then go and work on whatever it found.

## Where it goes wrong

- **Announcing the letter.** "Q... quite a nice day, isn't it" tells the audience you are solving a puzzle. The letter should arrive inside a sentence somebody would actually say.
- **Stopping when somebody slips.** Missing a letter is not a failure condition. Carrying on as though nothing happened is closer to the point of the whole form than getting it right.
- **Starting on X.** An audience will suggest it and it makes a worse game, because three of the hardest letters land in the opening thirty seconds, before anybody has a scene to hold on to.
