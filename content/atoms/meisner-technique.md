An actor training method built on a single instruction — put your attention on the other person and respond to what they actually do — trained through a repetition exercise that makes self-monitoring mechanically impossible.

Sanford Meisner taught at the Neighborhood Playhouse in New York for close to five decades, and summarised the aim as **living truthfully under imaginary circumstances**. The phrase is quoted constantly and explains nothing on its own. What makes it a technique rather than an aspiration is the exercise underneath it.

## The Repetition Exercise

Two people face each other. One says a simple observation about the other — *you are wearing a blue shirt*. The second repeats it back. The first repeats it again. It continues.

Described on paper it sounds like the least promising exercise in the performing arts. What happens in practice is that within about ninety seconds the mechanical repetition becomes impossible to sustain, because the other person's delivery keeps changing — they get bored, amused, self-conscious, irritated — and the words start carrying those changes. The repetition stops being about the shirt and becomes an accurate running report of what is happening between two people.

That is the whole design. **The words are fixed so that everything else has to do the work.** You cannot plan an interesting line, because the line is already decided. You cannot prepare a delivery, because the delivery has to answer whatever they just did. The only remaining option is to watch them.

Later stages add independent activity — a difficult physical task to complete while the repetition runs — and then imaginary circumstances, before arriving at scene work. But the repetition is the foundation and most of the value.

## Why It Belongs on a Site About Improv

Meisner arrived at the same conclusion improv keeps rediscovering, from a different direction and several decades earlier.

`internal-computation` is the name used here for the specific failure: retreating into your own head to plan and evaluate while the scene carries on without you. It is what makes a performer look absent, and it is nearly universal in beginners because planning feels like effort and effort feels like working.

Willpower does not fix it. Being told to listen more does not fix it. What fixes it is a structure that leaves no capacity for planning, and repetition is one of the most ruthless of those ever built. `active-listening` gets trained here not as a virtue but as the only available move.

Almost every listening exercise in improv is a variant of the same idea. Last-word response, mirroring, one-word-at-a-time storytelling — each removes the option of composing ahead, which is the only reliable way to produce attention.

## What It Is Not

### It Is Not the Method

The most common confusion, and the two are close to opposites in where they point attention.

Lee Strasberg's approach — the one usually meant by *method acting* — draws on the actor's own remembered emotion to produce feeling. Meisner considered that the wrong direction of travel, because it puts attention inside the performer at precisely the moment it is needed outside. Both descend from Stanislavski, both came out of the Group Theatre, and they disagree about where an actor should be looking.

The practical difference: a Method exercise asks what you feel. A Meisner exercise asks what they just did.

### It Is Not About Emotion at All, Initially

People arrive expecting emotional work and find themselves saying *you are wearing a blue shirt* for four minutes. Feeling is treated as a by-product rather than a target — it turns up when two people are genuinely responding to each other, and chasing it directly produces the performed version.

### It Is Not `viewpoints`

Both are ensemble training and they attend to different channels. Viewpoints works on the physical and spatial — tempo, shape, architecture, the real room. Meisner works almost entirely on the other person's behaviour, and the body looks after itself.

## Trying the Repetition Exercise

It needs one other person and no equipment.

- **Start with something observable and neutral.** What they are wearing, that they are smiling, that they just looked away. Not an interpretation.
- **Repeat exactly, including who it is about.** *You are wearing a blue shirt* comes back as *I am wearing a blue shirt*. Getting the pronoun right keeps it pointed at a person rather than becoming a chant.
- **Let it change when it changes.** The moment their delivery shifts, your repetition shifts to match what you just saw. Nobody decides to change it — you follow.
- **Do not be interesting.** The exercise is spoiled instantly by somebody trying to make it entertaining, which is the same instinct that spoils scenes.
- **Three minutes is plenty.** Most pairs find the first minute excruciating and the third one absorbing.

The thing to notice afterwards is not whether it was any good. It is how much of your usual conversation is spent preparing rather than watching.

## Where It Came From

Meisner was part of the Group Theatre in 1930s New York alongside Strasberg and Stella Adler, the company through which Stanislavski's ideas reached America and immediately split into competing interpretations. He spent the rest of his working life at the Neighborhood Playhouse refining a training built on responsiveness rather than recall.

The account of his teaching is `ref-meisner-on-acting`, compiled from a year of his classes, and it is closer to a transcript than a manual — which is the right form for a technique that is mostly about what happens between two people in a room.
