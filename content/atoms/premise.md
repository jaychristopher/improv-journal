Premise is the situation a scene is in before anything unusual has happened to it. Two people in an elevator. A daughter visiting her father. Someone returning a toaster. A premise is static — it describes an arrangement rather than a behaviour, and on its own it is not yet a scene.

## Premise is not where the comedy is

The most common beginner error is treating the premise as the creative act. Given thirty seconds to think, people reach for something striking — a heist, an alien, a funeral for a goldfish — on the assumption that a more interesting setup produces a more interesting scene. It does the opposite, and reliably.

An elaborate premise arrives with obligations. It has to be explained, it has to be sustained, and it usually has to be resolved, so the performers spend the scene executing something they decided before it began rather than finding what is in front of them. A thin premise leaves room. Two people doing the washing up can go anywhere, because nothing has been promised.

`game-of-the-scene` covers the distinction properly: the premise is the setup, the game is the pattern that emerges inside it. What is worth adding is that the relationship is not symmetrical. A strong game rescues a dull premise every time. A strong premise rescues nothing.

## Most premises are assembled, not chosen

The word suggests a decision, and in practice a premise usually accumulates. Somebody makes an `initiation`, the other person adds a fact, and by the third line the situation exists without anybody having selected it. That is the normal case and the healthy one — `base-reality` describes the same territory from the other side, as the groundwork rather than the label.

Where a premise really is chosen in advance, it tends to come from a `suggestion` or from a form that requires one, and the choice is best made small. The test is whether you could describe it without using the word "and then".

## When the premise does carry weight

Not every form treats it as thin. Short-form games announce a premise and make the constraint the point — the audience is told the rule, and watching performers work inside it is the entertainment. Genre work is similar: `genre-format` establishes a world whose conventions are the material, and there the premise is doing real structural work rather than getting out of the way.

The distinction is whether the audience has been told the premise. When they have, it is a frame and it should be strong. When they have not, it is scaffolding and it should be light enough to forget.

## The counter-position

Sketch comedy is premise-driven, and writers moving into improv tend to over-value it for that reason — a sketch has to arrive already knowing what it is about, because there is no time to discover anything. Napier's warning about searching for the game applies here in reverse: a performer who has learned that premises are unimportant can end up refusing to establish anything at all, which produces scenes so unspecified that no game can form in them either.
