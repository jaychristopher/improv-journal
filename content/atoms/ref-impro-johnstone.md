**Keith Johnstone. *Impro: Improvisation and the Theatre.* Faber & Faber, 1979.**

The book modern improv is downstream of. More of the vocabulary in use today starts here than in any other single source, and it is the most-cited work on this site by a wide margin.

For the school it founded — Theatresports, Loose Moose, and the status work running underneath all of it — see [the Johnstone tradition](/traditions/johnstone). This page is about the book.

## What is actually in it

Four sections, plus an autobiographical opening and an appendix, and they are close to independent of each other.

- **Notes on Myself** is Johnstone's account of his own education and why he came to distrust it. It reads as memoir and is doing argumentative work: the claim underneath the whole book is that spontaneity is not a talent some people have but a capacity most people were taught out of.
- **Status** is the section that changed the most. It sets out status as something transacted through behaviour rather than assigned by rank, and it is the reason `status` is a technical term in improv at all.
- **Spontaneity** contains the material most often quoted second-hand — the cult of the obvious, the observation that an improviser trying to be original produces the same tired ideas as everybody else who is trying.
- **Narrative Skills** is the least-read and arguably the most useful, covering how stories are actually assembled — `reincorporation`, and the break in normality this site calls `tilt`.
- **Masks and Trance** is the section people skip. It is the strangest and the most revealing about what Johnstone thought he was doing, which was closer to lifting inhibition than to teaching comedy.

## How to read it

The sections do not build, so the order on the page is not an instruction. Somebody who wants the useful part immediately should start at Status and read that section alone; it stands entirely by itself and is the part that has changed how most people who have read the book behave in a rehearsal room.

Notes on Myself is the section most often skimmed as throat-clearing, and skimming it costs you the argument. Everything after it depends on the claim being made there — that the problem is not an absence of imagination but the presence of a censor — and readers who skip it tend to take the later exercises as techniques for being more creative rather than as ways of getting out of your own way.

Masks and Trance is the one to read last and not to dismiss. It is where Johnstone says most plainly what he thinks is happening when the work goes well, and while the framing is the hardest to accept, the book is less coherent without it.

## What has aged, and what has not

The observations have held up remarkably well; the explanations have not always.

The status material is the strongest case for the first. It is descriptive — here is a thing people do, here is how to see it — and sixty years of subsequent social psychology has not embarrassed it. The same goes for the account of blocking, which names a mechanism anybody who has watched a bad scene will recognise immediately.

The trance and mask material is the strongest case for the second. Johnstone asserts a good deal about what is happening psychologically when a performer wears a mask, and the assertions are offered with more confidence than evidence. They can be read as a practitioner's description of an effect he had reliably produced rather than as a theory of it, and read that way they are still valuable — but the book does not invite that qualification, and a reader should supply it.

*Both bars are the same book, written in the same voice, at the same confidence.*


Two other things date it. The examples come from British drama schools of the 1960s and 70s, and the tone about students is occasionally severe in a way a contemporary teacher would not adopt. Neither undermines the content, but both are audible.

## What it does not contain

No long form, no Harold, and almost nothing about ensembles larger than a scene. The American traditions that most readers arrive from are not in this book, because they had not happened yet or were happening independently and elsewhere.

Theatresports is barely here either, despite being the format Johnstone is best known for outside improv circles. He invented it around the same period, but the book that documents it properly is the sequel. Anybody who came to *Impro* wanting the format will find the philosophy underneath it and not the format.

## Who it rewards, and who it does not

Almost anybody, and it does not read like a manual — it reads like a very opinionated person thinking aloud, which is why it has stayed in print for over forty years.

The catch is that it is not organised for retrieval. Exercises sit inside arguments, the sections do not build on each other, and there is no route through it for somebody who wants a curriculum. Teachers tend to use it alongside Spolin rather than instead of her: she supplies the structure, he supplies the reasons.

## Key contributions to the knowledge graph

`offers` as the fundamental unit, so that everything said or done on stage is something a partner can build with; `blocking` as the primary failure mode and the reason refusal is a structural problem rather than a rudeness; `status` as behaviour; `spontaneity` as recovered rather than acquired; and the line that most of this site's argument rests on — *"Improvisers usually try not to be altered. We need to be altered."*

## Editions, and the sequel

Faber published it in 1979 and it has never been substantially revised. The Routledge/Theatre Arts printing sold in the US is the same text, so edition is not a decision anybody needs to make.

*Impro for Storytellers* (1999) is the sequel and a genuinely different book: practical where this one is discursive, organised around Theatresports and around running a company, and considerably longer. The common mistake is treating it as a second edition. Read *Impro* for the argument and *Impro for Storytellers* for the practice, and if you only ever read one, the answer depends on whether you need to be convinced or need to run a session on Thursday.

## Where he was working

Johnstone developed most of this at the Royal Court Theatre Studio in London, where he was an associate director — a new-writing theatre rather than a comedy venue. The exercises were built to get actors and writers unstuck, and the fact that they turned into a comedy form happened later and somewhere else.

That somewhere else was Calgary. He moved to Canada in the 1970s, taught at the university, and founded Loose Moose Theatre Company, which is where Theatresports was built into a going concern. The book sits at the join: written in Britain out of studio work with actors, published just as the Canadian company that would carry the ideas outward was getting started.
