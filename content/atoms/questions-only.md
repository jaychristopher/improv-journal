**Trains:** noticing how much of a conversation is deferral. Two players hold a scene in which every line must be a question — and the exercise is most useful for what it reveals about the habit, not for the habit itself.

## Setup

Two players begin a scene. Every line must be a genuine question. A player is out if they make a statement, repeat a question, hesitate too long, or ask something that does not follow. The next player steps in and the scene continues.

Best played as a line — everyone queued up, replacements stepping in continuously — so the scene never stops.

## The catch worth knowing about

Most improv teaching treats questions as a problem, and it is right to. `initiation` puts it plainly: the test of a line is whether it asserts a reality or asks your partner to create one. "So... who are you to me?" hands the creative burden across the stage. A vague question is hesitation in costume.

So a game made entirely of questions is drilling the exact move the rest of the training tries to remove. That is not a reason to skip it — it is the reason it works. Players discover in about forty seconds how exhausting it is to receive nothing, and they feel it from the inside rather than being told.

## The distinction the game teaches

Not all questions defer. Some of them assert.

- **"Why did you come back after all these years?"** establishes a history, an absence and a grievance. It is an offer with a question mark on it.
- **"Do you want to, like, do something?"** establishes nothing and asks the other player to do the work.

Played well, Questions Only becomes a competition to see who can load the most reality into an interrogative. Played badly it is two people saying "what?" at each other. The gap between those two versions is the entire lesson, and it transfers directly to scenes where questions are allowed.

## Side-coaching

"Put a fact in the question." "You already know who they are — ask like it." "Don't ask what, ask why." "That was a statement with a question mark."

## Common failures

**Stalling questions.** "Really?" "Are you sure?" "What do you mean?" Technically legal, entirely empty. Rule them out after the first round.

**Ignoring the scene.** Players get so occupied with the constraint that they stop building a `base-reality`, and the result is a rally rather than a scene. If an observer cannot say who these two people are after thirty seconds, the constraint has eaten the content.

**Playing for elimination.** The line format tempts players to bait each other into statements. It gets laughs and teaches nothing.

## Lineage

A short-form staple, most familiar to audiences from televised improv, where it is played as an elimination game for speed and laughs. The training version is slower and cares about the scene. Johnstone's work on offers and blocking is the useful frame for what it is actually exposing: a question that gives nothing is a soft `blocking` move, and it is the most socially acceptable one there is.
