**Trains:** giving up authorship. A circle tells one story, one word per person, and nobody is allowed to know where it is going.

## Setup

Stand in a circle. Going round, each player adds exactly one word to a single continuing story. Punctuation is spoken ("period", "comma") or simply implied by a pause. No conferring, no skipping, no two words.

Run it for two or three minutes, or until the story reaches an ending — which, if the group is listening, it will find on its own.

## Not the same as One-Word Scene

`one-word-scene` is two players performing a scene in which each line is a single word. This is a whole circle narrating. The first is about compression under constraint; this one is about surrendering the plot. Worth keeping straight, because groups routinely ask for one and get the other.

## Why it works

One word is not enough to steer with. You can nudge the grammar, but you cannot impose a direction, which makes this the cleanest available demonstration that a story can be built by people who each control almost none of it.

It also exposes `steering` faster than any scene work does. A player who wants the story to be about a dragon will keep trying to bend the sentence back toward dragons, and everyone can hear it — the sentence starts fighting its own grammar. In a scene the same move is invisible and just feels like the scene is going badly.

## Side-coaching

"Take the word that finishes the sentence you were handed." "Don't be funny, be grammatical." "You are allowed to say 'the'." "Say it before you decide it."

## What to notice

The stories that work are almost always the ones where nobody tried. `obvious-choice` is the whole discipline: your first word is connected to the sentence, your second thought is connected to your idea of a good story. Groups discover this by hearing the difference between two runs — one where everyone reaches, one where everyone accepts — and the accepting one is invariably better and funnier.

## Common failures

**Every player ending the sentence.** A circle where each person adds a full stop produces eleven short declarative sentences and no story. Coach players to leave the sentence open.

**Comedy tax.** Someone inserts a word chosen purely to get a laugh — "banana", "suddenly", a name from the room. It gets the laugh and costs the story its thread, and the next four players spend their turns cleaning up.

**Refusing to say small words.** Players feel they have wasted their turn on "of" or "and". Those turns are the ones holding the sentence together, and a group that understands this is halfway to understanding support.

## Variations

- **One sentence each** instead of one word — easier, and closer to how a `story-spine` round runs.
- **Two words each.** Sounds trivial and changes the game completely: two words is just enough to steer with, and watching the group start steering is instructive.

- **With a stem.** Begin from "Once upon a time" and see whether the circle finds the shape on its own.
