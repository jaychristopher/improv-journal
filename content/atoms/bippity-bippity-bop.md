Somebody stands in the middle, points at a player and says "bippity bippity bop." The player has to get "bop" out before the caller finishes the phrase. If the caller says only "bop", the player must say nothing at all. Get it wrong either way and you swap places with the caller.

Most groups add a second layer once the first is running — a named group shape the caller can ask for instead, built by the target and the two people either side of them within a count of five. The specific shapes vary by school and are almost entirely arbitrary; what matters is that there are several and the caller chooses without warning.

## What it trains

Response latency and nothing else, which is the reason it works. There is no content to invent, no idea to have and no way to be clever — the only variable is how long it takes you to react to something you did not choose.

That makes it one of the few exercises where `hesitation` is isolated completely. Elsewhere a slow response can be explained away as thinking about the scene. Here there is no scene, so the delay is visible as exactly what it is, and it is the reason a group that has played this for five minutes starts scenes noticeably faster.

The silent version is the better half. Not saying "bop" is harder than saying it, because the reflex to answer is what has just been trained, and holding it requires listening to the whole instruction rather than to the first syllable.

## Running it well

- **Speed over accuracy of the shapes.** If the room is discussing whether that was a correct elephant, it has become a rules argument and stopped being a warm-up.
- **Keep the caller moving.** A caller who circles slowly choosing a victim gives everybody time to prepare, which removes the entire point.
- **Do not run it long.** Four or five minutes. It is high-energy and repetitive, and a group that plays it for fifteen is tired rather than warm.

## Side-coaching it

Four lines, all called over the top of the game rather than by pausing it, which would hand the room the deliberation this is removing.

*Faster.* Aimed at the caller more than the circle. A caller who slows down is being merciful and is destroying the exercise.

*Don't set up.* For the players visibly rehearsing "bop" under their breath while the caller circles. Preparing is the failure, not the delay.

*It doesn't matter.* For the first rules argument about whether a shape was correct, said before the argument gets going rather than after.

*Swap.* Keep the changeovers fast and unceremonious. A group that pauses to acknowledge each mistake is making a small event of it, which is the opposite of what the game is for.

## The honest objection

It is a reaction game, and reaction is a small part of improvising. Nobody has ever had a better scene because they can say "bop" quickly.

What justifies it is what it does to a room rather than to a skill: everybody fails constantly, publicly and without consequence, inside the first minute. That is the fastest available way to make failure boring, which is a precondition for everything else. Treat it as a device for changing the temperature of a group and it is excellent. Treat it as training and it is a game about a nonsense word.

## When to run it, and when not

If this is a device for changing the temperature of a room rather than training — which the section above argues and this page agrees with — then when to deploy it is the whole question, and the answer is narrower than its popularity suggests.

Run it on a cold room, on a group who do not know each other, or on a group that has gone careful — after a note that landed harder than intended, or midway through a session where everybody has started thinking. Five minutes of public, consequence-free failure resets that faster than anything you can say.

Do not run it on a group that is already loose. It costs five minutes and returns nothing, and a warm-up used out of habit teaches a room that warm-ups are a ritual rather than a tool.

Do not run it immediately before a show. It leaves people activated, and the state a set wants at the top is settled and attentive — `be-present` rather than energised. Something quieter belongs in that slot.

And do not run it with a group whose difficulty is being singled out. The caller mechanic points at one person at a time in front of everybody, which is precisely the shape of the fear you would be trying to dissolve. A whole-group game where everybody moves at once does the same job without the spotlight.
