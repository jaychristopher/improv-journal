**Keith Johnstone. *Impro for Storytellers: Theatresports and the Art of Making Things Happen.* Faber & Faber, 1999.**

Johnstone's second book, written twenty years after *Impro*, and the inverse of it in almost every respect. Where the first is an argument that happens to contain exercises, this is a manual that happens to contain an argument.

For the school it belongs to, see [the Johnstone tradition](/traditions/johnstone). For the earlier and more famous book, see [Impro](/library/ref-impro-johnstone). This page is about the sequel.

## What is actually in it

Sixteen chapters, and the shape of the list tells you what it is for: Theatresports, Audience Suggestions, Trouble with Feedback, Spontaneity, Making Things Happen, Story Games, Being There, Some Filler Games, Procedures, Serious Scenes, Character, Technical Stuff. That is a company manual. It covers how to run the format, how to handle an audience, how to judge, what to do when a show is failing, and what to say to a group afterwards.

It grew out of an earlier and much smaller publication called *Don't Be Prepared: Theatresports for Teachers*, which is the register it keeps — Johnstone talking to somebody who has to run a session on Thursday.

## Who it rewards, and who it does not

Anybody running a group or a show, and it is close to indispensable if that show is `theatresports`. There is no better account anywhere of the practical problems that format creates: scoring, judging, an audience with power, and what all of that does to the performers.

A general reader is better served by *Impro*, and it is fair to say so plainly. The theory is thin here by design, the games are indexed by function rather than by argument, and read cover to cover it is repetitive in the way a reference work is. People who loved the first book and bounce off this one have not changed their minds about Johnstone; they have picked up a different kind of object.

**Key contributions to the knowledge graph:** the full `blocking-taxonomy` — wimping, cancelling, bridging, hedging, pimping, looping, joining — which is the most detailed diagnostic of scene failure in the literature and does not appear in *Impro* at all; `wimping` named as its own move rather than as weak blocking; overaccepting, which is answering a small offer with enormous weight; and `reincorporation` pushed to the front of narrative craft, with the improviser described as somebody walking backwards, shaping a story by remembering what has been left lying about.

**On the two books together.** Read *Impro* for why and this for how, and expect the second to be less quotable and more useful once you are actually teaching. The twenty-year gap also shows: this is Johnstone after two decades of watching his own ideas taught badly, and a good deal of it is him correcting the misreadings — which is the most interesting thing in it and the reason it is worth reading even if you never run a Theatresports match.
