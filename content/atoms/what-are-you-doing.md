**Trains:** committing to a physical action while your mouth is doing something else. The standard first exposure to space work, and the reason most improvisers can mime at all.

## Setup

Two players, or a line taking turns in pairs. Player A begins miming an activity — say, brushing their teeth. Player B asks, "What are you doing?" Player A answers with an activity that is **not** the one they are doing: "I'm mowing the lawn."

Player B immediately starts mowing the lawn. Player A asks, "What are you doing?" B names something else. And on it goes, without pause.

## Why it works

The split is the whole exercise. Your hands are doing one thing and your mouth is naming another, which means you cannot rehearse an answer while also staying in the activity — attempting both is how players discover their capacity has a ceiling.

What survives that split is the obvious answer. `obvious-choice` says the first thing that comes to you is connected to the moment and the second is connected to your ego, and here the game enforces it: hesitate to find something clever and you have already broken the rhythm.

It is also the cheapest introduction to `space-work` there is. Nobody is asked to mime well. They are asked to mime *immediately*, which turns out to be the part that matters — an unconvincing action performed without hesitation reads far better than a detailed one performed late.

## Side-coaching

"Start the new activity before you finish the sentence." "Show me where the object is." "Don't tell me it's a lawnmower, use it like one." "Say the first thing." "Where are your feet?"

## What to notice

Players almost always shrink their space work to a small area in front of their chest — a sort of generic hand-waving that could be any activity. Coaching one adjustment, *put the object somewhere specific*, changes the quality immediately, because an object with a location can be picked up, put down and returned to. That is the difference between miming and building an `environment`.

Watch also for the point where a player stops naming activities and starts naming jokes. It is audible in the pause before the answer.

## Common failures

**Answering with a state rather than an activity.** "I'm being sad" gives the next player nothing to do with their hands. The answer has to be a verb somebody can perform.

**Freezing to think.** The activity stops while the player searches. Coach them to keep the current activity going while they speak — that split is the skill.

**Escalating to absurdity.** "I'm wrestling a bear made of soup." It gets a laugh and the next player cannot do it, which is a `cognitive-bandwidth` problem dressed as a joke: complex offers cost your partner more than they give.

## Variations

- **Three players in a rotation**, so you are answering one person while the next is already asking.
- **Same first letter.** Every named activity must begin with the same letter. Adds load and shows how quickly the obvious answers dry up when a filter is applied.
- **No talking.** Player B guesses the activity aloud and A must then switch to whatever B guessed. Turns it into a pure space-work exercise and exposes exactly how legible each player's mime is.
