A fixed sequence of eight sentence stems that produces a complete story with a beginning, a turn, consequences and an ending. Kenn Adams devised it for teaching long-form improvisation, and it has since escaped improv entirely — it is taught in classrooms, pitch training, and, in a near-identical form, at Pixar.

## The eight stems

1. **Once upon a time...** — the ordinary world
2. **Every day...** — what is routine about it
3. **But one day...** — the disruption
4. **Because of that...** — the first consequence
5. **Because of that...** — the second
6. **Because of that...** — the third
7. **Until finally...** — the climax the consequences were leading to
8. **And ever since that day...** — the new ordinary

Steps one and two are the world as it was. Step three breaks it. Four to six are the chain that follows. Seven resolves it and eight states what changed.

## Why the shape works

The first two stems are doing the job `base-reality` does in a scene: establishing a normal that the unusual thing can be unusual against. Skip them and "but one day" has nothing to interrupt.

The three "because of that" steps are the part people cut, and they are the part that makes it a story rather than a list. Each one has to be caused by the last. "Because of that" is a load-bearing phrase — swap it for "and then" and the structure collapses into a sequence of events, which is exactly the failure the spine exists to prevent.

"Until finally" only lands if the consequences have been escalating, which makes the spine a `heightening` exercise wearing a narrative costume. And "ever since that day" forces a `reincorporation`: the ending has to speak to the beginning, or the audience notices the story did not close.

## Running it as an exercise

In a circle, one stem per person, going round. No planning, no conferring, and no rewriting what came before. The discipline is `obvious-choice` — take the first thing the previous sentence implies rather than the cleverest thing you can attach to it.

Two rules make it work: nobody may skip a stem, and nobody may add a second sentence. The constraint is the teacher. A group given eight stems and no extra words will produce a coherent story on the first attempt roughly every time, which is a surprising and useful thing for a new room to discover about itself.

## The Pixar version

The template is widely searched as the "Pixar story spine" because a version of it circulated in 2011 as one of Emma Coats's "story rules", collected while she was a story artist at the studio. That version — "Once upon a time there was... Every day... One day... Because of that... Because of that... Until finally..." — is Adams's spine with the closing stem dropped.

Worth knowing which way the borrowing runs: the spine is Adams's, from improv pedagogy, and it reached a wider audience through the Pixar association rather than originating there.

## How it fails

**Used as a script.** The spine is a scaffold for finding a story, not a form to fill in. Improvisers who plan their "until finally" in advance stop listening for the one the middle actually earned.

**Applied to a scene.** It is a story structure, not a scene structure. A two-person scene does not need eight beats and will die if you march it through them. For what a scene needs instead, see `scene-structure`.

**Too many "because of that" steps.** Three is the number the shape wants. Extend the chain and the climax arrives too late to feel caused by anything in particular.

## Lineage

Kenn Adams developed the spine while teaching narrative improvisation and set it out in *How to Improvise a Full-Length Play*. Johnstone's *Impro for Storytellers* attacks the same problem from a different angle — his interest is in what stops improvisers telling stories at all, where Adams supplies a structure that makes it hard not to.
