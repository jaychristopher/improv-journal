The audience writes lines on slips before the show and they are scattered face-down across the stage. Mid-scene a player picks one up, reads whatever is on it aloud, and then has to make it belong.

## What it trains

`justification` with no preparation possible, which is the purest version of the skill available. Every other exercise lets you construct a reason for something you at least said yourself. Here the line arrives from a stranger, has nothing to do with the scene, and is often deliberately unhelpful — and the job is to behave as though it were always going to be said.

The move that works is almost never explaining the line. It is treating it as evidence about the character or the situation and letting the consequence do the work: somebody who says *"the badgers know what I did"* is not somebody who needs to clarify, it is somebody with a history the audience now wants.

![Two responses to the same slip drawn to length: a long explaining bar and a short reacting one.](/images/after-the-slip-is-read.svg)

*Length is the tell, and the audience reads it before they read the content.*


## Where it goes wrong

- **Reading it as a joke.** A slip delivered with a knowing look tells the audience the scene has paused. Delivered flatly, as something this person would say, it is far stranger and much funnier.
- **Explaining rather than absorbing.** Three sentences of rescue after the line is the standard failure. One reaction is enough.
- **Picking up slips to escape.** A player who is stuck reaches for paper. The scene then consists of random lines and no relationship, which is the version audiences find exhausting.

## The honest objection

The form works against listening, and it is worth naming because the game is often sold as a listening exercise.

A player standing near a slip is partly attending to the paper, and knows they are about to hand their partner something arbitrary. That is a divided attention the rest of improv spends its time eliminating — `active-listening` is precisely what the mechanic interrupts. Groups that run it often find their scenes get more episodic afterwards.

It earns its place as a show game, where the audience seeing their own handwriting come back at them is worth a great deal, and as a targeted drill for somebody who explains too much. It is a poor general workout.
