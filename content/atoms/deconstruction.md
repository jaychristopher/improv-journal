A long form built by [Del Close](/del-close) with The Family at ImprovOlympic, in which one unresolved scene becomes the material for everything that follows. The rest of the piece is an interpretation of that scene rather than a continuation of it.

## The shape

### The opening scene

The opening scene runs long — six to eight minutes, two people — and its job is to generate information rather than to be satisfying. A relationship is established and a problem is raised and deliberately not solved. It ends unresolved, which is the point and which performers trained on other forms find difficult to do on purpose.

### The thematic scenes

The thematic scenes take something a character in that opening wanted, feared or avoided, and play scenes about it. Not scenes continuing the story — scenes about what the story was made of. If the opening was two brothers failing to discuss their mother, the thematic scenes are about obligation, or about the things families agree not to say, and they may contain no brothers and no mother.

### The run

The run closes the piece: a series of ever-shorter beats, returning to images and lines from everything before. Pace matters at least as much as content here, and a run that slows down to be clever stops being a run.

## What makes it different

The Harold asks what else is like this. The Deconstruction asks what that was about. One is associative and the other is analytical, and the difference shows up immediately in how a cast listens — Harold performers listen for patterns they can repeat, and this form needs them listening for what the scene is unable to say.

It is also the only major long form whose opening is a scene rather than a game, a monologue or a pattern. That means the whole piece rests on one pair getting six minutes right, which is why it tends to be a form for established ensembles rather than a class exercise.

## Where it goes wrong

- **The opening gets resolved.** Somebody solves the problem out of kindness, and the piece has nothing left to be about. The discipline is to let a scene end while it is still unfinished.

- **The thematic scenes become sequels.** The commonest failure, and it is a failure of interpretation rather than of craft: the cast continues the story instead of examining it, and what should have been a deconstruction becomes a slow narrative with the same two characters in different rooms.

- **The run arrives too early.** Without enough thematic material the run has nothing to reincorporate and reads as the cast running out of time rather than converging.

## Specific sources

Three Mad Rituals was a show Del Close directed at ImprovOlympic in the early 1990s with The Family, the house team, comprising the Harold, the Deconstruction and the Movie — often from a single line of poetry taken as the suggestion for all three. **Attribution note:** the form is documented mainly through the teaching lineage and theatre practice rather than in a published text, so the section breakdown above reflects how it is commonly taught rather than a canonical description Close himself wrote down.
