The moment the established normality breaks. Johnstone's term for the event that turns a situation into a story — the balance of ordinary life is tilted, and everything after it is consequence.

## Why a scene needs one

A platform with nothing tilting it is a pleasant scene about nothing. Two people establish that they are brothers, that it is Sunday, that the roast is in the oven, and the audience waits — agreeably at first — for the thing that is going to happen. If it never comes, the scene was competent and the audience will not remember it an hour later.

The tilt is small far more often than beginners expect. "Mum is coming" is a tilt. So is one brother admitting he has already eaten. What makes it a tilt is not scale but irreversibility: after it, the room cannot go back to what it was doing.

## Tilting too early is the common error

If the platform has not been built, there is nothing to break. A scene that opens with the tilt — a gun, an affair, a diagnosis in the first twenty seconds — leaves the performers with a large event and no relationship to play it through, which is why those scenes escalate so fast and end so badly. The audience has no idea what normal looked like, so they cannot feel it being lost.

The reverse error is rarer and cheaper to fix: a cast so committed to `base-reality` that nobody ever disturbs it. Johnstone's blunt version was that improvisers are afraid to let anything happen, because everything that happens creates obligations.

## Tilt and game are not the same move

This is the clearest point where the two major traditions diverge, and conflating them causes real confusion in mixed rooms.

Johnstone's tilt asks *what happens next*. The break creates consequences, the consequences create more, and the shape of the piece is the chain — which is why the narrative tradition talks about reincorporation and endings.

The Chicago question is *what else is like this*. The first unusual thing is not a hinge but a pattern to be found and repeated at increasing pressure, and `game-of-the-scene` work treats the ending as almost incidental. Same noticing, opposite follow-through: one asks the event to cause something, the other asks it to recur.

Neither is more advanced. They produce different shows, and a performer who has trained one will feel that the other is doing it wrong.

## Specific sources

Johnstone, *Impro for Storytellers* — the tilt lists and the platform-tilt sequence in the narrative chapters. Johnstone, *Impro*, Ch. 4 on narrative skills, where the underlying argument is that being interrupted by consequence is what students spend most of their effort avoiding. **Counter-position:** teachers in the game tradition often argue the tilt is a story-writing idea imported into a form that does not need it, and that scenes should be built from behaviour rather than from events. The counter-counter is that a show made entirely of patterns with no consequences is exhausting to watch for an hour, which is why most long forms borrow from both whatever their teachers claim.
