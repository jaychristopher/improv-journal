**Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi. *Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience.* Harper & Row, 1990.**

The book that named the state, built from several thousand experience samples across surgeons, climbers, factory workers and chess players rather than from introspection. What they reported in common is the interesting part: absorption in a task, a collapse of the sense of time, and the disappearance of self-consciousness.

That last one is the reason this is cited here, and it is more precise than the popular version. Flow does not eliminate consciousness; it eliminates the part of consciousness that monitors the self. The performer keeps perceiving, choosing and responding, and stops running a parallel process assessing how it is going — which is exactly the distinction between a performer who is present and one who has left to grade themselves.

The conditions Csikszentmihalyi identifies are also a fair description of a well-run scene: clear goals, immediate feedback, and a challenge matched to skill. That last condition is why the state is unreliable for beginners and why it arrives more often as competence grows, and it is a better explanation than talent for why experienced improvisers look calmer.

**Key concepts drawn from here:**
- The loss of self-consciousness as the disappearance of self-monitoring, not of awareness
- Challenge matched to skill as a precondition, which makes the state trainable rather than lucky
- Immediate feedback as structural — improvisation supplies it whether or not anyone wants it
- Autotelic experience: the activity being worth doing without reference to its result
