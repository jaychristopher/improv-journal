Somebody says "let's paint the fence." Everybody answers "Yes, let's!" and starts painting the fence. Somebody else says "let's climb the fence," and the room climbs it. It takes four minutes and it is the most-taught first exercise in improv.

## What it trains

Answering out loud, in unison, before you have evaluated the proposal. The verbal response is a formality; what the exercise is actually installing is the physical habit of beginning something on somebody else's word rather than your own.

It also removes the cost of proposing. In a scene, an offer that nobody takes is a small public failure, and beginners learn very quickly to stop making them. Here every proposal is accepted by an entire room within half a second, which is the most emphatic possible demonstration that offering is safe. A group that has done this is measurably more willing to start things ten minutes later.

## Where it goes wrong

- **Waiting for a good suggestion.** The room goes quiet because everybody is auditioning proposals in their head. The correct proposal is the first one, and "let's stand up" is a perfectly good offer.
- **The same three people proposing.** Ask for suggestions from somebody who has not made one yet. This is more useful than it sounds — the quiet members are the ones the exercise exists for.
- **Miming nothing.** "Yes, let's!" followed by vague hand-waving is the whole failure in miniature. Whatever was proposed, do it specifically enough that a stranger could name it.

## Side-coaching it

Called while the room is moving, never by stopping it — a halt here reintroduces exactly the deliberation the exercise removes.

*Take the first one.* The single most useful line, aimed at the silence where everybody is auditioning proposals.

*Specifically.* For the vague hand-waving. It is faster than explaining why specificity matters and it works immediately.

*Somebody who has not gone yet.* Say it to the room rather than to a person, so nobody is singled out and the quiet members get an opening they did not have to claim.

*Keep going.* Rounds die from politeness — a pause after each activity while everybody waits to see whether it is finished. It is finished when somebody proposes the next thing.

## The honest objection

It teaches agreement and calls it `accepting-the-offer`, and those are not the same thing.

Nothing in this exercise requires anybody to add. You say yes, you copy, you wait for the next proposal — which is structurally `wimping`, the politest way there is to stop a scene, being drilled in as a positive habit. Groups that run it a great deal produce students who are enthusiastic and contribute nothing, and are puzzled about why their scenes go nowhere when they did everything they were told.

It is a warm-up for the acceptance half only. The addition half has to be trained by something else, and it is worth saying so in the room rather than letting a group assume they have just practised "yes, and".

## The variant that answers the objection

The section above says the addition half has to be trained by something else, and leaves it there. It does not need a different exercise, only a small change to this one.

Require the addition. Each person answers "yes, let's" and adds a single detail as they begin — the fence is wet, the fence belongs to somebody who is watching, the paint is the wrong colour. Acceptance now carries an obligation to contribute, which is the thing the plain version quietly omits.

Or make the proposal build. The next suggestion has to extend what the room is already doing rather than replace it: painting the fence becomes painting the gate to match, not climbing it. That trains extension against substitution, which is the failure `wimping` names and the one this exercise otherwise rehearses.

Both cost something, and it is worth knowing what. They slow the round and reintroduce a little evaluation, which is the pressure the plain version exists to remove. So run it plain first, until proposing is visibly cheap, and add the requirement once nobody is hesitating — not before, or the exercise loses the one thing it does better than anything else.
