**Viola Spolin. *Improvisation for the Theater: A Handbook of Teaching and Directing Techniques.* Northwestern University Press, 1963; third edition 1999.**

The foundational text of improvisational theatre pedagogy, and the origin of the phrase "theatre games". Nearly everything taught in an improv class anywhere descends from this book, usually through several intermediaries who did not cite it.

For the tradition that grew out of it, and where it parts company with the Chicago schools that inherited it, see [the Spolin tradition](/traditions/spolin). For the games themselves as they are played now, see [theatre games](/theatre-games). This page is about the book.

## What is actually in it

The subtitle is the honest description: it is a handbook, not an argument. Around two hundred numbered games occupy most of its length, and each is set out in the same format — the game, its **Point of Concentration** (the single thing players are to attend to), the side-coaching lines a teacher calls during it, and the evaluation questions to ask afterwards.

That format is the book's real contribution, more than any individual game. It encodes a claim about how the skill is acquired: you do not explain improvisation to somebody, you give them a problem narrow enough that solving it requires the behaviour you wanted, and then you say almost nothing while they solve it.

The opening theory chapters carry the rest — spontaneity, the Approval/Disapproval Syndrome, transformation of the object — and they are much shorter than their influence suggests.

## Who it rewards, and who it does not

Teachers and directors get an entire curriculum out of it. Somebody planning a class can open it, take a Point of Concentration, and have a session that works, which is why it has stayed in print for sixty years and sold well over a hundred thousand copies in its first two editions alone.

Performers find it harder going, and it is fair to say so. It is written to be worked from rather than read, the prose is of its period and can be forbidding, and the games are indexed by the problem they solve rather than by anything a reader would think to look up. People who bounce off it have not missed the point; they have used a reference book as a narrative one.

**Key contributions to the knowledge graph:** the whole idea that constraint produces `presence` rather than instruction doing it; `side-coaching` as real-time direction that keeps a player connected instead of stopping the work to correct it; `space-work` and `environment` as the physical route into attention; and evaluation as something structurally retrospective, done by the group about the problem rather than about the person.

**On editions.** The third edition of 1999 is the one to buy — revised, and coedited by Paul Sills, Spolin's son and the founding director of Second City, which makes the lineage from this book to Chicago improv a literal family one. Earlier printings are the same games with less apparatus.

**The lineage worth knowing.** Spolin did not come from the theatre. She learned this method in the 1920s from Neva Boyd at Hull House in Chicago, where games, folk dance and storytelling were being used as social work — tools for getting immigrant children to participate. The techniques were built to draw out people who were not performers and had no intention of becoming any, which is why they still work on a corporate away-day, and why the book reads as though it were written for teachers. It was.
