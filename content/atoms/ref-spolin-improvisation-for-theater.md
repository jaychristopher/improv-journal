**Viola Spolin. *Improvisation for the Theater: A Handbook of Teaching and Directing Techniques.* Northwestern University Press, 1963; third edition 1999.**

The foundational text of improvisational theatre pedagogy, and the origin of the phrase ["theatre games"](/theatre-games). Nearly everything taught in an improv class anywhere descends from this book, usually through several intermediaries who did not cite it.

For the tradition that grew out of it, and where it parts company with the Chicago schools that inherited it, see [the Spolin tradition](/traditions/spolin). For her life and the argument about what she invented, see [Viola Spolin](/viola-spolin). This page is about the book.

## What is actually in it

The subtitle is the honest description: it is a handbook, not an argument. Around two hundred numbered games occupy most of its length, and each is set out in the same format — the game, its **Point of Concentration** (the single thing players are to attend to), the side-coaching lines a teacher calls during it, and the evaluation questions to ask afterwards.

That format is the book's real contribution, more than any individual game. It encodes a claim about how the skill is acquired: you do not explain improvisation to somebody, you give them a problem narrow enough that solving it requires the behaviour you wanted, and then you say almost nothing while they solve it.

The opening theory chapters carry the rest — spontaneity, the Approval/Disapproval Syndrome, transformation of the object — and they are much shorter than their influence suggests.

## How the games are grouped

Not by difficulty, and not by how a class would run. The games are sorted by the problem they solve, which is why the book is hard to navigate and why it stays useful for sixty years: a chapter is a diagnosis.

The chapters that get used most are the ones on Where, Who and What — the three questions Spolin treats as the substance of any scene, and the direct ancestor of the checklist improvisers now know as `base-reality` or CROW. The Where chapter is much the longest of the three, and that asymmetry is deliberate: she thought place was the concrete thing an actor could physically engage, and that relationship and activity would follow from it rather than the other way round.

Around those sit chapters on sensory awareness, on being part of a whole, on emotion, on speech, on character and on the development of story — plus separate later material on rehearsal and on working with children. A teacher with a specific problem in the room can go to the relevant chapter and find eight games aimed at it.

## How to use it without reading it

Nobody who gets value from this book reads it front to back, and the people who try are the ones who report that it is impenetrable.

Work backwards from a problem instead. A group that is talking rather than doing needs the Where games; a group that is performing at each other needs the ones on part of a whole; a group that is going blank needs the sensory work. Take one game, run it, and use the evaluation questions printed with it rather than inventing your own — those questions are load-bearing, and they are the part teachers most often skip.

The glossary is also worth reading on its own. Spolin's vocabulary is precise and is not what the words mean in ordinary use, and half the misreadings of her method come from taking terms like *transformation* or *the point of concentration* in their everyday senses.

## Who it rewards, and who it does not

Teachers and directors get an entire curriculum out of it. Somebody planning a class can open it, take a Point of Concentration, and have a session that works, which is why it has stayed in print for sixty years and sold well over a hundred thousand copies in its first two editions alone.

Performers find it harder going, and it is fair to say so. It is written to be worked from rather than read, the prose is of its period and can be forbidding, and the games are indexed by the problem they solve rather than by anything a reader would think to look up. People who bounce off it have not missed the point; they have used a reference book as a narrative one.

## What it does not contain

Anything about improvisation as a performance form. There is no long form in it, no account of a set of scenes building into a show, nothing on how an audience is handled, because none of that existed in a settled shape in 1963 and Spolin was not trying to invent it.

The games are training for theatre. The thing they trained people to do — go out and improvise in front of a paying room, as the show rather than the rehearsal — is what her son and his colleagues did with them afterwards. Readers who come to this book expecting the origin of modern improv performance will find the origin of its exercises and none of its forms, and that gap is the reason the Chicago traditions had to build the rest themselves.

## Key contributions to the knowledge graph

The whole idea that constraint produces `presence` rather than instruction doing it; `side-coaching` as real-time direction that keeps a player connected instead of stopping the work to correct it; `space-work` and `environment` as the physical route into attention; and evaluation as something structurally retrospective, done by the group about the problem rather than about the person.

## Editions, and the companion volumes

The third edition of 1999 is the one to buy — revised, and coedited by Paul Sills, Spolin's son and the founding director of Second City, which makes the lineage from this book to Chicago improv a literal family one. Earlier printings are the same games with less apparatus.

Spolin later broke the material out into more usable formats, and for most people one of those is the better purchase. *Theater Game File* (1975) puts the games on cards, which is what a teacher actually wants in a room. *Theater Games for Rehearsal: A Director's Handbook* (1985) and *Theater Games for the Classroom: A Teacher's Handbook* (1986) each take the subset relevant to one job and arrange it for that job. The handbook remains the complete source; the spin-offs are the ones that get opened.

## Where it came from

Spolin did not come from the theatre. She learned this method in the 1920s from Neva Boyd at Hull House in Chicago, where games were being used as social work — and the full account of that, and of what it means for who the method was built for, is on [Viola Spolin](/viola-spolin).

The relevant point for the book is narrower. The techniques were designed to draw out people who were not performers and had no intention of becoming any, which is why the book reads as though it were written for teachers. It was.

## Common questions

### Is it still worth reading, sixty years on?

As a workshop resource, yes, and it remains the source most exercises in circulation were copied from. As a book to read through, less so — it was written as a handbook for teachers and directors, and it reads like one, with the exercises laid out as instructions rather than as an argument you follow.

The honest recommendation is to use it rather than read it. Somebody running sessions will return to it for years; somebody looking for a theory of why improvisation works will find that theory largely implied and mostly located elsewhere.

### What is the point of concentration?

Spolin's device for getting a player out of their own head by giving the attention somewhere specific to go — a physical object, a task, a sensory quality — so that self-consciousness has nothing to work on. The exercise supplies a focus, and behaviour follows from attending to it rather than from deciding how to behave.

It is the mechanism underneath most of what the book contains, and the reason her games produce spontaneity without ever instructing anybody to be spontaneous. Telling somebody to relax rarely works; giving them something to concentrate on does.

### Which edition should you buy?

The third, from 1999, which is the one still in print and the one with the improved organisation and index. The earlier printings are historically interesting and harder to teach from.

The companion volumes are separate purchases and serve different jobs — the theater games files reformat the exercises as cards for session use, which is more practical for a teacher actually running a room than the handbook itself.
