**Trains:** building something collectively out of parts nobody negotiated. One player starts a repeating movement and sound; everyone else joins on, one at a time, until the group is a single working machine.

## Setup

One player steps into the space and begins a simple repeating physical action with a sound attached — a piston, a lever, a sweep of the arm with a corresponding noise. It must be something they can sustain.

A second player joins, choosing a movement that connects to the first: physically adjacent, rhythmically related, or causally implied. Then a third. And so on until everyone is part of the machine.

Once it is running, the group can be asked to speed up, slow down, or break down — all together, without discussion.

## Why it works

Every part must fit the whole, and nobody may explain their part. That makes it the clearest physical model of `accepting-the-offer` available: your movement is a response to what already exists, and the moment you add something unrelated the machine stops being one machine.

It also rewards simplicity in a way that is hard to argue with. `be-simple` says a complex offer costs your partner more than it gives, and here the cost is literal — an elaborate movement is unsustainable and unjoinable. The parts that hold the machine together are almost always the boring ones.

## Side-coaching

"Find a part of the machine that is missing." "Connect to somebody, not to the space." "Simpler — you have to do that for four minutes." "Everybody speed up. Everybody slow down." "Listen to the rhythm you are all in."

## What to notice

The moment the machine develops a shared tempo without anyone setting one. It happens reliably and nobody can say who decided it. That is `group-mind` in a form you can watch from outside the circle, which makes it a useful thing to point at later when the same phenomenon shows up invisibly in a scene.

Notice too which players join early and which wait. The late joiners are usually waiting for a good idea, which is exactly the habit the exercise is built to expose — by then, all the easy connections are taken.

## Common failures

**Joining without connecting.** A player stands somewhere free and does an unrelated movement. The group becomes several machines, and the fix is physical: coach them to touch, or nearly touch, an existing part.

**Movements that cannot be sustained.** Anything requiring a full-body throw or a deep squat dies inside thirty seconds and takes the rhythm with it.

**Sound without commitment.** Half-voiced noises, because making a machine noise in front of people is mildly embarrassing. The whole machine reads as tentative until somebody goes fully in — which is why the first player matters more than they realise.

## Variations

- **Themed machine.** A machine for a named purpose: making breakfast, ending a friendship, processing a tax return. Adds an interpretive layer and produces the best version for a room that has already done it once.
- **Emotional machine.** Each part expresses a named emotion at a chosen intensity.
- **Remove a part.** Tap a player out mid-run and see whether the machine compensates. It usually does, which is a quiet demonstration that the ensemble is a system rather than a set of individuals.

## Lineage

A staple of the theatre-games tradition descending from Viola Spolin's group physicalisation work, taught nearly everywhere under names including Machine, The Machine and Group Machine. Like most circle exercises it was carried between rooms rather than published, and the variants outnumber any canonical version.
