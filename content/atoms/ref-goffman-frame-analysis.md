**Erving Goffman. *Frame Analysis: An Essay on the Organization of Experience.* Harper & Row, 1974.**

Goffman's subject is the question people answer constantly without noticing they are answering it: what is going on here. His argument is that the answer arrives through a frame — an interpretive schema that decides what counts as an event, what counts as an interruption, and what can safely be ignored — and that frames are held in common, negotiated, and occasionally broken.

The relevance to improvisation is close to exact, which is unusual for a sociology text. Two performers walking onto an empty stage are doing frame work and nothing else for the first thirty seconds: establishing what kind of situation this is, so that everything after it can be read against something. Goffman also has the vocabulary for what happens when that goes wrong — keying, breaking frame, and the awkward business of a participant who is operating inside a different one from everybody else.

He is writing about ordinary life rather than performance, and the transfer runs both ways. His examples are drawn heavily from theatre, cons and games precisely because those are the cases where the framing is deliberate and therefore visible. What improvisers do on purpose in a scene, everyone else is doing accidentally at dinner.

## Key concepts drawn from here

- The primary framework — the schema that decides what an event even is
- Keying: the same activity re-framed as rehearsal, joke, or threat, and the signals that do the re-framing
- Breaking frame, and why it is so much more disruptive than being wrong inside one
- Frames as maintained jointly, which makes them fragile in exactly the way a scene is
