Party Quirks is one of the most recognisable shortform games, and the one most often played badly. A host throws a party knowing nothing. Three guests each receive an identity from the audience — a profession, an animal, a famous person, a condition — and arrive one at a time playing it. The host works out who each guest is and guesses them out.

## What it actually trains

Most of the site's material on `endowment` is about giving your partner an identity. Party Quirks runs it the other way: you have been handed one, and the entire task is making it legible to somebody who cannot ask you directly. That is a genuinely useful and quite rare skill — showing a choice rather than narrating it — and it is the reason the game survives despite its reputation.

The host's job is the harder one and is almost always overlooked. A host who interrogates — "so, what brings you here, what do you do for a living" — has turned the party into a customs desk, and the guests can only answer in riddles. A host who actually hosts, offering drinks and making introductions, gives the guests ordinary behaviour to be strange inside, which is where the game is.

## The failure everybody has seen

Guests stop being people and become a sequence of clues. Given "postman", the player mimes a bag, mimes a letter, mimes a dog biting them, and none of it is a character — it is charades performed at somebody who is not allowed to say the word. The audience watches a puzzle being assembled rather than a party happening.

The fix is `obvious-choice` applied to behaviour rather than to premise. Pick the most obvious thing a postman would want at a party and then simply want it. The clue arrives as a by-product of playing the person, which is both funnier and easier than engineering it.

*Both rows deliver the clue; only one of them also delivers a party.*

## Running it well

- **Three guests is the ceiling.** Four produces a queue, and the fourth waits eight minutes to do ninety seconds of work.
- **Guess them out one at a time.** Holding all three to the end is tidier and kills the pace, because the host has nothing to do with the information once they have it.
- **Let the host be wrong out loud.** A confident wrong guess is usually the best moment in the game and gives the guest something to react to.
- **Take suggestions that are people, not concepts.** "Someone who has just been fired" plays. "Existential dread" does not, and the audience member who suggested it will not enjoy watching it fail either.

## The honest objection

Some teachers will not run it, and the argument is worth taking seriously: the game rewards playing an adjective, and a performer who gets good at Party Quirks may be getting good at signalling rather than at relating. It is the clearest example of a shortform game that trains a real skill in isolation and can quietly teach a bad habit alongside it — which is the general risk with games built on a secret, and the reason `game-of-the-scene` work usually comes after this kind of thing rather than before.
