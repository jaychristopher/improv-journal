Systemic Collapse Modes are the warning lights. How shared reality falls apart.

Three modes of collapse:

## 1. Latency

Response latency increases. The pause, the hesitation. When actors violate "be present" and start internal computation, the rhythm breaks. The reality lags. You can literally feel the energy drop in the room. The audience experiences it as a stutter in the shared world.

## 2. Fracture

Shared state fractures. Actor A thinks they're in a boat. Actor B thinks they're in a car. The reality breaks because signals conflicted or were unread. This often comes from violating "be positive" — denying offers — or from violating "be present" — missing them entirely.

## 3. Decay

Meaning decays or disappears. If you don't reinforce the reality, if you stop mentioning established facts, those facts fade away. The scene becomes thin and boring because nothing matters anymore. The gun you established ten minutes ago no longer exists in anyone's mind.

## Modes cascade

Latency often triggers fracture (partners fill the gap differently), which accelerates decay (the fracture kills momentum and meaning drains out). Scenes rarely die from one mode alone — the death spiral is latency → fracture → decay, each feeding the next.

When you watch bad improv, you can usually identify exactly which collapse mode is happening. "He denied her offer" = fracture. "She's thinking too much" = latency. "They're just talking but nobody's building — pleasant nothing" = decay. It stops being "they aren't funny" and becomes "they're suffering from systemic collapse."

## Telling them apart while it is happening

Naming the mode afterwards is easy and largely useless. The distinctions that matter are the ones available mid-scene, and each has a different tell.

### Latency: the timing goes before the content does

Nothing has been contradicted and nothing is missing — the replies are simply arriving a beat late, and the audience registers it as the scene being effortful without being able to say why.

### Fracture: two people describing different rooms

It is the only one of the three with a factual signature, so it is the easiest to confirm and the one performers most often let run rather than name.

### Decay: everything works and nothing matters

No mistake has been made. The established facts have simply stopped being referred to, so they stop being real, and the scene continues in a place that is no longer anywhere.

## What each one actually needs

The repairs are not interchangeable, and applying the wrong one is what turns a recoverable scene into a lost one.

![For latency, fracture and decay alike, what the scene feels like it needs runs opposite to what actually repairs it: speed against outward attention, smoothing against naming, novelty against reinforcement.](/images/collapse-repairs.svg)

*Which is why "this scene is not working" points at no repair at all — only the mode does.*

### Latency wants attention moved outward, not speed

Told to be faster, a lagging performer computes faster and stays inside their own head, which is the cause rather than the symptom — [latency recovery](/how-it-works/diagnosis/latency-recovery) covers the move.

### Fracture wants naming, not smoothing

Both realities cannot be true, and continuing as though they can produces a scene the audience has stopped believing. Pick one, out loud if necessary — [fracture recovery](/how-it-works/diagnosis/fracture-recovery) is about doing that without stopping.

### Decay wants reinforcement, and this is the one people get backwards

A scene where nothing matters feels like it needs something new, so a performer introduces a fresh element — which adds another unreinforced fact and accelerates exactly the failure it was meant to fix. [Decay recovery](/how-it-works/diagnosis/decay-recovery) runs the other way: return to something already established and make it cost somebody something.

Which is the practical value of separating the three at all. All three feel from inside like *this scene is not working*, and that sensation points at no repair. The mode does.

## Attribution note

"Latency, fracture, decay" as a formal taxonomy of collapse modes is original synthesis — no published improv source categorizes scene failure in these terms. The concept draws on scattered practitioner diagnostics: Napier's scene-failure analysis (*Improvise*), UCB's implicit treatment of divergent scenes and game loss, and common improv teaching vocabulary. **Counter-position (Napier):** Labeling failure modes formally may increase the very self-monitoring that causes latency. This framework is designed for post-scene coaching and teacher diagnosis, not performer self-evaluation during performance.
