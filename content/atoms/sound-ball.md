A circle. You throw an imaginary ball to somebody and make a sound as it leaves your hands. They catch it making the same sound, then invent a new one and throw it on. It takes about four minutes, and it is one of the few warm-ups a group can say something specific about afterwards.

## The two halves, and only one is hard

Throwing is easy. Catching is the exercise, because catching means reproducing the sound you were given rather than the sound you would have preferred, and the reflex to improve it is close to universal.

Watch a circle for two minutes and the same failure appears repeatedly: somebody receives a flat bip and returns a richer, funnier version of it. They are being generous, in their own account of it. What they are doing is declining the offer and substituting their own, which is the whole of `accepting-the-offer` compressed into half a second and made visible to everybody watching.

## What it is for

It makes an offer small enough that refusing one has nowhere to hide. In a scene, a partner who quietly overrides what you established can look like a good player having better ideas. Here the original is two seconds old and everybody heard it, so the substitution is unmistakable — which is why this works on people who have been told they block and do not believe it.

The throwing half trains something smaller but real: producing a sound with no time to evaluate it. `hesitation` in a circle looks like a pause while somebody auditions options, and the pause is more conspicuous than any sound could be.

## Side-coaching it

The whole exercise turns on one correction, and it has to be called while the ball is moving rather than saved for afterwards — stopping the circle to explain is `side-coaching` done badly, and it hands everybody time to start deliberating.

*Same sound.* This is the only note that matters and it will be needed repeatedly. Say it flatly, to nobody in particular, and let the circle carry on.

*Don't improve it.* The version of the same note that lands with people who think they are being generous, which is most of them.

*Faster.* Speed is the mechanism here, not a stylistic preference. A round slow enough to think in is a round that trains nothing.

*Look at who you are throwing to.* Circles drift into throwing at the floor within a minute, and once the throw stops being aimed at a person the catching stops being an obligation.

## Variants worth knowing

Adding a movement is the most useful change and the one to reach for second. Sound and movement together make the substitution obvious even to the person doing it — you can talk yourself into believing a sound was the same, and you cannot do that with a gesture your body just refused to copy.

Two balls in the circle splits attention and is a genuine test rather than a warm-up. Most groups discover they had been tracking only the person opposite them, and the second ball removes that option.

Adding names — say the person's name as you throw — slows the round slightly and fixes the throwing-into-the-void problem more reliably than side-coaching does. Worth it with a group who do not know each other yet, where it is doing two jobs at once.

Anything competitive is worth avoiding. An elimination version exists and turns a warm-up into an audition, which is the failure this exercise was chosen to avoid.

## Running it well

- **Do not let it get clever.** Once the circle starts competing on funny noises it has become a performance and stopped being an exercise. Boring sounds are correct.
- **Keep it fast.** Speed is what removes deliberation, and a slow round is one where everybody has time to prepare.
- **Say what you saw afterwards.** The value is in naming who improved the sounds they were given — nobody notices that about themselves in the moment, and everybody recognises it immediately when it is described.
