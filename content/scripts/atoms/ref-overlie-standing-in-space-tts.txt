[curious] So what's the framework, stripped down?

[teaching] Six materials. Space, Shape, Time, Emotion, Movement, Story. And here's the part that matters — the arrangement is the argument. They're six things of equal standing. Not a hierarchy. Not a pyramid with something at the top.

[curious] [short pause] Which sounds almost like a non-statement until you notice what's sitting in that list.

[emphatic] Story. Story is in there as one of six. Same size as the others. And conventional theatre does the opposite — everything serves story. Space serves story, emotion serves story, time serves story. Overlie takes story off the shelf and sets it down on the floor with the other five.

[curious] So it's a demotion.

[teaching] It's a deliberate demotion. And she's insistent that it's a refusal, not an oversight. She's not saying "I forgot to organize these." She's saying "I declined to."

[conversational] Which for an improviser is a genuinely uncomfortable idea. Because we're trained to serve the scene. We talk about what the scene needs, where the story wants to go, what the game is. Story is the gravity we all move under.

[teaching] And she's asking what happens if you turn the gravity off. Not forever — as a way of looking. What if for the next twenty minutes, the shape your body is making has exactly as much claim on your attention as what the scene is about? What if the tempo is not a tool for delivering the story, it's just — tempo. Its own thing. Worth watching.

[curious] Where does that come from? Because that's not how actor training talks.

[matter-of-fact] It isn't, and that's the key to the whole book. Overlie came out of postmodern dance — the Judson Dance Theater world. Not actor training. So the framework reads as a way of looking rather than a set of exercises. It's a lens, not a lesson plan.

[teaching] [pause] Yes. If you open it looking for something to run on Tuesday night — a warm-up, a structure, a side-coaching script — you will be disappointed. It's a philosophical account. [curious] Now the other half. There's a second book with Viewpoints in the title, and most people read that one.

[teaching] Bogart and Landau. They expanded the physical elements into nine and organized them for rehearsal. Nine channels, and you can work them. That's why it spread — it's usable. And Overlie's position was that this was a different project, not an improvement on hers.

[conversational] There's something almost funny about it. The version that got organized for rehearsal beat the version that refused to be organized. By a lot. For decades.

[curious] So who should actually read this?

[teaching] The clearest case: anyone who met Viewpoints through Bogart and Landau and found the nine channels more prescriptive than the work seemed to need. [matter-of-fact] Two groups. Teachers looking for material — there's far less here than in Bogart and Landau, by design. And anyone working on narrative.
