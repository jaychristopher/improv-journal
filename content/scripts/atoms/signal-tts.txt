[curious] I want to start with the thing nobody tells you in your first class. You're always transmitting. Whether you mean to or not.

[warm] No off switch.

[curious] Right. Even when you think you're being neutral — that's a signal. "I am being neutral right now" is itself a transmission your partner is reading.

[teaching] Anything that goes from you to your partner is a signal. Intentional or not. The category is broader than people think — words are signals, sure. So is tone. So is posture. So is timing. So is the half-second delay before you respond.

[short pause]

[contemplative] So when I think "I haven't decided what to do yet" — my partner is already reading my hesitation.

[matter-of-fact] They're reading "hesitation." Which is content. Which means you've already made an offer, you just don't know it yet.

[curious] Multiple channels?

[teaching] Words. Tone — pitch, pace, volume. Physical — posture, gesture, facial expression, eye contact, movement. Spatial — where you are on stage relative to your partner and the environment. Temporal — when you respond. Immediacy versus delay. All simultaneously.

[curious] And they can contradict each other.

[matter-of-fact] Constantly. Saying "I'm fine" with a clenched jaw. Acting angry while winking at the audience. Both layers are read. The audience picks up on the incongruence.

[short pause]

[curious] Is incongruence always noise?

[teaching] Not always. Sometimes it IS the content. A character lying — on purpose. The words say one thing, the body says another. The audience reads both. The performer is congruent — they're honestly playing the lie. The character is not. That's the texture.

[warm] You're not solving for "all channels saying the same thing." You're solving for "you know what each channel is doing."

[curious] So congruent signal — all aligned — reads as what?

[matter-of-fact] Truth. Whatever you're transmitting reads as true. Not necessarily accurate to facts — but emotionally truthful. Convincing. Land-able.

[contemplative] And incongruent without intent reads as...

[teaching] Noise. Confusion. The audience doesn't know which signal to follow. Energy leaks. Coherence frays.

[long pause]

[curious] Status lives in the signal layer, right?

[teaching] Status is read and assigned through signals — usually below conscious awareness. The tilt of the head, the pause before responding, eye contact held or broken. Status transactions are conducted through signal channels, not through declared positions. Nobody walks on stage and announces "I'm high status." They signal it. The audience reads it.

[curious] Or fails to. Which is how status confusion happens.

[matter-of-fact] Right. Your conscious self thinks you're playing low status. Your body is signaling high. The audience reads the body. The scene becomes incoherent because the performer is fighting their own signal.

[short pause]

[curious] Signal-to-noise ratio.

[teaching] The quality of a scene depends not on the brilliance of individual signals but on the signal-to-noise ratio of the entire interaction. Congruent signals build shared reality. Distorted signals erode it. One signal at a time is fine. The aggregate is what shapes the scene.

[curious] So the discipline isn't being clever. It's being clean.

[warm] Clean. Honest. Simple. Be Honest and Be Simple aren't aesthetic preferences. They're structural necessities. Clarity is what makes signals receivable. Simplicity is what reduces the surface area for noise.

[long pause]

[contemplative] There's something Wittgenstein-ish about this. "The meaning of a word is its use in the language." Signals don't carry meaning intrinsically.

[teaching] They're observer-interpreted. What you intend to signal and what your partner receives may differ. You cannot control reception, only transmission. Which is why clarity matters — not because clarity guarantees correct reception, but because it minimizes the work the receiver has to do to land where you wanted.

[curious] Shannon and Weaver thought.

[matter-of-fact] Shannon-Weaver communication theory — the formal origin of "signal" as a technical term. 1948 mathematical theory of communication. The improv vocabulary borrows the metaphor and gives it social texture.

[short pause]

[curious] No improv author uses "signal" as a technical term in this way, right?

[teaching] Johnstone says "offers" and "status transactions." Spolin says "focus" and "the problem." UCB says "game moves." Signal is a bridging metaphor — engineering vocabulary applied to performance. Defensible because the structural intuition matches. Should be acknowledged as novel synthesis, not inherited tradition.

[contemplative] And Stuart Hall's counter — meaning is constructed in reception, not encoded in signals.

[matter-of-fact] Aligned with the meaning-is-relational law. Signal is a useful frame. It has known limits. The full picture is — you transmit, they receive, meaning emerges in the gap. Signal describes the transmission side cleanly. The metaphor breaks down at the reception side, where signals stop being signals and start being interpretations.

[long pause]

[curious] Practical takeaway?

[emphatic] Everything you do is signal. Choose the ones you send on purpose. Notice the ones you send by accident. The improvers who feel "magnetic" on stage aren't louder or wilder. Their signals are cleaner.

[warm] Same skill off stage. The most powerful people in any room aren't usually the loudest. They're the ones whose body, voice, words, and timing all say the same thing.
