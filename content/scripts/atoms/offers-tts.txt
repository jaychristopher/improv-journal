[curious] Okay, "offer." It's one of those words that gets thrown around in every class I've ever taken, and I'm not sure I've ever heard anyone define it.

[teaching] Anything that advances the scene. A word, a gesture, a look, a shift in posture, a silence. If it adds to the shared reality, it's an offer. But here's the part that actually matters — Johnstone's phrasing isn't "everything is an offer." It's treat everything as an offer.

[curious] That's a small difference.

[matter-of-fact] It's the whole thing. It moves the responsibility from the person who did it to the person who saw it. Your partner scratches their nose because their nose itched. That's not an offer until you decide it is. Meaning gets completed by uptake, not intent.

[conversational] So "I have a gun" isn't a genre.

[teaching] Right. "I have a gun" is a cop drama or a Christmas morning, depending entirely on what comes next. The line has no meaning sitting there on its own. It gets its meaning from the response.

[curious] So what counts? Give me the range.

[matter-of-fact] Verbal — the words. Physical — gesture, posture, space work. Emotional — the tone, the thing underneath the words. Relational — who you are to each other, what you want. Environmental — the space, the objects, the cold, the clock. [short pause] And the richest ones are almost never the big ones. Not "we're on a spaceship." A shift in weight. A pause a half-second too long. Those carry emotional information, which is what actually grounds a scene in something real.

[contemplative] Which is also just how conversations work.

[warm] Entirely. Ordinary talk runs on the same currency, and mostly in the small bills. What you do with what you were handed beats whatever clever thing you were holding in reserve.

[curious] Here's my real question, though. "Make more offers" is advice I've been given, and it has never once helped me.

[emphatic] Because it's the wrong axis. The question isn't volume, it's whether the offer is usable. And a usable offer does two things at the same time — it commits to something specific, and it leaves your partner somewhere to go.

[curious] Give me both failures.

[teaching] The vague one first. "So... here we are." Nothing in it. There's nothing to build on because nothing was said. [pause] Now the other end. "I'm your brother and I've come to tell you our mother died and the will is contested and I've been drinking." Enormously specific. Also dead. The only available response is to stand there and receive information.

[curious] Huh. So too much is the same failure as too little.

[matter-of-fact] Same test read from opposite ends. Which is exactly why "make more offers" fixes neither one — one player needs to commit harder, the other needs to commit to less.

[conversational] Is there a test?

[confident] Ask what your partner's next line has to be. If you can only imagine one, you've written their part for them. Compare it to "I'm your brother." Three words, gives a whole relationship, and leaves a hundred doors open.

[curious] Okay, the one everyone fights about. Questions. I've had teachers ban them outright.

[teaching] And that ban fixes the common case, which is why it survives. The common case is a question that transfers the work. "What are we doing here?" You've handed your partner the entire scene while appearing to participate. It looks like offering. It's a refusal wearing a costume.

[curious] But not all of them.

[emphatic] "Why are you wearing my coat?" Six words. There's a relationship, a history, a grievance, and a location in there. That's a powerful offer that happens to end in a question mark.

[contemplative] So what's the actual rule?

[matter-of-fact] Not "no questions." A question has to add before it asks. Take the question mark off and see what's left standing. If nothing's left, it wasn't an offer — it was extraction. [short pause] And that's the same line blocking turns on. You either put something into the shared thing or you take something out of it.

[curious] One more. You said everything can be treated as an offer. Does everybody actually believe that?

[serious] Not quite, and it's worth knowing where it strains. Treating everything as an offer makes the receiver responsible for meaning — that's generous, and in a class it's liberating. Nobody's offer is wrong. Nothing is wasted.

[curious] And in a show?

[teaching] The game-first schools want you to identify one pattern and heighten it. Under that account, treating every twitch as significant isn't attentiveness — it's a failure to prioritize. One frame says nothing is wasted. The other says most of it should be.

[contemplative] Those don't reconcile.

[warm] They don't need to. They're instructions for different rooms. [pause] And there's a version of this pointed at yourself, too, which I think gets missed. The position you took, the word that escaped your mouth, the thing you mimed by accident — those are offers you made to yourself, and you now owe the scene something for them.

[curious] Meaning I don't get to pretend I didn't.

[emphatic] You commit to what you actually did instead of wishing you'd done something else. That's the bridge from offers to commitment. [short pause] Beginners lose whole scenes hunting for the right offer. There is no right offer. There's the one that got made, and what you do with it.
