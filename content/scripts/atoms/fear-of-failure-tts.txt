[curious] Here's something I noticed going through my old class notes. "You blocked that." "You were in your head." "You went for the joke instead of the moment." "Your offer was too vague." Four different notes. Four different weeks. Am I actually doing four different things wrong?

[teaching] No. You're doing one thing wrong in four different costumes. Every antipattern in improv is a fear response. Not a talent problem, not a creativity problem — fear.

[curious] Okay, but that feels almost too tidy. "It's all fear" is the kind of thing that stops being useful the second you say it.

[teaching] Right, and that's the trap. Because it's not one big fear radiating outward into everything. It's more specific than that. Blocking is fear of losing control — your partner just made you a divorce lawyer and you didn't agree to that. Hesitation is fear of the unknown direction. Playwriting in your head is fear of the present moment, so you retreat to something known. Cleverness is fear of sincerity. Hedging — making an offer so vague it can't be wrong — that's fear of commitment.

[emphatic] Six antipatterns, six separate fears. Parallel lines that don't cross. If it were one shared cause you'd see a fan shape, everything spreading from one point. It's not. It's six separate cables running the same direction.

[curious] Why does that distinction matter, though? If they're all fear —

[matter-of-fact] Because the fix is specific. "Be less afraid" is not a note anyone can take. "You're hedging because you don't want to be the one who defines the relationship — so define it, be wrong, we'll build on wrong" — that's a note.

[teaching] That's wimping. Johnstone's word. You accepted without adding. And it looks like agreement — it looks like yes-and — but you took the offer and handed back an empty box, because defining who "she" is means being the person who might get it wrong.

[teaching] Brené Brown's research is useful here. Her line is that shame is the secret killer of innovation. There is no version of committing to a character that isn't exposure.

[serious] And she names perfectionism as fear's most insidious disguise. That belief of "I am what I accomplish, and how well I accomplish it." That produces a performer who monitors every move instead of playing one.

[curious] Monitoring instead of playing. That's the whole thing, isn't it. That's the person you can see thinking.

[warm] That's the person you can see thinking. And here's where improv training gets interesting, because the traditional answer is "get comfortable with failure." Spolin didn't go there. [curious] But you have to do something with the fear. You can't just declare it invalid.

[teaching] Her student Gary Schwartz has the move: fun is the antidote to fear. Teaching students to get used to failure keeps failure in the premise — it stays the thing you're managing. Instead, start with a game. Have enough fun and the fear dissipates on its own, because there's no room left for it.

[contemplative] Rachel Denyer has three principles that map almost too cleanly. Be kind — that's the fear of being judged. Be bold — fear of getting started. Embrace uncertainty — fear of the unknown.

[curious] Is there anything fear is actually good for? Because I've had shows where the nerves made me sharper.

[matter-of-fact] Yes, and that's a real distinction, not a hedge. Adrenaline sharpens focus. Reappraising fear as excitement measurably improves performance. [conversational] And the thing improv has that almost no other art form has — it comes with teammates built in. You're never alone out there. If your anxiety takes over, your scene partners will come get you.
