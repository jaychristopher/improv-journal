[curious] There's an exercise almost everybody has done, and almost nobody thinks about after they've done it. The machine. Everyone stands in a circle, one person makes a repeating movement with a sound, everybody piles on. Why does that one survive when so many warmups don't?

[teaching] Because it needs nothing. No chairs, no props, no premise, no prep. That's the whole family it comes from — the games that travel are the games that need nothing at all. But that's why it survives. Why it *works* is a different question, and the answer is that it's the cleanest physical picture of accepting an offer that anyone has built.

[curious] Say more. It looks like a warmup. Get the blood moving, make some noise, get over yourself.

[teaching] It does that too. But watch what's actually being asked. You step in, you find a movement, and there's exactly one rule underneath everything: your part has to connect to what's already there. Physically adjacent, rhythmically related, or causally implied. And you can't explain it. There's no negotiation. You can't say "so I'm the conveyor belt feeding your stamper." You just do a thing, and either it belongs or it doesn't.

[conversational] Which is the scene. That's just the scene, standing up.

[teaching] That's the scene with the words taken away. In a scene you can paper over a non-acceptance with dialogue — you can be charming, you can be funny, you can talk fast enough that nobody clocks that you ignored what your partner gave you. In the machine there's nowhere to hide. Somebody stands off in a free corner doing an unrelated movement, and the group doesn't become one machine with a weird part. It becomes three machines.

[curious] And everybody can see it. From the outside it's obvious.

[emphatic] Instantly. That's the gift. You don't have to give a note about it in the abstract. You point. Three machines. And the fix isn't a note about listening, it's physical — get closer. Touch somebody, or nearly touch them. Connect to a person, not to the space.

[contemplative] There's something a little brutal about that failure, though. Nothing's wrong with any individual part. Every single person is doing a fine, committed, sustainable movement.

[teaching] That's exactly what makes it worth pointing at. Nobody failed. The parts are all good. And it still isn't a machine. [pause] That's most bad scenes, honestly. Two people doing perfectly reasonable work that isn't the same work.

[curious] Okay, second failure. What kills a machine faster than anything?

[matter-of-fact] Somebody being interesting. [short pause] A big full-body throw, a deep squat, something with three beats and a flourish. It's great for twenty seconds. It's dead at forty. And when it dies it takes the rhythm with it, because everybody downstream was keying off it.

[conversational] Be simple, and here the cost of not being simple is literally measured in your quads.

[teaching] It's the only room I know where "be simple" isn't a preference — it's physics. A complex offer costs your partner more than it gives them. In a scene that cost is invisible; your partner just quietly has more work to do and you feel clever. Here you can watch the cost. The elaborate movement is unjoinable, because to connect to it you'd have to decode it first. The parts that hold the machine together are almost always the boring ones. A lever. A hiss. An arm going up and down.

[curious] Third one — sound. I've seen so many silent machines.

[warm] Half-voiced machines. Everyone's mouth is moving and nothing is coming out, because making a factory noise in a room full of adults is mildly humiliating. And the whole thing reads tentative until one person goes fully in. Which is why the first player matters more than they know — you're not just setting the movement, you're setting the permission level for the volume.

[contemplative] Here's the part I actually want to talk about. The tempo.

[teaching] Yes. [pause] It happens every time. Six, eight people, all doing different movements at different rates, and somewhere around the ninety-second mark the whole thing locks into a shared tempo. Nobody set it. And here's the thing — ask afterward. Ask who decided. Nobody can tell you. Nobody knows.

[curious] That's group mind you can see from outside the circle.

[emphatic] That's the whole reason to keep this exercise. Group mind is normally invisible. It shows up in a scene as three people arriving at the same idea and you can't teach it, you can only notice it happened. But the machine externalises it. You can literally stand outside and watch a group agree on something without speaking. And then six months later when it happens in a scene, you have a thing to point at. "That. That's the tempo thing."

[conversational] What else should you be watching, if you're outside the circle?

[matter-of-fact] Who joins early and who waits. Watch that every time.

[curious] The waiters are the interesting ones.

[teaching] The waiters are looking for a good idea. That's the whole diagnosis. They're standing there running options, waiting to have something worth adding — and the exercise is built to punish exactly that, because by the time they've thought of something, all the easy connections are taken. The people who stepped in second and third didn't have better ideas. They had no ideas. They walked over to a body and did something adjacent to it.

[contemplative] That's a hard habit to see in yourself.

[warm] It's a hard habit to see anywhere. It's the meeting where you've got a half-formed thought and you polish it silently while three other people build the thing without you. By the time it's good enough to say, it's not needed. [pause] The machine gives you a thirty-second version of that with your body, and thirty seconds is short enough that you can actually feel it happen.

[curious] Once a room's done it clean, where do you take it?

[teaching] Give it a purpose. A machine for making breakfast. A machine for ending a friendship. A machine for processing a tax return. Suddenly every part has to mean something as well as fit, and the group has to read each other's intent, not just their rhythm. Or give each part an emotion at an intensity.

[conversational] And the one where you pull somebody out mid-run.

[teaching] Tap a part out and watch. The machine almost always compensates — somebody widens their movement, somebody shifts to cover the gap, and nobody was told to. [pause] Which is the quietest and best argument I know that an ensemble isn't a group of individuals who agreed to cooperate. It's a system. You can remove a piece and the system reaches for the hole.
