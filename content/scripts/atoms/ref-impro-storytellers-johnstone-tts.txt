[curious] So there's a sequel to Impro. Twenty years later. And I get the sense most people who loved the first one never finished the second.

[teaching] That's about right. And it's not because it's worse. It's because it's a different kind of object entirely. Impro is an argument that happens to contain exercises. Impro for Storytellers is a manual that happens to contain an argument.

[curious] Meaning what, practically? If I pick it up, what am I holding?

[matter-of-fact] Sixteen chapters, and the table of contents tells you everything. Theatresports. Audience Suggestions. Trouble with Feedback. Spontaneity. Making Things Happen. Story Games. Some Filler Games. Procedures. Serious Scenes. Character. Technical Stuff.

[conversational] That's a company manual. That's how you run the format, how you handle an audience, how you judge, what you do when a show is dying in front of two hundred people, and what you say to the cast afterward.

[curious] So it's for teachers.

[teaching] It grew out of a small earlier thing called Don't Be Prepared — Theatresports for Teachers. And it never leaves that register. The whole book is Johnstone talking to somebody who has to run a session on Thursday night. [short pause] Which is either exactly what you need or completely not.

[curious] Okay, formats. It documents three of his, right?

[matter-of-fact] Three, all still played worldwide, all set out here in enough detail that you could actually run them tomorrow. Theatresports is the one in the subtitle — teams, scenes, judges, an audience with real power over the outcome. This book isn't one account of Theatresports among several. It's the primary source.

[teaching] Then Micetro, which drops teams entirely and eliminates individuals round by round. Which sounds like a small variation until you're running a drop-in night and you have no idea who's walking through the door or how much experience they have. Micetro doesn't care. That's the point of it.

[curious] And the third?

[confident] Gorilla Theatre. And it's the strange one. Each performer takes a turn as director — and then gets judged on a scene other people perform.

[curious] Wait. You're evaluated on work you didn't do?

[emphatic] You're evaluated on work you caused. Nobody else puts the pressure there. Every other format, the pressure is on the person in the scene. This puts it on the person who set the scene up and then has to stand at the side and watch it happen.

[contemplative] Which, if you've ever directed anything, or managed anyone, is a very specific and very familiar kind of helplessness.

[curious] So how do you actually read this thing? Cover to cover?

[matter-of-fact] No. And that's the whole trick. You read it by chapter, against a problem you already have. It's arranged to make that possible and it reads badly any other way — which is most of why the Impro crowd bounces off it.

[teaching] Show dying in front of an audience? Theatresports and Procedures. Group's gone polite and cautious and nobody will commit to anything? Spontaneity and Making Things Happen. Cast can improvise beautifully but can't tell a story to save their lives? Story Games.

[curious] What about the scenes that just stall, and nobody in the room can say why?

[emphatic] That's the blocking taxonomy, and it will usually name the move within a page. That's the single biggest contribution in the book, and none of it is in Impro.

[curious] Give me the names.

[teaching] Wimping. Cancelling. Bridging. Hedging. Pimping. It's the most detailed diagnostic of scene failure anybody has written. And wimping in particular gets promoted — it's not filed as weak blocking anymore, it's its own move. Which matters, because wimping is what most decent improvisers actually do. They don't say no. They just... don't say anything with weight in it.

[contemplative] And you know that from meetings. Nobody blocks the idea. Everybody hedges it to death.

[curious] What else comes out of this book that wasn't in the first one?

[matter-of-fact] Overaccepting — answering a tiny offer with enormous weight. And reincorporation pushed right to the front of narrative craft. His image for it is the improviser walking backwards. You're not inventing what comes next, you're shaping the story out of what you've already left lying around.

[warm] And I'd single out the chapter on feedback. Giving notes badly is the most common way a teacher makes a group worse, and almost nothing else in the literature treats it as a skill with its own failure modes. Johnstone does.

[curious] Alright — who should actually buy this?

[confident] Anybody running a group or a show. If that show is Theatresports, it's close to indispensable. There is no better account anywhere of the problems that format creates for itself — scoring, judging, an audience holding power, and what all of that does to the people on stage.

[matter-of-fact] And if you're a general reader who just wants to think about improv? Read Impro instead. I'll say that plainly. The theory here is thin by design. The games are indexed by function rather than by argument. Read cover to cover it's repetitive the way a reference work is repetitive.

[warm] The people who loved the first book and stalled on this one haven't changed their minds about Johnstone. They picked up a different kind of object and were disappointed it wasn't the same one.

[curious] So — the two of them together.
