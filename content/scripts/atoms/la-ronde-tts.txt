[curious] Okay, La Ronde. I know the name, I know it's a chain of two-person scenes. But what is it actually for?

[teaching] It's a format where every scene shares a character with the next one. You and I do a scene. Then you leave, I stay, and someone new walks in. Then I leave, they stay, someone else walks in. Five scenes later, the last person meets the first person, and the circle closes.

[curious] So it's a daisy chain of duets.

[teaching] That's the mechanic. But the mechanic isn't the point. The point is what the audience learns by watching one person get handed to four different partners.

[short pause]

[teaching] Because you don't stay the same. You're the same character — same name, same job, same history — but you're a different animal depending on who's across from you. That's the whole format. That's the thesis.

[curious] Say more, because I think most people run this as "structure exercise" and stop there.

[teaching] Think about your own life. There's a version of you that exists with your mother. There's a version with your boss. There's a version with the friend you've known since you were nineteen. Those aren't masks. They're all genuinely you. They're just different you's, summoned by different people.

[contemplative] La Ronde puts that on stage and makes the audience watch it happen in real time.

[curious] Where does it come from?

[matter-of-fact] Arthur Schnitzler wrote a play in 1897 called Reigen — a chain of ten sexual encounters, each sharing a partner with the next, closing the loop. Craig Cackowski saw the film adaptation in Chicago in the mid-nineties and thought, that's a longform. He brought it into Frank Booth rehearsals and they ran it for over a year as an exercise. Miles Stroth independently landed on a version of the same thing at iO while teaching story structure.

[curious] Two people arriving at it separately is a good sign the idea has bones.

[teaching] It does. And it means the format keeps getting rediscovered by people who notice the same thing — that relationship, not plot, is the engine.

[short pause]

[curious] Okay, so how do you run it badly?

[emphatic] You treat the shared character as a baton. You hand it off, you announce yourself, you play the same three notes in every scene because you think consistency means sameness.

[teaching] That's the most common failure and it's the exact inverse of the format. Consistency in La Ronde doesn't mean playing identically. It means the audience can trace *why* you shifted. Same guy, softer with his daughter, sharper with his brother, and the sharpness has a shape you recognize from the softness.

[curious] So the through-line is the person, not the performance.

[teaching] Right. If you're playing "loud guy" in all four scenes, you've done nothing. If you're playing a man who's loud because he's terrified of silence, and we watch that terror behave differently with his wife, his employee, and his priest — now the audience is building a human being out of five scenes.

[short pause]

[curious] There's a variant with two waves, isn't there?

[teaching] Four players do a full round of scenes. Then something happens offstage — a death, an affair, someone loses a job. You don't play it. It just happens. And then you run the same pairs again.

[emphatic] It's devastating when it works. Because now the audience has a baseline. They know what this relationship sounded like on Tuesday. And they get to watch what it sounds like after.

[curious] That's a lot of trust in the audience's memory.

[matter-of-fact] Which is why size matters. Four to six players is the sweet spot. Past seven or eight, the circle gets too long — by the time you close the loop, nobody remembers who scene one was, and the ending lands as trivia instead of as an ending.

[short pause]

[curious] What about the objection? Because I can hear a UCB-trained voice in my head saying this format doesn't find a game.

[matter-of-fact] It's a fair objection and I wouldn't argue with it. La Ronde has a predetermined scene order. You know who's coming out next before anyone opens their mouth. Compared to organic forms, or a Harold that discovers its own shape, it can feel mechanical.

[teaching] And it will absolutely expose anyone who can't hold a character for more than one scene. There's nowhere to hide. You can't reinvent yourself when the lights come up, because half the audience just watched you be someone specific.

[curious] Is that a bug?

[confident] Depends what you think improv is for. If your pedagogy is game-first, La Ronde is a diagnostic tool with a show attached. If your pedagogy is relationship-first, that exposure is the entire curriculum. It's not a neutral format — it has an argument about what matters.

[short pause]

[curious] So what's the note you give someone struggling in it?

[teaching] Stop deciding who you are. Decide what this specific person does to you.

[warm] Because that's the move. When the new player walks on, your job isn't to keep being the guy from the last scene. Your job is to let this new person pull something different out of the guy from the last scene. Same clay, different hands.

[contemplative] And when the circle closes — when you finally meet the person you started with — the audience isn't watching a structural payoff. They're watching someone come home changed by everyone they met on the way there.
