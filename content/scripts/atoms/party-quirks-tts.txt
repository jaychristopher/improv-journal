[curious] Party Quirks. Okay. I have a complicated relationship with this game.

[warm] Everyone does. It's the one your aunt has seen on TV, and it's the one your teacher quietly stops running after the third month.

[curious] So what is it actually doing? Because on paper it's simple — host throws a party, three guests arrive with secret identities from the audience, host guesses them out.

[teaching] On paper it's simple. Underneath, it's running endowment backwards. Almost everything we talk about with endowment is about giving — you decide who your partner is, you hand them a history, they get to be someone because you said so. Party Quirks hands *you* the identity and says: now make this legible to a person who is not allowed to ask.

[curious] Which is a weirdly specific skill.

[matter-of-fact] It's a rare one. Showing a choice instead of narrating it. Most scene work lets you say the thing. This game takes the saying away and leaves you with only behaviour. [pause] That constraint is the whole value, and it's why the game survives its own reputation.

[curious] So why does it have that reputation?

[emphatic] Because of what guests do under pressure. You get "postman." And the player mimes the bag. Then mimes a letter. Then mimes a dog biting them on the leg.

[conversational] Oh, I've been that person.

[serious] We all have. And none of it is a character. It's charades performed at somebody who isn't allowed to say the word. The audience isn't watching a party — they're watching a puzzle get assembled. You can feel a room go polite when that starts.

[curious] So what's the fix? Because the clue has to land somehow.

[teaching] The fix is the obvious choice, but applied to behaviour instead of premise. Don't ask what would signal "postman." Ask what the most obvious thing a postman would *want* at a party is — and then simply want it. Want to leave early because you're up at four. Be weirdly relaxed about someone's aggressive dog. Know everyone on the street by name and mention it without thinking about it.

[curious] And the clue comes off the side of that.

[warm] The clue comes off the side of it. Which is both funnier and much easier than engineering it. You're not building an escape room. You're playing a person, and the information falls out of them the way information falls out of people at real parties.

[curious] Okay, but I want to talk about the host, because in every Party Quirks I've watched, the host is basically furniture.

[emphatic] The host has the harder job and it's almost universally overlooked. Here's the failure: the host starts interrogating. "So — what brings you here? What do you do for a living? Where'd you grow up?"

[conversational] That's a customs desk.

[matter-of-fact] That's a customs desk. And when you interrogate, the guests can only answer in riddles, because a direct question about identity has no honest answer in this game. You've forced them into evasion, and evasion reads as coy, and coy is death.

[curious] So what does a good host do instead?

[teaching] Hosts. Actually hosts. Offers a drink. Introduces guest one to guest two. Worries about the music. Asks someone to help with the ice. [short pause] What that does is give the guests ordinary behaviour to be strange *inside*. You can't be a bizarre houseguest if there's no house. Give them a coat to take off, a plate to refuse, a conversation to derail — and suddenly the quirk has a surface to play against.

[contemplative] The host isn't the detective. The host is the normal.

[warm] The host is the normal. That's the whole role. And the more real the party is, the sharper every strange choice looks against it.

[curious] Say I'm running this in a jam or a show. What do I actually control?

[matter-of-fact] Four things. First — three guests is the ceiling. Four sounds generous and it produces a queue. Your fourth player waits eight minutes to do ninety seconds of work, and by the time they arrive the audience has spent their attention.

[curious] Second?

[matter-of-fact] Guess them out one at a time. It's tempting to hold all three to the end because it feels tidier, like a reveal. It kills the pace. Once the host knows, they've got nothing to *do* with the knowing. Cash it in, clear the stage, keep moving.

[curious] Third.

[emphatic] Let the host be wrong out loud. Confidently, specifically wrong. "You're a dentist who hates his father." [pause] A confident wrong guess is usually the best moment in the game, because now the guest has something to react to — they get to be offended, or flattered, or briefly consider it. Hosts who guess silently in their heads are protecting themselves and starving the scene.

[curious] And fourth?

[teaching] Take suggestions that are people, not concepts. "Someone who just got fired" plays beautifully. "Existential dread" does not, and here's the part that matters — the audience member who shouted it won't enjoy watching it fail either. Nobody wins. You're not being precious by steering that; you're protecting the person who offered it.

[curious] Okay. I want to give the objection its due, because I know teachers who won't run this game at all.

[serious] And the argument is worth taking seriously. The claim is that Party Quirks rewards playing an adjective. That a performer who gets *good* at this is getting good at signalling — at broadcasting a trait outward — rather than at relating to the person in front of them.

[contemplative] Which are almost opposite muscles.

[matter-of-fact] They can be. And this is the clearest example of a shortform game that trains a genuinely real skill in isolation while quietly teaching a bad habit alongside it. That's the general risk with any game built on a secret. The secret pulls your attention off your partner and onto your own delivery.

[curious] So is it a no?

[warm] It's a sequencing question, not a ban. Do the game-of-the-scene work *after* this kind of thing, not before. Learn to show rather than tell — then learn that showing is something you do *to somebody*, not at a room.

[contemplative] The version of this game that works isn't three people demonstrating traits at a bewildered host. It's four people at a party where one of them is trying to figure out what's going on, and the other three genuinely came for the drinks.
