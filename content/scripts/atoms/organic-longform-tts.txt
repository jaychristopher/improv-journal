[curious] Okay, so a group walks onstage, takes a suggestion, and just... starts. No opening. No three-by-three. Nobody's counting beats. What are we actually watching?

[teaching] Organic longform. And the honest origin of the name is funnier than people expect — Will Hines put it plainly: if you didn't use an opening, it got called organic improv. That's it. That's the whole definition at first. It's a negative space term. It's defined by what it refuses.

[curious] Which makes it sound like the absence of a form rather than a form.

[matter-of-fact] It sounds that way, and that's the trap. Because there is a structure. It's just not on the whiteboard — it's in the performers. The shape of the show comes from what the scenes need. This scene has more in it, so we stay. This thread went quiet twenty minutes ago and it's ready to come back, so we call it back. Nobody decided that in advance. Everybody felt it at the same time.

[curious] So who decides when to edit?

[teaching] Whoever knows. And that's not a dodge — that's the actual skill. In a Harold, the form tells you. Three scenes, group game, back to the scenes. You can be a mediocre listener and still be in the right place, because the scaffold holds you. Pull the scaffold out and the only thing left holding the show together is the ensemble's shared sense of what this show is.

[contemplative] Group mind, but load-bearing.

[emphatic] Load-bearing. In a Harold, group mind is a nice bonus. In organic longform it's the entire architecture.

[curious] Where does this come from? Because I always heard Harold was Del Close's baby, and organic sounds like a rebellion against him.

[teaching] It's the opposite. It's a return. Close's original Harold in the sixties was loose — experimental, a container for whatever the ensemble was chasing. The three-by-three thing got codified later, in the eighties at iO, as a teaching tool. Training wheels. And Close never stopped saying it: the first rule is there are no rules. Content and the need to develop an organic commentary on the suggestion trump predetermined structures. He wrote that down. People kept doing the training wheels version anyway.

[curious] Because it's teachable.

[matter-of-fact] Because it's teachable. You can grade a Harold. You cannot grade a feeling.

[curious] So who picked up the thread?

[teaching] David Razowsky, mostly. Susan Messing. Joe Bill and Mark Sutton — the BASSPROV guys, two dudes on folding chairs fishing, that's the whole show, and it's structurally perfect because it's structurally nothing. And Razowsky brought in something from outside improv entirely — Viewpoints. Mary Overlie's dance work, by way of Anne Bogart and Tina Landau. Nine tenets of time and space.

[curious] Dance technique. In improv. How does that help you find a scene?

[teaching] Because it gives you navigation that isn't narrative. If you're not steering by plot — and organic longform, you're often not — you need something else to steer by. Tempo. Spatial relationship. Kinesthetic response. Where is my body in relation to yours, and what changes if I move three feet closer. That's information. It's just information most improvisers were never taught to read.

[contemplative] So instead of "what's the story," it's "what's happening in the room."

[warm] And "what's happening in the room" is always true. The story might not be there yet. The room always is.

[curious] What's the Razowsky move specifically?

[teaching] Emotional commitment. You don't start with a premise, you start with a feeling — an actual one, in your body — and you heighten it honestly. Not perform it bigger. Heighten it truthfully, which means you let it change. Grief curdles into anger. Anger collapses into embarrassment. That transformation is the plot. You didn't need a plot.

[curious] Okay. So then why does organic longform have a reputation for being kind of a slog?

[emphatic] Because most of the time it is, and we should say that out loud.

[short pause]

[serious] The UCB side of the argument is not stupid. Their position is: without a clear game, scenes meander. You get shows that are interesting and not funny. Beautiful, textured, well-listened-to, and the audience is politely waiting for it to end. And the counter-argument to "structure is constraint" is that structure is a sonnet. Fourteen lines and a rhyme scheme have produced better poems than free verse ever has, on average, and it's not close.

[curious] So is organic better or worse?

[matter-of-fact] It's neither. It's downstream of training. Here's the thing everyone misses: the performers who make organic longform sing are the ones who spent years in the three-by-three. Harold didn't get discarded, it got internalized. When a Family show or a Razowsky show returns to a thread at exactly the right moment, that's not intuition from nowhere — that's a thousand reps of a form that taught them what "the right moment" feels like.

[contemplative] Jazz pianist. Scales first, then improvise.

[emphatic] Scales first. Nobody's impressed by a pianist who never learned the scales and calls it freedom. And that's the failure mode, precisely — it isn't that untrained performers do organic longform badly. It's that they do a different thing entirely and use the same word for it. One is a structure you've absorbed so deeply you don't have to think about it. The other is no structure at all. From the outside, for about four minutes, they look identical.

[curious] How do you tell the difference?

[teaching] Watch what happens at minute twelve. The trained version starts converging — things come back, threads braid, the show reveals it's been about something. The untrained version keeps generating. New scene, new scene, new scene, all fine, none of them touching. Meandering isn't the absence of good scenes. It's the absence of return.

[contemplative] And that's true off stage too. The people who improvise well in a meeting, in a hard conversation — they're not winging it. They've just run the reps so many times the structure went invisible.

[warm] Right. Freedom that looks like nothing is almost always something, buried. And if you skip the burying, you don't get freedom.

[confident] You get a very long evening.
