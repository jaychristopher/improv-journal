[curious] Someone in a workshop asks "what kind of game was that?" and four people answer and none of the answers match. Is there an actual taxonomy, or are we all improvising the vocabulary too?

[teaching] There's no official document. UCB doesn't publish a list with these exact labels. But sit in 201 and 301 long enough and the same categories keep surfacing, and they're worth naming — because what they actually tell you is where the unusual thing is coming from.

[curious] The source, not the shape.

[teaching] The source. And the fastest way in is one question: is the pattern living in the character, in the relationship, or in the world?

[curious] Start with the character.

[teaching] Four flavors. Behavioral — a repeating tic. The guy who apologizes for everything, including a rainstorm. The pattern is the behavior itself. Emotional — the state doesn't match the situation. Serene during the plane crash, or completely destroyed because the sandwich came out lukewarm. The gap is the engine.

[curious] And the mouth and the body get their own categories?

[matter-of-fact] They do. Verbal — a speech pattern drives it. Everything comes out as a question. Or someone using corporate language for the least corporate thing in the world. [short pause] "Let's circle back on our feelings about Dad's funeral."

[curious] [warm] That's a whole person I've met.

[teaching] And physical — the body makes the pattern without help. Gestures wildly out of scale with the conversation. Someone who gets an inch shorter every time they're contradicted. That's distinct from behavioral because it's non-verbal at its root; you could watch it on mute and still get it.

[curious] Okay, so that's character. What lives in the relationship?

[teaching] Status, first. The power dynamic is the game — who has authority, and how it moves. The job interview where the candidate slowly, politely takes the room. Johnstone's entire status framework feeds this one.

[conversational] And that transaction is running in a real interview too. What you ask isn't information gathering — it's a status move. Ask "what does success look like in the first ninety days" and you've done something to the room that "what are the hours" doesn't do.

[curious] What's the other relational one?

[teaching] Mapping. Playing one reality through the lens of another. A breakup conducted as a hostage negotiation. Two people choosing a restaurant like it's a presidential debate. The comedy lives in the distance between the actual situation and the frame you've dropped on top of it — and the wider that gap, the harder it pays.

[curious] And the world?

[teaching] Scenic games. The space generates the pattern and the characters are just living in it. Every room they walk into is smaller than the last. Everything in the house is broken and no one comments. The office has one inexplicable feature everybody treats as completely normal.

[curious] So seven buckets. How do I actually use this without turning it into a checklist?

[emphatic] You don't use it during. That's the whole thing. Nobody has ever improved a scene by thinking "I should play a status game today." It's a lens for after — you sit down and ask, what kind of game was that? Did we play it? And did we heighten inside the category, or did we drift?

[curious] What does drift look like?

[teaching] You start with a verbal game — everything's a question — and three lines later you're doing a behavioral thing about how he can't open jars. It feels like motion. Nothing compounds. The audience can tell something's happening and can't tell what.

[curious] [contemplative] So the taxonomy is a post-mortem tool.

[matter-of-fact] Hines puts it about as cleanly as it gets: playing game is not the same as naming the game.

[curious] Meaning I can name it perfectly and still not be playing it.

[teaching] There are four cells and only one of them is comedy. Named and played — great. Played but never named — also great, that's most good improv. Neither — that's a scene, it happens. But named and not played is the painful one. Someone says out loud, "wow, you really do apologize for everything," and then the scene moves on to something else. They pointed at the door and didn't walk through it.

[curious] [short pause] So naming isn't necessary and it isn't sufficient.

[teaching] Which is exactly what makes it a lens and not a step. And there's a serious counter-position here — Napier rejects the categorizing outright. Do something fully, commit, and the pattern reveals itself. You don't need the label; the label is a crutch people use instead of committing.

[curious] Who's right?

[confident] Both, at different hours. Watch TJ and Dave — they're producing what a UCB teacher would happily call status games, and they would never in their lives name one mid-performance. The category is a thing you know, not a thing you're doing.

[warm] The taxonomy is for the walk home. On stage, you just apologize for the rain.
