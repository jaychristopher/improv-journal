[curious] Point of view. Every teacher says it, nobody defines it the same way twice. Give me the sharp version.

[teaching] It's the filter your character puts on everything. Not what they want, not what they do — what they see. A specific, emotionally loaded lens on reality, and it never comes off.

[curious] Why does that matter more than knowing their job, their name, what they had for breakfast?

[teaching] Because a strong lens generates the scene for you. Say your character believes everything is a competition. Now the shared meal is a contest. The walk in the park is a contest. Reading a bedtime story to a kid is a contest — who's better at bedtime. You didn't invent any of that.

[conversational] So the offers were already in the room. The lens just decides which ones you notice.

[teaching] Right. The world is full of offers. The filter picks. Character who sees danger everywhere — every creak in the house is loaded, every neighbor has a motive, every gift is a trap. You're not writing material. You're looking at the same room through something.

[curious] And a weak point of view?

[matter-of-fact] "Nice." "Normal." "A guy at work." Those filter nothing. [short pause] And here's what nobody says out loud — the work doesn't disappear when the lens is weak. It moves. It moves onto you. You're now generating every line from scratch, in real time, in front of people.

[emphatic] Which is the whole reframe. The point of view isn't extra homework you do before the scene. It's the same work, done by something other than you.

[curious] So is this just game? Because "everything is a competition" sounds like a pattern I'd play.

[teaching] They're cousins, not twins. Game is the pattern of unusual behavior. Point of view is often how the character produces that pattern. Hines puts it as a chain — a philosophy becomes a point of view, the point of view becomes a game, and then it's something you can do over and over. And once the game shows up, the character gets deeper because of the lens. The viewpoint is what separates the character from you.

[curious] Where do I get one? Because "decide your character's core belief" is not a thing I can do in two seconds at the top of a scene.

[teaching] You don't decide it. A strong emotion hands you one instantly. An unusual physical choice implies one. Hines's version is three words — know things, care about things, say things. And when you're drowning out there, give yourself a point of view you're good at. An attitude. An agenda. It's a rescue rope.

[contemplative] Here's where it gets interesting, though. This is a UCB idea. And the tradition with the strongest claim on character doesn't use it at all.

[curious] Johnstone.

[teaching] Johnstone. His question isn't what does this character believe. It's where does this character sit in the pecking order. And status isn't one attribute among several for him — it is the lens. It does exactly the job we've been assigning to point of view. Every interaction is a negotiation of position.

[curious] Is one of them just better?

[matter-of-fact] They ask you to produce different things, and that's the practical difference. A belief has to be invented before you can play it. "Everything is a competition" is a proposition. And a nervous performer will state a proposition rather than act from it — which is exactly why classes taught this way produce characters who announce their attitude in the first line.

[conversational] Whereas status you can just... do.

[teaching] Immediately. Physically. Take up more room. Hold the look a second longer. Stop apologizing. And if anybody needs a belief, it shows up afterward as a description of what your body already did.

[curious] So what does each one catch that the other doesn't?

[teaching] Point of view explains consistency. Why does this character react the same way to a shared meal, a funeral, and a parking ticket? That's the filter, and that's where material comes from. [short pause] Status explains effect. What are these two people doing to each other? And point of view doesn't touch that at all.

[curious] Meaning you could have two brilliant lenses and a dead scene.

[emphatic] Two vivid, specific, completely unrelated filters — and nothing either person does changes the other. Rich and totally static. Meanwhile a performer working only from status builds a real relationship and then notices the same seesaw showing up in every character they've ever played.

[contemplative] They're not describing different phenomena. They're pointing at different halves of the same exchange.

[serious] And the half you can see without effort is just whichever vocabulary you got taught first.
