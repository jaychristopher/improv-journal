[curious] There's a moment in a scene where you can feel the floor move. What is that, actually?

[teaching] That's the tilt. Johnstone's word. You've established a normal — two brothers, it's Sunday, the roast is in the oven — and then something breaks it. The tilt is the break. Everything after it is consequence.

[curious] Why does a scene need one? Sometimes those Sunday-roast scenes are lovely.

[matter-of-fact] They're pleasant. And the audience is patient about it, for maybe ninety seconds. They're waiting. They're agreeable, they're leaning in, because they assume something is coming. If it never comes, you did competent work that nobody remembers by the time they get to their car.

[curious] Okay, but "something happens" sounds big to me. A gun. An affair. A diagnosis.

[teaching] That's the trap. The tilt is almost always smaller than people expect. "Mum's coming." That's a tilt. One brother admitting he's already eaten — that's a tilt.

[curious] Those are nothing, though.

[emphatic] They're not big. They're irreversible. That's the actual test. Not scale — irreversibility. After it lands, the room cannot go back to what it was doing. You can't un-hear that Mum is coming. The roast is still in the oven, but the oven means something different now.

[contemplative] [pause] And that's the whole shift, right there. The situation became a story, and it took four words.

[curious] So what's the mistake people make?

[teaching] They tilt too early. Twenty seconds in, before there's a platform, someone pulls a gun or announces the affair. And now you've got this enormous event and no relationship to play it through.

[curious] Which is why those scenes get so loud so fast.

[confident] Exactly. They escalate because there's nowhere else to go. But here's the deeper problem — the audience never saw normal. They don't know what these people had. So they can't feel it being taken away. The tilt only hurts if we watched the thing it's breaking.

[curious] Is there an opposite failure?

[matter-of-fact] There is, and it's rarer and much easier to fix. A cast so devoted to base reality that nobody ever disturbs it. Beautiful specificity, real environment, everyone's got a job — and forty minutes of nothing happening.

[curious] Why does that happen? That's not fear of the scene, that's people doing the work.

[serious] Johnstone's version was blunt. Improvisers are afraid to let anything happen, because everything that happens creates obligations. If Mum is coming, now you have to deal with Mum. If your brother already ate, you have to be a person who's been lied to. So you keep chopping vegetables instead.

[contemplative] Which is also just — how people avoid conversations. Everything's fine, the roast is in the oven, let's not.

[teaching] Right. Nothing happens, so nothing is required of you.

[curious] Now — I want to ask about something that confuses me in mixed rooms. Is the tilt the same as the game?

[emphatic] No. And this is the cleanest place where the two big traditions split, so it's worth being precise.

[teaching] Johnstone's tilt asks: what happens next? The break causes something. That something causes something else. The shape of the piece is the chain — which is why the narrative tradition cares so much about reincorporation, about endings, about landing it.

[curious] And Chicago?

[teaching] The Chicago question is: what else is like this? The first unusual thing isn't a hinge, it's a pattern. You find it, you name it, you repeat it under increasing pressure. Game-of-the-scene work treats the ending as almost incidental — you're heightening until the edit comes.

[curious] So the same noticing.

[confident] The same noticing, opposite follow-through. One asks the unusual thing to cause something. The other asks it to recur.

[curious] Is one of them more advanced?

[matter-of-fact] Neither. They make different shows. But a performer trained hard in one will genuinely feel the other is doing it wrong — like watching someone find a beautiful thing and then just walk past it. Or find a beautiful thing and then flog it for eight minutes.

[curious] So what do I do if I don't know which room I'm in?

[warm] Notice the break either way. That's the shared skill. The moment something disturbs the normal, you've been handed a choice — follow the consequence, or find the pattern. Beginners aren't failing to choose. They're failing to notice, or noticing and quietly declining.

[teaching] The teachers in the game tradition will tell you the tilt is a story-writing idea smuggled into a form that doesn't need it. Build from behaviour, not events. And they've got a point.

[curious] What's the counter?

[confident] That a show made entirely of patterns, with no consequences, is exhausting to sit through for an hour. Which is why most long forms quietly borrow from both, whatever their teachers say out loud.

[contemplative] [short pause] The tilt isn't a plot device. It's the moment you agree to be changed by what just got said.
