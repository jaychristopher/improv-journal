[curious] I want to start with something you said the other day — that almost nobody blocks out loud.

[teaching] Right. Flat denial is rare. "We're in a hospital." "No we're not." That happens in week two of a level one class and then basically never again. It's obvious, it's easy to name, it's easy to fix. The refusals that actually kill scenes look like cooperation.

[curious] So they're hiding.

[teaching] They're hiding in plain sight. Johnstone catalogued five of them, and the reason he bothered to separate them out is that each one dodges a different obligation. Which means each one needs a different fix. Give the wrong note and you've corrected somebody for something they weren't doing.

[curious] Okay. Give me the five.

[matter-of-fact] Wimping. Accepting and adding nothing. "We're in a hospital." "We certainly are." No denial. Full cooperation. And the scene is exactly where it was.

[curious] That's the one that feels good in the moment. You said yes.

[teaching] You said yes to everything and you contributed nothing. What's being avoided there is having an opinion. And the fix is not "be more interesting" — it's add one thing that wasn't already true. That's it. It does not have to be good.

[curious] What's next?

[matter-of-fact] Cancelling. You make an offer and take it back in the same breath. "I've got a gun — no, I'm kidding, it's a lighter."

[conversational] Oh, I've done that one.

[warm] Everybody's done that one. You feel the size of what you just put in the room and you retract it before anyone has to deal with it. What's being avoided is consequences. And the fix is almost embarrassingly simple — let it stand for thirty seconds before you decide it was too much. [short pause] It usually wasn't.

[curious] Three?

[matter-of-fact] Bridging. Delaying the action with preparation. Two people who need to rob a bank spend six minutes discussing how they're going to rob the bank.

[curious] And that one really doesn't feel like avoidance. That feels like work.

[emphatic] That's exactly why it's so durable. It looks productive. You're building the plan, you're establishing the stakes, you're — stalling. The planning is the avoidance. What's being dodged is the actual event.

[curious] So what, skip the plan?

[teaching] Start after the decision. Walk in already doing the thing. The audience will fill in the meeting you had.

[contemplative] That one lives outside the room too, doesn't it.

[warm] It lives everywhere. The novel you're going to write once you've finished the outline. The conversation you'll have with your dad once you've figured out how to have it. Preparation is the most respectable way to not do something.

[curious] Alright, four.

[matter-of-fact] Hedging. Offers so vague they can't fail. "There's something weird about this place." "Something's happening to me."

[curious] I hear that constantly. It sounds atmospheric.

[teaching] It sounds like content and it's unfalsifiable. Nothing specific has been claimed, so there's nothing for your partner to catch. What's being avoided is being wrong. And the note is: name it. What's weird about this place. What's happening to you.

[curious] The specific version is riskier.

[confident] The specific version is riskier and it's the only one anyone can build on. Vagueness is safe because it can't be checked, and it can't be checked because it isn't saying anything.

[curious] And the fifth.

[matter-of-fact] Forcing your partner to do the creative work. "Why don't you show everyone your famous dance?" Johnstone called it pimping — a lot of teachers have retired the word and kept the observation. Now it usually gets taught as putting your partner on the spot.

[curious] And the offer costs the person making it nothing.

[emphatic] They've handed somebody else the bill. Which is why the fix is do it first, or do it with them. Dance, and then invite them.

[contemplative] So here's what I keep circling. These sound like five different mistakes but you've said they're not.

[teaching] They're not. Every one of them is a bandwidth-preserving move. Each one buys the performer a moment where they're not yet committed to anything — and that uncommitted state is exactly what a scene can't be built from. They're not five unrelated errors. They're five exits from the same room.

[curious] Which is the room where something is owed.

[warm] Something is owed, right now, and there are five doors out. One avoids having an opinion. One avoids a consequence. One avoids an event. One avoids being wrong. One avoids your own exposure.

[curious] And this is why "stop blocking" doesn't land.

[emphatic] It's why that note fails constantly. A performer who's hedging isn't refusing offers. They're making offers that can't be caught. Telling them to accept more is answering a question they didn't ask. They walk out of class thinking they're a blocker, and they'll spend the next month saying yes harder to a problem that was never about yes.

[curious] So how do you actually use this when you're watching a scene?

[teaching] You change the question. Instead of asking what they're doing wrong — which produces a list of symptoms — you ask what they're avoiding. And there are only five answers. An opinion, a consequence, an event, being wrong, or their own exposure.

[matter-of-fact] Five answers, five notes.

[confident] And the names matter less than the answers. Nobody needs to memorize Johnstone's vocabulary. What you need is to be able to look at a scene that's dying politely and know which door they went out.

[contemplative] Politely is the word, isn't it. None of these are hostile.

[warm] Not one. Every performer doing these is trying to be a good scene partner. That's what makes them so hard to see — the refusal is wearing cooperation as a costume.
