[curious] Okay, the Alphabet Game. Every line starts with the next letter. Audience calls a letter, you go twenty-six lines and you're done. Everybody's played it. What I want to know is why we keep playing it.

[teaching] That's the right question, because most people play it for the wrong reason. They play it as a warm-up. It's not a warm-up. It's a load test.

[curious] Meaning what, exactly?

[teaching] Meaning it doesn't teach you anything. It measures you. The letter constraint sits there eating a chunk of your attention continuously — you never get to put it down. And when your capacity runs short, you drop something. The whole point of the game is finding out what you drop.

[short pause]

[conversational] And here's the thing. You don't get to choose.

[curious] So what do people drop?

[teaching] Three flavours, mostly. Some people stop listening. Their lines are grammatical, they start with the right letter, and they have nothing to do with what their partner just said. They're not in a scene anymore, they're taking turns making sentences.

[curious] Oh, I've watched that happen. It looks fine for about eight letters and then you realise nobody's been home for a while.

[teaching] Right. Second flavour — the character evaporates. They're still listening, still responding, but the specific person they walked out as is gone. Now it's just an improviser saying reasonable things. The voice flattens out, the point of view goes, and you're watching someone's neutral.

[curious] And the third?

[teaching] The third holds the scene and misses letters. Stays connected, stays somebody, skips from L to N and doesn't notice.

[warm] And that third one is by far the healthiest failure, which is not what people assume.

[curious] Why is that the healthiest?

[teaching] Because it tells you what the load-bearing wall is. Under pressure, everybody keeps one thing and sheds the rest. If the thing you keep is your partner and your character, and the thing you shed is the puzzle — your improv is built on the right foundation. The puzzle is the least important thing in the room and your instincts know it.

[matter-of-fact] If the thing you keep is the alphabet, and what you shed is your scene partner — that's information too. Less comfortable information.

[curious] And most people would guess wrong about themselves.

[emphatic] Almost everyone guesses wrong. Ask a room of improvisers what they'd lose first and they'll all say "oh, I'd probably miss letters." Then you run it and half of them go completely deaf at about letter six. You cannot introspect your way to this answer. You have to see it.

[short pause]

[contemplative] Which is the actual value of the game, and it's a one-time value per person, roughly.

[curious] Say more about that. Because plenty of groups run this weekly.

[teaching] And those groups are getting better at the alphabet. Not at improv.

[curious] That's a strong claim.

[teaching] It follows from what the game is, though. Think about what searching for a Q-word requires. You go inside your own head and look. That's internal computation — attending to your own mental process instead of to your partner. And reducing that is basically what all improv training is for. Every exercise about listening, about following the follower, about getting your attention out of your skull and into the room.

[emphatic] This game requires the opposite. You cannot start a line with Q without going and looking for a Q.

[curious] So running it regularly trains the exact habit it's designed to expose.

[teaching] You get smoother, and the smoothness is real, but it's the smoothness of someone who has pre-loaded a list. You've got your Q, your X, your Z sitting in a drawer ready to go. That's not scene work getting better. That's a lookup table getting faster.

[warm] Play it to find something out. Then go and work on whatever it found. If it found that you stop listening under pressure, the fix isn't more alphabet — it's twenty minutes of exercises where listening is the only thing you have to do.

[curious] Alright. Where does it go wrong in performance? Because it also fails as a piece of theatre in specific ways.

[matter-of-fact] Three, and they're all avoidable.

[teaching] First — announcing the letter. "Q... quite a nice day, isn't it." You can hear the seam. The performer has solved the puzzle and then bolted a sentence onto the solution, and the audience watches the bolt go in. The letter should arrive inside a sentence a human being would actually say. Nobody should be able to tell where the constraint is.

[curious] The audience knows the rule, though. They're tracking it.

[teaching] They're tracking it, but there's a difference between watching someone play a game well and watching someone solve for you. The pleasure is in the constraint disappearing into the scene. If I can see you reaching, the scene stops and the crossword starts.

[curious] Second?

[emphatic] Stopping when somebody slips. Missing a letter is not a failure condition. Someone goes L, N, everyone hears it, and the temptation is enormous — break, laugh, acknowledge, restart.

[teaching] Carrying on as though nothing happened is closer to the point of the entire form than getting it right. The whole art is: something went wrong, and we absorbed it, and the world stayed real. If you break for a missed letter, you've told the audience the puzzle mattered more than the scene. Which is exactly the priority you just spent an hour trying to unlearn.

[curious] And the third one is structural, right? Not about performing at all.

[matter-of-fact] Starting on X. An audience will absolutely suggest it, because it sounds funny to suggest, and it makes a worse game every time.

[curious] Because X, Y, Z land immediately.

[teaching] Three of the hardest letters in the first thirty seconds, before there's a scene to hold on to. You've got no relationship, no location, no want — nothing to lean on. So the players have no choice but to solve, and the audience watches two people do vocabulary for the opening beat.

[warm] Start on D and those same letters land past the halfway mark, when there's a whole scene built up to carry them. Same difficulty, completely different experience, because by then the scene is doing the work and the letters just ride along.

[contemplative] It's worth sitting with that. The suggestion the audience most enjoys making is the one that decides the game before anyone's said a word. They're not sabotaging you. They just don't know that the fun part needs thirty seconds of scene first.

[matter-of-fact] So take the letter. Just don't hand them the pen.
