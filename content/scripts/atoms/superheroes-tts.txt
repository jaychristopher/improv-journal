[curious] Superheroes. Everybody's played it, nobody's quite sure what it's for. What is it actually training?

[teaching] Endowment. In its most exposed form. You're offstage, someone onstage is in trouble, they yell for help — and in the half second before you arrive, they decide who you are. Captain Mild Disappointment. The Human Tuesday. You didn't pick that. You walk into it.

[curious] And there's no negotiating it.

[matter-of-fact] None. That's the whole design. In a normal scene, if your partner endows you with something you don't love, you've got moves. You can soften it, redirect it, absorb it slowly over three lines. Here you've got a name, an entrance, and a room full of people already looking at you. The only available play is total acceptance.

[teaching] Which is the skill. Not "can you accept an endowment" — everyone says yes to that in a workshop. It's "can you accept one you had no hand in, immediately, and then find out what it implies."

[curious] Find out what it implies. Say more.

[warm] Okay. You're Captain Mild Disappointment. The bad version is you walk in and say "I am mildly disappointed" and everybody laughs and that's it, you're done, you have nowhere to go. The good version — you walk in, and you're a person whose power is that everyone around them feels a small, deflating letdown. That has consequences. That person has a marriage. That person has been fired. That person has learned to enter rooms carefully.

[emphatic] Right — the question is never "what does the name mean," it's "who is somebody with that power?" How has it shaped their life?

[teaching] And once you've got that, the crisis becomes interesting, because now you're not a joke walking in — you're a specific human being with a specific problem trying to help.

[curious] So that's failure mode one: playing the name instead of a person.

[matter-of-fact] Playing the name is a joke. Playing the person is a character. The joke lasts four seconds and then you're standing there.

[curious] What's the second one?

[serious] Names that are already the whole joke. This one's subtler because it looks like a gift. Someone names you The Reverse Bar Mitzvah Guy Who Only Speaks In Yelp Reviews — and it gets a big laugh on the naming — and then you've got nothing. The joke's been told. Your only option is to repeat it.

[teaching] Whereas a name that suggests a personality — the generous kind — hands you material. Captain Almost. Mister Sincere. Those aren't funny by themselves. That's the point. They're funny because of what you build.

[warm] Generous names leave room. Punchline names take the room with them.

[curious] Third failure?

[matter-of-fact] Solving the crisis too fast. Every hero who arrives should make it somewhat worse. If your second arrival fixes everything, you now have three players standing in the back with nothing to do and a scene with no engine.

[conversational] The comedy is in escalation. Each person tries to help, and their particular gift is exactly wrong for the situation. That's the machine. Help that complicates.

[curious] [pause] Now — the part I've never seen anybody coach. The reverse exit. Why does that matter? It always felt like a formality to me.

[emphatic] It's the most undervalued thing in the game, and it's the reason it's more than a naming contest.

[curious] Go on.

[teaching] Leaving in reverse order means the last person to go is the first person who was there. The original hero. And by the time they leave, four other people have piled complications onto a crisis that started simple. That hero has to walk out and make sense of all of it. Retroactively. Out loud. In one beat.

[contemplative] That's justification. Real justification, not the workshop version where you justify one weird thing you said. You're justifying a whole accumulated mess that other people built.

[warm] And it's a genuine narrative skill. The ability to look at chaos that has already happened and say the thing that makes it a story instead of a pile — that's what good improvisers do at the end of every longform.

[curious] So the exits are the writing.

[matter-of-fact] The exits are the writing. The entrances are the casting.

[curious] [pause] Okay. Let's do the honest objection, because I think there is one.

[serious] There is. The laughs live in the naming. Which means the game quietly rewards the person doing the naming, not the person doing the playing.

[curious] Huh. Which inverts the exact ethic it appears to teach.

[emphatic] Completely. On paper this is the most ensemble-minded game there is — I define you, you define the next person, we're all holding each other. In practice, watch a group play it twice. The names get longer. More elaborate. More architecturally clever. And the characters get thinner. Every time.

[curious] Because everyone's worked out where the applause is.

[matter-of-fact] Everybody knows where the applause is within one round. And they'll go there. That's not a character flaw, that's just how attention works.

[teaching] It's performing cleverness with a really good disguise. Because it looks like generosity. It looks like you're giving your partner a gift. You're not — you're taking your turn at the microphone and handing them the cleanup.

[curious] So what's the fix? Do you change the game?

[confident] No. It's a coaching note, not a rule change. Two parts. First: name your partner in three words or fewer. That's it. Three words. It kills the elaborate-name arms race at the source, because a three-word name can't contain a whole joke — it can only contain a suggestion.

[curious] And the second part?

[emphatic] Judge the round on what they did with it. Out loud. Give the note to the player who took a nothing name and built a person out of it, not the one who got the biggest laugh on the handoff.

[contemplative] [short pause] And this is where it stops being about the game, honestly. Because the same thing is true of every room you've ever been in. Somebody names you fast — the funny one, the difficult one, the one who's good with details. You didn't pick it, and you were not consulted.

[warm] And the move is the same one. You don't argue with the name. You find out who a person with that name actually is, and you play them all the way to the end.
