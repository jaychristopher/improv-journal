[curious] So there's this thing improvisers pass around — eight sentences that supposedly guarantee you a story. That can't actually work, can it?

[teaching] It does. Consistently. You put eight people in a circle, give each of them one stem, tell them no planning and no do-overs, and you get a real story on the first attempt almost every time. It's a little unsettling the first time you watch it happen.

[curious] Give me the eight.

[matter-of-fact] Once upon a time. Every day. But one day. Because of that. Because of that. Because of that. Until finally. And ever since that day.

[curious] That's it. That's the whole technology.

[teaching] That's the whole thing. Kenn Adams built it for teaching long-form, and it has since escaped improv entirely — it's in classrooms, it's in pitch training, and a near-identical version of it is famously associated with Pixar.

[curious] Okay, but I want to know why it works, because on paper it looks like a fill-in-the-blank worksheet.

[teaching] Look at what each section is actually doing. The first two — once upon a time, every day — those are base reality. That's you establishing a normal. And nobody wants to do them, because they're boring. There's no joke in "every day."

[emphatic] But if you skip them, "but one day" has nothing to interrupt. You've broken a world you never built. That's the same failure as walking into a scene and immediately being weird — unusual only registers against usual.

[curious] So the boring part is load-bearing.

[matter-of-fact] The boring part is the whole reason the exciting part reads as exciting.

[curious] What about the three "because of that" steps? That's where I always feel the group get impatient.

[teaching] That's the part people cut, and it's the part that makes it a story instead of a list. Try this — swap "because of that" for "and then." Same events, same order. And then, and then, and then.

[short pause]

[contemplative] It dies. Instantly. It becomes a sequence of things that happened.

[curious] Because nothing is causing anything.

[emphatic] Right. "Because of that" is a load-bearing phrase. It forces every beat to be the child of the beat before it. You can't jump to the fun idea you've been sitting on — you have to take what the last sentence actually implies. Which is the obvious choice, wearing a narrative costume.

[curious] And that's the discipline in the circle. Take the obvious thing, not the clever thing.

[teaching] The person who tries to be clever at step five is the person who kills the story. Everyone downstream of them now has to build on something that came from nowhere.

[curious] Then "until finally."

[teaching] Which only lands if the consequences escalated. If your three middles were all the same size, "until finally" is just the fourth one. So the spine is secretly a heightening exercise — each because-of-that has to cost more than the last.

[curious] And the eighth one? The one Pixar's version drops?

[warm] "And ever since that day." That's reincorporation. The ending has to speak to the beginning. You have to say what's different now about the world you set up in stem one — and if you can't, the audience feels it. They know the story didn't close, even if they couldn't tell you why.

[curious] Say more about the Pixar thing, because that's how most people encounter this.

[matter-of-fact] A version circulated in 2011 as one of Emma Coats's story rules, collected while she was a story artist there. It's Adams's spine with the last stem removed. Worth knowing which direction the borrowing runs — this came out of improv pedagogy and reached a wider audience through the association, not the other way around.

[curious] How does it fail? Because I've seen it fail.

[serious] Three ways. First — using it as a script. The spine is a scaffold for finding a story, not a form to fill in. The moment an improviser decides their "until finally" in advance, they stop listening for the one the middle actually earned. They're now driving toward an ending the story didn't ask for.

[curious] Which is the same sin as planning a punchline in a scene.

[teaching] Identical. Second failure — applying it to a scene. It's a story structure, not a scene structure. A two-person scene doesn't need eight beats and it will absolutely die if you march it through them.

[curious] And the third?

[matter-of-fact] Too many "because of that" steps. Three is the number the shape wants. Add more and the climax arrives so late it doesn't feel caused by anything in particular — the chain gets long enough that the audience loses the thread back to the beginning.

[curious] The two rules you mentioned — nobody skips a stem, nobody adds a second sentence. Why so rigid?

[warm] Because the constraint is the teacher. The rules do the work you'd otherwise have to do with notes. Someone who wants to explain their beat, elaborate, cover for it — they get one sentence. That's it. And what a new room discovers is that they can do this. Together. Without discussing it. That's a surprisingly useful thing for a group to learn about itself in eight minutes.

[curious] Does it follow you off stage?

[contemplative] It ruins bad presentations for you, permanently. Once you've felt the difference between "because of that" and "and then," you can hear which one someone is doing. Most status updates are and-then. Most pitches that don't land are a list of things that are true, in order, with no causation between them.

[short pause]

[warm] And it works on your own life, in a slightly uncomfortable way. Most of us tell our story as every day, every day, every day — and then something happened. The spine asks a harder question: what did that thing cause. Then what did that cause. And then it makes you finish the sentence that starts with "and ever since that day."

[contemplative] A lot of people find they can't finish it yet. Which is its own kind of information.
