[curious] Okay, sound ball. Circle, imaginary ball, you make a noise when you throw it. Four minutes, everyone's warm. What's actually being trained there?

[teaching] Almost nobody's answer to that is right, which is what makes it worth doing. People think it's the throwing. It isn't. Throwing is the easy half. The exercise lives entirely in the catch.

[curious] Because catching means what — making the sound back?

[teaching] Making the sound *you were given*. Not the sound you would have preferred. And that gap — between what you got and what you wanted — is where basically everyone fails.

[conversational] Watch a circle for two minutes. Someone throws a flat little bip. Nothing special, kind of a dud. And the person catching it returns something richer. Funnier. A bip with a bit more shape on it.

[curious] Which feels generous, honestly. They're punching it up.

[emphatic] That's exactly how they'd describe it. They're helping. Improving the material. [short pause] What they're actually doing is declining the offer and substituting their own — in about half a second, in front of everybody.

[contemplative] That's the whole of accepting the offer, compressed. Made visible.

[teaching] And that's the point of the exercise. It makes an offer small enough that refusing one has nowhere to hide.

[curious] Say more about the hiding.

[teaching] In a scene, someone who overrides what you established looks like a good player having better ideas. They're charming, the audience laughs, the scene goes somewhere. You can't prove the block. It's buried under two minutes of dialogue and a costume choice.

[matter-of-fact] Here the original is two seconds old and everybody heard it. There's no reading of it where the substitution isn't a substitution.

[warm] Which is why this works on people who've been told they block and genuinely don't believe it. You can argue with a note. You can't argue with a circle of eleven people who all heard the bip.

[curious] What's the throwing half doing, then? If it's the easy part.

[teaching] Something smaller but real — producing a sound with no time to evaluate it. Hesitation in a circle is very legible. Someone pauses, and you can watch them audition options behind their eyes.

[matter-of-fact] And the pause is more conspicuous than any sound could have been. Nobody remembers the boring noise. Everybody remembers the three seconds of nothing.

[contemplative] There's a whole life in that sentence. The thing you were protecting yourself from was less visible than the protecting.

[warm] It is. But start from the circle.

[curious] So how do you run it? Because I can imagine watching a lot of substitution happen and not knowing when to say anything.

[teaching] The whole exercise turns on one correction, and the timing matters more than the wording. You call it while the ball is moving. You don't stop the circle to explain.

[curious] Why not? Stopping seems clearer.

[emphatic] Because stopping hands everybody time to deliberate. That's the one resource you're trying to take away. You halt the round to explain the note, and now eleven people are standing there thinking about their next sound. You've just built the problem you were fixing.

[teaching] So: *same sound.* That's the note. Say it flatly, to nobody in particular, and let the circle carry on. You'll say it a lot.

[curious] And for the people who think they're being generous?

[teaching] *Don't improve it.* Same note, different door. That's the version that lands on someone who's been helping.

[conversational] Then *faster* — and that one's not a stylistic preference, it's the mechanism. A round slow enough to think in is a round that trains nothing.

[curious] What else drifts?

[matter-of-fact] Circles start throwing at the floor within about a minute. So: *look at who you're throwing to.* Once the throw stops being aimed at a person, the catching stops being an obligation. It becomes ambient noise everyone's making near each other.

[contemplative] Which is a fairly complete description of a bad meeting.

[warm] It is, yeah.

[curious] Variants — what's worth reaching for?

[teaching] Adding a movement is the first one I'd add. Sound *and* gesture together. And the reason it's good is specific: you can talk yourself into believing a sound was the same. You cannot do that with a gesture your body just refused to copy.

[curious] The body doesn't let you lie about it.

[confident] Right. The self-deception has nowhere to go.

[teaching] Two balls in the circle is the next one, and that's a real test, not a warm-up anymore. It splits attention. Most groups discover they'd only been tracking the person directly opposite them the whole time — the second ball removes that option.

[conversational] Adding names is the quiet one. Say the person's name as you throw. Slows the round a touch, but it fixes the throwing-into-the-void problem better than side-coaching ever does. Good with a group that doesn't know each other yet — it's doing two jobs at once.

[curious] Anything to avoid?

[serious] Anything competitive. There's an elimination version floating around and it turns a warm-up into an audition. That's precisely the failure this exercise was picked to avoid.

[teaching] Same category: don't let it get clever. The moment the circle starts competing on funny noises, it's become a performance and stopped being an exercise. [short pause] Boring sounds are correct. That's not a consolation, it's the standard.

[curious] And afterwards?

[emphatic] Say what you saw. Name who improved the sounds they were given. That's where the value actually is.

[teaching] Because nobody notices that about themselves in the moment — you're moving too fast, you're just making noises. But describe it afterwards and everyone recognizes it instantly. There's usually a beat of silence, and then somebody says *oh.*

[contemplative] Four minutes for a thing they'll now see themselves doing in every scene for the rest of the month.
