[curious] Okay, premise. Everyone uses the word. I'm not sure everyone means the same thing by it.

[teaching] Here's the tight version. The premise is the situation your scene is in before anything unusual has happened to it. Two people in an elevator. A daughter visiting her father. Someone returning a toaster to a store. That's it. It's static — it describes an arrangement, not a behaviour.

[curious] And that's not a scene yet.

[matter-of-fact] That's not a scene yet. It's the room the scene will happen in.

[curious] So why does it feel like the most important decision you make?

[teaching] Because you make it first, and first feels load-bearing. Give somebody thirty seconds to think before they step out, and watch what they reach for. A heist. An alien. A funeral for a goldfish. The assumption underneath is that a more interesting setup produces a more interesting scene.

[emphatic] It does the opposite. Reliably.

[curious] Why reliably? That's a strong word.

[teaching] Because an elaborate premise arrives with obligations. It has to be explained. It has to be sustained. And it usually has to be resolved. So you spend the whole scene executing a decision you made before the scene started, instead of finding what's actually in front of you.

[short pause]

[contemplative] You're doing homework on stage. And homework doesn't watch well.

[curious] Whereas the boring one —

[warm] Whereas two people doing the washing up can go absolutely anywhere. Nothing's been promised. There's no debt to pay off. Every offer is free to become the thing the scene is about.

[curious] So the premise is the setup and the game is the pattern that emerges inside it. I know that distinction.

[teaching] Right, but here's the part that gets skipped. The relationship isn't symmetrical. A strong game rescues a dull premise every single time. A strong premise rescues nothing.

[short pause]

[emphatic] Nothing. There is no setup so good it survives an absence of pattern. You've watched that scene. The alien lands, the audience leans in, and then eleven minutes of nobody finding anything.

[curious] Okay. Can I push on the word "premise" itself? It sounds like a decision. Something you pick.

[matter-of-fact] It mostly isn't. In practice a premise accumulates. Somebody makes an initiation, the other person adds a fact, and by the third line the situation exists without anybody having chosen it.

[curious] Nobody selected it.

[warm] Nobody selected it. And that's the healthy case, not the failure case. That's just base reality assembling itself in real time.

[curious] And when you genuinely do choose one in advance?

[teaching] Then it usually comes from a suggestion, or from a form that demands one. And the move is: make it small. Here's the test I like. Could you describe your premise without using the words "and then"?

[short pause]

[curious] "A daughter visits her father." Passes.

[matter-of-fact] Passes. "A daughter visits her father and then discovers he's been running a counterfeiting operation out of the garage" — that's not a premise, that's an outline. You've written the second half of a scene you haven't started.

[curious] But some forms treat the premise like it matters a lot. Short-form games, for one.

[teaching] They do, and this is the useful carve-out. Short-form announces its premise. The audience is told the rule, and watching performers work inside that constraint is the entertainment. Same with genre work — you establish a world, and the conventions of that world are the material. The premise there is doing real structural work.

[curious] So what separates those from the elevator?

[emphatic] Whether the audience has been told.

[short pause]

[teaching] When they've been told, the premise is a frame. It should be strong — they're watching it, they're measuring you against it. When they haven't been told, it's scaffolding. It should be light enough to forget. Light enough that it disappears the moment something more interesting shows up.

[contemplative] Same scene inside both. The choice isn't about the material — it's about the audience.

[curious] Let me take the other side for a second, because I think there's a way to over-learn this.

[warm] Please. There absolutely is.

[curious] Sketch comedy is premise-driven. Sketch has to arrive already knowing what it's about — there's no time to discover anything.

[teaching] Correct, and writers coming into improv over-value the premise for exactly that reason. Their whole craft is front-loaded. But the failure mode runs the other direction too, and I see it more often in intermediate players.

[curious] Someone who's been told premises don't matter.

[matter-of-fact] And responds by refusing to establish anything at all. Two people, undefined space, undefined relationship, nothing specified, everyone waiting for the game to show up. Napier's warning about searching for the game — this is that, in reverse. No game can form in a vacuum either. A pattern needs something to be a pattern *of*.

[short pause]

[contemplative] Thin isn't the same as empty. The washing up is thin. It's also a kitchen, a shared task, and two people standing close enough to talk. That's plenty of surface.

[curious] So the target is —

[teaching] Enough situation that offers have somewhere to land. Not so much that you've already promised the audience something you now have to go deliver.

[warm] And honestly, this is the note I'd give about most decisions made under pressure. The impressive-sounding choice usually costs you your freedom to respond. The plain one keeps it.
