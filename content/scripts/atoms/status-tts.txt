[curious] Status. Everyone in improv knows the word. But I want it named precisely, because I think people use it to mean three different things.

[teaching] Here's the precise version: status is the relative social position a character occupies, expressed through behavior. Not title. Not rank. Behavior. A king can play low — nervous, deferential, small. A servant can play high — confident, expansive, still.

[curious] So it's not who you are.

[teaching] It's what you do. And it's readable. Sustained eye contact, stillness, economy of movement, slow speech, taking up space, comfortable silence, not reacting when someone pokes you — that's high. Breaking eye contact, fidgeting, fast speech, qualifying everything you say, shrinking, filling every silence, over-reacting — that's low.

[matter-of-fact] Watch someone walk into a room. You've already read them before they open their mouth.

[curious] Okay, but that's just body language. Sociologists have known that forever.

[emphatic] Right, and that's the part people miss. Johnstone's actual insight isn't that people signal status. It's that status is complementary. When one person raises, the other feels pressure to lower. When one drops, the other rises to fill the space.

[contemplative] Which means status doesn't live inside either player. It lives between them. It's a property of the relationship. That's what makes it theatrical instead of just observational.

[curious] So a scene is a negotiation.

[teaching] Every scene. Usually implicit. And scenes get good when it moves — the confident one cracks, the nervous one finds their ground. That's the seesaw doing its work.

[serious] And here's the corollary nobody says out loud: refusing to accept a status shift is a form of blocking. Your scene partner lowers, offers you the high position, and you stay exactly where you were. Nothing moved. That's a denial. It just doesn't look like one because nobody said "no."

[curious] What about when the seesaw doesn't seesaw? When you're both high?

[teaching] That's its own dynamic, and it's not an edge case. Two people both playing high generates competition — that's most great comedy. Two people vying for control of a situation neither will yield.

[warm] Both low generates something else entirely. Indecision. Pathos. Two people in a room who both need someone else to decide, and nobody will. [short pause] You've been in that scene. You've probably been in that meeting.

[curious] Give me one concrete example. Something I can see.

[teaching] Restaurant scene. Waiter and customer. The taught version is the customer plays high and the waiter plays low — fine, it works, it's stable. Now play them both high. The customer is expansive and slow and certain. The waiter is also expansive and slow and certain. Neither breaks eye contact. Nobody's apologizing.

[confident] The scene is instantly about who gets to be right about the soup. And it's funny before anyone writes a joke, because the structure is doing the work.

[curious] Here's what I actually want to ask. Does everyone have a default?

[teaching] Johnstone's claim is yes. Most people have a habitual status they run regardless of context. And becoming aware of yours — then learning to play the opposite — is one of the most transformative things improv can do to a person.

[contemplative] Because yielding your default power position takes courage. If you're a habitual high, playing low feels like exposure. If you're a habitual low, playing high feels like arrogance you haven't earned.

[curious] Is there a version of that default that follows you off stage?

[serious] A permanently low default has a name outside the theatre. It's people pleasing. Lowering on every single transaction regardless of what the transaction actually called for. Not reading the room — just conceding to it, automatically, forever.

[matter-of-fact] And there's a high-status trap that's less obvious. Reaching for complexity to seem smart. Performing cleverness. Using irony as a shield.

[curious] Irony reads as high, though.

[emphatic] It reads as intellectual superiority and it isolates you from the scene. Same with overcomplication — reaching for the sophisticated word actually reads as reaching, which is low. Both moves trade signal clarity for perceived status. And in a scene, clarity is the thing you cannot afford to spend.

[curious] Last thing. Is this vocabulary universal?

[matter-of-fact] No, and this is worth knowing because it explains something confusing. The UCB manual has no chapter on status. Napier's Improvise doesn't center it either. The American longform tradition largely replaced Johnstone's status lens with game mechanics — point of view, pattern, heightening.

[teaching] So a performer trained entirely in that lineage has a complete, functional diagnostic vocabulary that just doesn't contain this one. They can be excellent and never notice the gap. Johnstone sees status as the engine of all interaction. UCB sees game as the engine of comedy.

[contemplative] They're not describing different scenes. They're describing the same scene with different instruments — and each instrument shows you something the other one can't see.
