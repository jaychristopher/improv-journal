[curious] There's a book everybody in improv is descended from and almost nobody has actually read. What's the deal with Spolin's handbook?

[teaching] The subtitle tells you everything. "A Handbook of Teaching and Directing Techniques." It's not a book of ideas about improvisation. It's a book of about two hundred numbered games, each one laid out in the same format, and that format is the actual contribution.

[curious] What's the format?

[teaching] Four parts, every time. The game itself. The Point of Concentration — the one thing players are supposed to attend to. The side-coaching lines a teacher calls out while it's running. And the evaluation questions to ask after.

[matter-of-fact] And that structure is an argument. It says you don't explain improvisation to somebody. You give them a problem narrow enough that solving it requires the behaviour you wanted, and then you shut up while they solve it.

[curious] So the theory is in the shape of the thing, not in the prose.

[confident] Mostly. There are opening chapters — spontaneity, the Approval/Disapproval Syndrome, transformation of the object — and they're much shorter than their influence suggests. People quote those chapters constantly. They're maybe a fifth of the book.

[curious] Okay, the games. How are they organised? Beginner to advanced?

[teaching] No. And this is the thing that makes it infuriating to read and useful for sixty years. They're sorted by the problem they solve. A chapter is a diagnosis.

[short pause]

[teaching] The ones that get used most are Where, Who and What. Place, relationship, activity — the three questions she treats as the substance of any scene. That's the direct ancestor of what you know as base reality, or CROW, or whatever your school calls the checklist.

[curious] Are they equally weighted?

[emphatic] Not remotely. The Where chapter is far longer than the other two, and that's on purpose. She thought place was the concrete thing an actor could physically engage with — that if you actually handled the door, the relationship and the activity would follow. Not the other way round.

[contemplative] Which is a real claim, and it's the opposite of how a lot of people coach now. We tend to say find the relationship first. She'd say the relationship shows up once your hands are busy.

[curious] What else is in there?

[matter-of-fact] Chapters on sensory awareness, on being part of a whole, on emotion, on speech, on character, on developing story. Then separate material on rehearsal and on working with children. If you're a teacher with a specific problem in the room, you can go to the relevant chapter and find eight games aimed at it.

[curious] So how do you actually use it? Because I've picked it up and bounced.

[warm] Everybody bounces. Nobody who gets value from this book reads it front to back — the people who try are exactly the ones who report it's impenetrable.

[teaching] Work backwards from a problem instead. Group's talking instead of doing? Where games. Group's performing at each other instead of with each other? The part-of-a-whole chapter. Group's going blank, nothing coming? Sensory work.

[emphatic] Then take one game, run it, and use the evaluation questions printed with it. Don't invent your own. Those questions are load-bearing and they're the part teachers skip most.

[curious] Why load-bearing?

[teaching] Because the evaluation is structurally about the problem, not the person. "Did the players solve the problem?" not "was that good?" Once you improvise your own questions you drift straight back to judgement, and the whole apparatus was built to keep judgement out of the room.

[curious] Anything else worth reading straight through?

[confident] The glossary. Genuinely. Her vocabulary is precise and it is not what those words mean in ordinary English. Half the misreadings of her method come from taking "transformation" or "point of concentration" in their everyday senses.

[curious] Say more on point of concentration — that phrase gets thrown around a lot.

[teaching] It's her device for getting a player out of their own head by giving the attention somewhere specific to go. A physical object, a task, a sensory quality. Something outside you. And then self-consciousness has nothing to work on, because you're busy.

[contemplative] The exercise supplies the focus, and the behaviour follows from attending to it — rather than from you deciding how to behave. That's why her games produce spontaneity without ever instructing anyone to be spontaneous.

[warm] Telling somebody to relax basically never works. Giving them something to concentrate on does. That's true at a party, in a job interview, on a first date. The advice "just be yourself" is useless. "Go find out what that person cares about" works immediately.

[curious] So who is this book actually for?

[matter-of-fact] Teachers and directors get an entire curriculum out of it. You can open it, take a Point of Concentration, and have a session that works. That's why it's been in print for sixty years and sold well over a hundred thousand copies in its first two editions.

[serious] Performers find it harder going, and it's fair to say so. It's written to be worked from, not read. The prose is of its period and it can be forbidding. And the games are indexed by the problem they solve, not by anything you'd think to look up.

[warm] If you bounced off it, you didn't miss the point. You used a reference book as a narrative one.

[curious] What's not in it?

[emphatic] Anything about improvisation as a performance form. No long form. No account of scenes building into a show. Nothing about handling an audience. None of that existed in a settled shape in 1963, and she wasn't trying to invent it.

[teaching] The games are training for theatre. Rehearsal technique. The thing they trained people to do — go out and improvise in front of a paying room, as the show — is what her son and his colleagues did with them afterwards.

[contemplative] So if you come to this looking for the origin of modern improv performance, you'll find the origin of its exercises and none of its forms. That gap is precisely why Chicago had to build the rest.

[curious] Which edition?

[matter-of-fact] The third, from 1999. Revised, better index, and coedited by Paul Sills — her son, founding director of Second City. Which makes the lineage from this book to Chicago improv a literal family one. Earlier printings are the same games with less apparatus.

[teaching] But honestly, for most people one of the spin-offs is the better purchase. She later broke the material out into more usable formats. The one that puts the games on cards is what a teacher actually wants in a room. There are also separate volumes for rehearsal and for the classroom, each taking the subset relevant to that one job.

[confident] The handbook stays the complete source. The spin-offs are the ones that get opened.

[curious] Last thing — she didn't come out of the theatre at all, did she?

[teaching] No. She learned this in the 1920s from Neva Boyd at Hull House in Chicago, where games were being used as social work. The techniques were designed to draw out people who weren't performers and had no intention of ever becoming any.

[warm] Which explains the whole tone of the book. It reads as though it was written for teachers because it was.
