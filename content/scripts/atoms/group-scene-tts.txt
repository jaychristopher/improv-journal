[curious] Okay, "group scene." Three or more people. Why does that need its own name? It's just a scene with more bodies.

[teaching] Because the moment you add a third person, the job changes. Two-person scene, you're building a relationship. Who are we to each other, what just shifted between us. That's the whole engine. Add a third body and that engine doesn't scale — now you're managing where the audience is looking.

[curious] Focus.

[teaching] Focus. That's the discipline. At any second in a group scene, the audience should know exactly where to look, and there are only three legal answers. Shared focus — everyone doing the same thing at once. Chanting, moving as one, the whole group reacting identically. Single focus — one person's got the spotlight, everyone else is framing them. Or shifting focus — it passes, player to player, like a round of examples.

[curious] And the illegal answer is?

[matter-of-fact] Split. Two things happening at once. That's the only real failure mode, and notice it's not about headcount. You can have seven people all going full tilt and it works fine, because they're going full tilt at the same thing. Two people quietly doing two different bits will kill it faster.

[curious] So the question I'm asking myself out there is —

[confident] Am I the focus right now, or am I the frame? That's it. That's the whole internal monologue. And the honest answer most of the time is: frame.

[conversational] Give me a concrete one.

[teaching] Say someone establishes a support group where everyone's addicted to something incredibly minor. First person: "I check if the door's locked. Twice." Second person doesn't invent a new premise — they find the next rung. "I read the terms and conditions. All of them." Third person heightens again. That's shifting focus, and every entrance is doing the same job: adding to the pattern, not changing the subject.

[curious] What's the version where it goes wrong?

[matter-of-fact] Someone walks in and says "actually I'm the therapist and I have a secret." Now we're somewhere else. The pattern's dead and we're starting over with five minutes gone.

[curious] So you enter when you've got a heighten.

[emphatic] You enter when you've got something that serves the pattern. And here's the part nobody wants to hear — if you'd just be redundant, stay out. More bodies is not more scene. Staying out is a support move, and it's the hardest one, because standing on the back line feeling useless is genuinely uncomfortable and stepping in fixes that feeling for you and no one else.

[contemplative] That's a real thing. The urge to enter is often about your own discomfort.

[matter-of-fact] Almost always. You feel like you're not contributing, so you contribute, and the contribution is noise.

[curious] Okay. Bigger question. Harold has group games baked in. Del Close treated them as where the ensemble proves it can think as one organism. So they're essential, right?

[teaching] They're essential to the Harold. That's a different claim than essential to improv.

[curious] Meaning?

[confident] TJ and Dave. Two people, one hour, no group scenes, because there is no group. Widely held to be the strongest longform act working. Nobody walks out of that show thinking something was missing.

[contemplative] Huh.

[teaching] So a group scene is a format requirement, not an artistic necessity. The Harold demands them. A two-person show can't have them and loses nothing it needed. And once you see that, you get a much better question than "when is the group game" — you get "does this actually need a group?"

[curious] What's the answer?

[teaching] A group scene earns its place when the thing you're exploring is genuinely collective. A shared belief. An institution. A crowd behaving as one organism. Something two people talking about it can't show you — they can only describe it. That's when you need the bodies.

[matter-of-fact] And when you run it as an obligation instead — seven people each hunting for a line to contribute because it's time for the group game — you get exactly the diffuse, shapeless stretch that audiences remember as the part where the Harold sagged.

[contemplative] It's the difference between the group having a point of view and the group having a turn.

[emphatic] Right. One voice. Not everyone saying the same words — everyone sharing the same reaction, the same angle, the same escalating behavior. One person makes a choice, everyone else recognizes the pattern and commits to it. If-this-then-what, run at ensemble scale.

[curious] And the hedging version?

[serious] Half-joining. Someone establishes that we're all terrified of the vending machine, and you come in at sixty percent because you're not sure yet. That sixty percent reads to the audience as doubt, and doubt is a split focus even when everyone's technically doing the same thing.

[contemplative] Which is not a stage-only problem.

[warm] No. Any room where the group's supposedly aligned and one person is at sixty percent — everyone can feel it. Nobody can name it. The meeting just doesn't land.
