[curious] Okay, presence. Everybody says it. "Be present." I've heard that note a hundred times and I still don't totally know what I'm supposed to do with it.

[teaching] Because it's not a thing you do. That's the confusion. "Be present" is a directive. Presence is the capacity that makes the directive possible. One is an instruction, the other is a muscle. Getting told to be present when you don't have the capacity is like getting told to lift the couch.

[curious] So what is the capacity, then?

[teaching] Sustained sensory contact with what's actually happening. Your partner. The space. Your own body. Right now. Not the version of it you're processing in your head — the actual thing.

[matter-of-fact] And it's somatic before it's cognitive. It lives in the body first. Breath. Weight on the floor. Eyes actually landing on a face.

[curious] Wait, say more about that. I always thought of presence as a mental state.

[teaching] Everyone does, and that's why they can't get there. You don't think yourself present. You sense yourself there. That's Spolin's whole pedagogy — she never says "be present." She gives you a point of concentration. Something physical to put your attention on. Feel the weight of the cup. Find the temperature of the room.

[warm] And presence shows up as a side effect. You were aiming at the cup and you arrived at the moment.

[curious] Why does the indirect route work better?

[contemplative] Here's the reframe that took me years. [pause] Presence isn't added. Nothing gets acquired. Contact with reality was already there — it was underneath a band of noise. The planning, the self-monitoring, the rehearsing of your next line. Drop the noise and what's left is exactly what was always there.

[emphatic] That's the whole thing. You can't work at presence directly because there's nothing to build. You can only stop doing the thing that's covering it.

[curious] Okay but "present" still sounds passive to me. Soft. Receptive.

[matter-of-fact] It's the opposite. A present improviser is not blank and waiting. They're hungry. Johnstone's image is the cat at the mouse hole — completely still, absolutely coiled. That's active receptivity.

[teaching] And you can tell the difference in one second of watching. A present performer reacts to what was said. An absent performer reacts to what they expected to be said.

[curious] Oh, that's brutal. That's every bad scene I've ever been in.

[warm] It's diagnosable, that's the good news. Presence is observable. Look at the eyes — are they looking at you or through you? Listen to the timing — is there latency? Is there a half-beat where they're catching up? And watch what they respond to. If someone answers a point you made thirty seconds ago, they left thirty seconds ago.

[curious] So where do people go when they leave?

[serious] Inward. And always for the same reason — self-protection. Fear of the silence. Fear of being wrong. Fear of where this scene is about to go. The mind retreats to plan a safe response, and the second it does that, presence is gone. You cannot be scanning for safety and making contact at the same time.

[teaching] Which is why trust isn't a nice-to-have. You can only stay present if you believe the ensemble will catch you while you're exposed. Bravery isn't a separate virtue over here — it's load-bearing for this.

[contemplative] And notice what falls apart downstream. You can't accept an offer you didn't hear. You can't defer judgment on something you missed. You can't send an honest signal from inside your own head. You can't keep it simple when you're busy reconstructing the context you lost. Presence isn't one principle among many. It's the floor the others stand on.

[curious] So is the goal to never drift?

[warm] No. Everybody drifts. Constantly. The practice isn't staying — it's noticing you've left and coming back. Not once. Continuously. The skill is the speed of the return.

[emphatic] And here's the part I love. The return usually isn't internal discipline. It's not you white-knuckling your way back. It's your partner. They make an offer, they land a look, and the sheer force of their presence pulls you into the room.

[contemplative] Which means presence is not actually an individual achievement. You hold it for each other. Somebody's always drifting, and somebody's always the anchor, and the roles keep swapping without anyone announcing it.

[curious] That reframes what I'm doing when I'm not the one talking.

[matter-of-fact] Completely. Being present while someone else drives isn't waiting for your turn. It's the thing that gets them back when they go.

[teaching] And off stage it's the same mechanic. The conversation where you're loading your response instead of listening. The meeting where you answer the question you prepared for. You didn't fail at being smart. You left the room and kept talking.

[contemplative] The cat doesn't try to concentrate on the mouse hole. It's just there, and the mouse hole is just there, and nothing is between them.
