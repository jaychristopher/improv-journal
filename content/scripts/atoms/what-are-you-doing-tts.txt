[curious] Okay, "What Are You Doing?" Everybody's done it. Two people, one's miming, the other asks the question, you name something you're not doing. Why does that exercise survive? It's in every intro class on the planet.

[teaching] Because it does one thing that almost nothing else does that cheaply. It splits you. Your hands are brushing your teeth, your mouth is saying "I'm mowing the lawn." Those are two separate processes running at once, and the exercise makes you hold both.

[curious] And that's the point? The split?

[teaching] That's the whole exercise. Everything else is scaffolding. Because the moment you're doing two things, you can't rehearse. There's no room. You cannot be brushing your teeth in a committed, specific way and also quietly auditioning three funny answers in the back of your head. [short pause] You'll find out very fast that your capacity has a ceiling.

[conversational] Which sounds like a limitation, but it's actually the gift.

[teaching] It's the gift. Because when you can't rehearse, what comes out is the obvious answer. And the obvious answer is the good one. Johnstone's frame is that the first thing that comes to you is connected to the moment — the second thing is connected to your ego. Most exercises ask you to trust that. This one just makes it structurally impossible to do anything else.

[curious] Say more about that. Because I think a lot of players hear "say the obvious thing" as "be boring."

[matter-of-fact] They do. And then they hesitate for half a second to find something better, and the rhythm's already broken. That's the tell. The exercise is unforgiving about timing in a way that's genuinely useful — you can hear the pause before a clever answer. It's audible. There's a specific moment in every run of this game where a player stops naming activities and starts naming jokes, and everyone in the room hears it happen.

[curious] What does that sound like?

[matter-of-fact] Silence. [short pause] Just a beat too long. And then something like "I'm wrestling a bear made of soup."

[conversational] Which gets a laugh.

[serious] Gets a laugh, and hands your partner a problem. Because now they have to mime wrestling a bear made of soup. That's not generosity, that's a bandwidth tax dressed up as a joke. Complex offers cost your partner more than they give them. The best offer in this game is "I'm folding laundry," because your partner can start folding laundry before you've finished the sentence.

[curious] There's the other half of it too — this is most people's first real space work. First time anyone asks them to mime.

[teaching] And here's what's sneaky about it. Nobody's asked to mime *well*. They're asked to mime *immediately*. Which turns out to be the part that actually matters. An unconvincing action performed without hesitation reads better to an audience than a beautifully detailed one performed a second late.

[curious] Why? That seems backwards.

[teaching] Because the audience isn't grading your technique, they're reading your commitment. Late means uncertain. Uncertain means we don't believe there's a lawnmower there — and if you don't believe there's a lawnmower there, no amount of detail is going to convince you.

[conversational] So what do you actually watch for when you're running this?

[teaching] One thing, mostly. Everyone shrinks. Watch a room do this and you'll see all the space work collapse into a little zone right in front of the chest — this generic hand-waving that could be washing dishes, could be playing violin, could be anything. It's activity-shaped rather than activity-specific.

[curious] And the fix?

[emphatic] One adjustment. Put the object somewhere. Somewhere specific. That's it — and the quality changes instantly, because an object with a location is an object you can pick up, put down, and come back to. The one in front of your chest can only be waved at.

[contemplative] That's the line between miming and building an environment, actually. Miming is doing a shape with your hands. An environment is a place where things have positions.

[curious] What about the failure where somebody answers with a feeling? "I'm being sad."

[matter-of-fact] Dead end. Gives the next player nothing to do with their hands. The answer has to be a verb somebody can perform — that's not a stylistic preference, it's load-bearing. You're not naming a state, you're handing over a task.

[conversational] There are variations that crank it up, right?

[teaching] A few good ones. Three players in a rotation, so you're answering one person while the next is already winding up to ask you — that removes the last bit of recovery time. Or same-first-letter, where every activity has to start with the same letter, which is wonderful because you get to watch how fast the obvious answers dry up the second you apply a filter.

[curious] And the no-talking version?

[teaching] That one's my favorite. Player B doesn't ask, they just guess out loud what A is doing — and A has to switch to whatever B guessed. Which means the whole thing becomes a test of how legible your mime actually is. You find out immediately whether the thing you thought was obviously "chopping vegetables" reads as chopping vegetables.

[contemplative] Usually it doesn't.

[curious] Where does this show up off stage? Because I feel it, I just can't name it.

[warm] Anytime you're doing something with your hands and talking at the same time — which is most of life. Driving and having a real conversation. Cooking while someone tells you about their day. The split is normal. What's not normal is being asked to notice it, and to notice that when the load goes up, the thing that gets dropped is the performance, not the truth.

[contemplative] You get very honest when you're too busy to edit.

[emphatic] That's what the exercise is really teaching. Not mime. Not speed. It's teaching you what you sound like when there's no room left to be clever — and then quietly suggesting that person is the better scene partner.
