[curious] Okay, World's Worst. Everybody's played it. Category gets called, people step out, say a line, step back. Is there anything actually happening there, or is it just a warm-up we do because it's easy?

[teaching] It's easy on purpose. And what makes it easy is the one thing no other form gives you — an offer with no consequences.

[curious] Meaning what, exactly?

[teaching] Every other form asks you to live with what you said. You start a scene, your partner builds on it, and now that choice is load-bearing. If it was weak, it stays in the room. You're carrying it for the next four minutes.

[teaching] Here your offer lasts three seconds and then the slate gets wiped. The next person starts clean. Nothing carries over. This is the cheapest place in improv to be wrong.

[curious] [short pause] And that's the point?

[emphatic] That's the whole point. That's why it works on the person who has frozen.

[teaching] Think about who's in your class or your team who can't start a scene. Just physically can't step out. You've watched them wait for someone else to go every single time. Now call world's worst dentist. They'll step forward. Almost always.

[curious] Why does that work? Same person, same room, same thirty seconds ago.

[teaching] Because the thing stopping them was never invention. Everyone assumes the block is "I can't think of anything." It almost never is. The block is hesitation about what the thing they think of will commit them to.

[warm] Remove the commitment, and it turns out the supply was there the whole time. They had ideas. They had ideas the entire time they were standing at the back of the room not going.

[contemplative] [pause] That's a rough thing to notice about yourself.

[warm] It's a useful thing to notice about yourself. And it's not just a stage problem. That's the meeting where you had the thought, and you were still deciding whether it was worth saying when the moment closed. Not a supply problem.

[curious] So underneath the game, what's actually being drilled?

[matter-of-fact] Initiation, over and over, with the follow-through deleted. That's it. It's a first-line drill wearing a costume.

[teaching] The first-line drill isolates that moment more honestly — here's the top of a scene, say something, we stop, do it again. But it's naked. People know they're being examined. World's Worst hides the same rep inside a game, which is often the only way to get a nervous room to do it forty times in five minutes.

[curious] Forty times. Okay, so if I'm running it — what am I watching for?

[teaching] Three failure modes, and they're all common.

[emphatic] First: waiting for a good one. Somebody's standing there holding out for something clever while three other people step forward and back. They think they're maintaining quality. They've misread what's being trained.

[curious] But isn't a better line just... better?

[matter-of-fact] The rhythm is the game. That pause costs more than a mediocre line would have. A mediocre line keeps forty reps moving. The pause tells the room that stepping out requires a permit.

[teaching] Second one: explaining instead of showing. "World's worst wedding speech" turns into a thirty-second monologue with a beginning, a middle, and a callback.

[curious] [short pause] Which is a sketch.

[matter-of-fact] Which is a sketch. The form is a snapshot. One image, one line, out. If you're doing a bit, you've stopped playing the game and started performing at it.

[teaching] Third, and this is the one people don't fix: only the confident step forward. There's no rotation, so the same four people take every turn and everyone else gets a really nice evening of watching.

[curious] And the fix is just — a fixed order?

[confident] Run it in order. Costs nothing. It's almost always better, because the point is the reps, not the highlights. The four people who'd take every turn anyway don't need the reps. Everyone else does.

[contemplative] [pause] Can I raise the honest objection though?

[warm] Please.

[serious] This trains punchline production. It does not train scene work. And those two things pull against each other.

[curious] Say more.

[serious] Somebody who has gotten genuinely good at World's Worst has practised one specific skill: arriving with a finished joke. Walking in already holding the thing.

[emphatic] Which is close to the opposite of what game of the scene asks. Game asks you to notice what's already happening and build from it. It requires you to arrive empty and pay attention.

[curious] So you get people entering scenes with material instead of attention.

[matter-of-fact] Every time. And they're often the funniest people in the warm-up, which makes it worse, because nobody wants to tell them the thing they're best at is the thing hurting their scenes.

[confident] Not remotely. Used for exactly what it is — a way to get a cold room, or a frightened room, making offers fast — there is very little that does the job better.
