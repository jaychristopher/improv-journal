[curious] I want to talk about the form where you actually tell a story. Not scenes that rhyme with each other — an actual beginning, middle, and end.

[teaching] Narrative longform. An improvised play. An improvised film. One through-line, and everybody's serving it.

[curious] So how is that different from what I already do? Because a Harold has scenes in it.

[teaching] A Harold is a collage. You've got three unrelated threads and they're held together by theme, and the comedy comes from the juxtaposition — the fact that scene two and scene five are secretly about the same thing and nobody said it out loud. Narrative is different. In narrative, scene three happens *because* scene two happened. It's causal. Harold runs on game. Narrative runs on consequence.

[curious] Consequence. Say more.

[teaching] In a game-based scene, the question is "what's the pattern and how do we heighten it?" In a narrative scene, the question is "what did the last scene cost, and who's paying for it now?" Same skill of listening. Totally different thing you're listening *for*.

[conversational] Okay, so if I'm building one of these, where do I actually start?

[teaching] Johnstone gives you three pieces and they're worth memorizing. Platform. Tilt. Reincorporation.

[curious] Platform first.

[teaching] Platform is the boring part everybody wants to skip. Where are we, who are you, what's normal here. Establishing shot before character. It's film convention — you see the house before you meet the family. And improvisers hate it because it feels like nothing's happening.

[emphatic] But nothing *should* be happening yet. That's the whole point. You cannot disturb a room you haven't built.

[curious] And then the tilt.

[teaching] The tilt is the moment the balance of normality gets pushed over. Johnstone's phrase. The letter arrives. The stranger walks in. The thing you've been doing every day for twenty years — today it doesn't work. And here's the useful bit: in *Impro for Storytellers* he actually gives you lists of tilts. Pre-made. Because he knew improvisers would sit in the platform forever, being charming, waiting for inspiration.

[matter-of-fact] Inspiration isn't coming. Tilt it.

[curious] And reincorporation is the ending?

[contemplative] Reincorporation is the whole art. This is the line — the improviser is like a man walking backwards. He sees where he's been. He pays no attention to the future.

[curious] [short pause] Say that again.

[teaching] He sees where he's been. He pays no attention to the future. You're not planning the story. You're walking forward while looking backward, and everything you already said is the material. The dog you mentioned in scene one. The limp. The brother nobody talks about. You shelved those. Reincorporation is going back and picking them up.

[emphatic] And this is the part that actually surprises people — that's how you find the ending. Toward the end, reincorporations start coming faster. Faster, faster, and then you run out of shelved material. That's the ending. It arrives. You don't decide it.

[curious] So the ending is just... when there's nothing left to bring back?

[warm] Almost always. And when a narrative show feels unsatisfying, nine times out of ten it's because there's still stuff on the shelf. The audience can feel the loose threads even if they couldn't name them.

[conversational] What are the actual forms people run?

[matter-of-fact] The Movie — one of the Three Mad Rituals from The Family under Del Close. Cast paints three locations, announces a title, and you rotate through those locations. The backline calls camera directions — close-up, pan, split screen, slow motion. Genre does a lot of the work for you.

[teaching] The Musical, which is Baby Wants Candy territory — songs, story, choreography, all of it invented. BATS out of San Francisco pioneered two-hour single-story shows with an intermission. An actual improvised play. And then Story Spine, which is the scaffold — once upon a time, every day, until one day, because of that, until finally. It takes the guesswork out of what part of the story you're in.

[curious] Okay, and where does this go wrong? Because I've seen narrative shows that were rough.

[serious] Two failure modes, and they're both real.

[teaching] First one — the UCB critique, and it's not wrong. When you commit to narrative, people get so busy advancing the plot that they walk right past the funny. Somebody makes an unusual choice and instead of exploring it, the group goes "great, so what happens next?" You're managing a story instead of playing a scene. Del Close held that tension deliberately — the Harold is non-linear on purpose.

[curious] And the second one?

[matter-of-fact] Narrative asks for acting skills. Character arcs. Dramatic structure. Emotional stakes that hold for ninety minutes. A lot of improvisers have never trained that, so what you get isn't good improv or good theater — it's a plot summary performed by people.

[curious] So how do you not do that?

[teaching] Stay in the scene. The plot is not your job — reincorporation handles the plot. Your job is to be a person in a room who wants something and can't have it. If everybody does that and somebody's tracking the shelf, the story assembles itself.

[contemplative] And honestly — the walking backwards thing outlives the form. Most of us live the opposite way. Squinting forward, trying to script the next five years, treating everything behind us as finished business.

[warm] Johnstone's saying the material's already there. You've been laying it down the whole time. You just have to turn around and use it.
