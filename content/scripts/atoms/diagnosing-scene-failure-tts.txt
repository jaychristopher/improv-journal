[curious] Okay, so a scene doesn't work. You come off stage, you know it didn't land. What do you actually do with that?

[teaching] Most of us do the same thing. We say "that was rough," we shrug, and then we sign up for another class. More reps, more stage time, more volume. Which feels like the responsible answer.

[curious] But it's not?

[matter-of-fact] Volume improves whatever you're already doing. That's the trap. If the thing you're already doing is the thing that broke the scene, more reps just carves the groove deeper.

[curious] So what's the alternative? Because when I try to describe a bad scene, all I've got is — it was slow. It felt forced. We weren't connecting.

[teaching] Right, and listen to those. Every one of them describes what the failure *felt* like. None of them describe what the failure *was*. You can't practise "it felt forced." There's no Tuesday night drill for that.

[short pause]

[teaching] A useful diagnosis has to name something specific enough that a drill attaches to it.

[curious] So how do you get from the feeling to the mechanism?

[confident] Three steps. Name the collapse mode. Find the shadow. Practise the principle.

[curious] Start with collapse mode.

[teaching] There are three, and the good news is you can tell them apart from the outside. From the audience seat.

[teaching] Latency — the rhythm lagged. Responses arrived a half-beat late. The energy just kind of sank. Nobody did anything *wrong*, exactly, everything was just... behind.

[teaching] Fracture — you were in two different scenes. Someone's reality got denied, or just missed. You're playing a funeral and they're playing a birthday and neither of you noticed.

[teaching] And decay — nothing accumulated. This is the sneaky one. The scene was pleasant. Busy, even. Lots of talking. But the things you established stopped mattering. Weightless.

[curious] Decay sounds like the one I'd miss.

[emphatic] Decay is the one everyone notices *last* and diagnoses *first* — because it's how the scene ended. And that's the trap in step one.

[curious] What do you mean?

[teaching] The modes cascade. A scene that ends in decay very often started with latency. You got in your head at the top, so you were late, so you missed each other, so nothing landed, so nothing built. By minute four it's all weightless. But if you go home and drill "make things matter," you're treating the last domino.

[contemplative] You have to run the diagnosis backwards from what you noticed. Name the *first* mode, not the loudest one.

[curious] Okay. So I've named it. Say it's latency. What's step two?

[teaching] Find the shadow. Every collapse mode gets produced by a behaviour, and every one of those behaviours is a principle turned inside out.

[curious] Give me the latency ones.

[matter-of-fact] Three. Internal computation — you're working out the clever thing before you say it. That's the shadow of Be Present. Hesitation — you had it, you didn't commit. Shadow of Be Brave. And judgment — you weighed your partner's offer instead of taking it. Shadow of Be Thankful.

[curious] And fracture?

[matter-of-fact] Negation, which is the shadow of Be Positive. Bulldozing — shadow of Be Supportive. And steering, where you're gently driving the scene toward the version you already imagined. That's the shadow of Be Changeable.

[curious] Steering feels so innocent.

[warm] It's the most polite way to refuse someone. Which is why it's worth naming.

[curious] And decay?

[matter-of-fact] Overcomplication — shadow of Be Simple. And performing cleverness — shadow of Be Honest. Both of them add material without adding weight.

[curious] Where's blocking in all this? I expected it to have its own row.

[teaching] Blocking's the family name, not a member. It's what we call refusing what you were offered — and it shows up as negation, or steering, or bulldozing, depending on how you delivered the refusal. Naming it "blocking" is already too coarse to practise.

[curious] So step three is the drill.

[emphatic] Step three is the *principle*. And this is the whole point of the exercise. "Be less awkward" is not practisable. But — I retreated into my head at the top of the scene, that's internal computation, that's the shadow of Be Present, so this week I'm drilling first responses. That's a plan. That's something a Tuesday can hold.

[contemplative] Diagnosis is what makes practice compound instead of just accumulate.

[curious] I want to push on something. Isn't there a risk here? If I'm running a taxonomy in my head about collapse modes and shadows —

[serious] Yes. Absolutely yes. And that objection is the real one, not a footnote.

[serious] Self-monitoring is what *causes* latency. If you're on stage checking whether you're currently in fracture, congratulations, you've just built the problem you were trying to avoid. Napier would say it, Johnstone would say it louder.

[matter-of-fact] So this is a tool for after. For the drive home. For notes, for coaching, for planning what you work on. Not for minute two of the scene.

[curious] Onstage you're just...

[warm] Onstage you're just there. That's the entire job.

[contemplative] And the off-stage version of this is almost too neat. Think about the meeting that went badly, or the conversation with someone you love that went sideways. You get the same useless report from yourself — it was tense, it felt off. Same problem. That's a symptom, not a mechanism.

[curious] So you'd ask which part broke.

[teaching] Were you late — computing your response while they were still talking? Were you in different conversations entirely, each answering something the other didn't say? Or did it just... not accumulate. Pleasant, busy, and nothing you said actually landed anywhere.

[emphatic] Same three. And the same rule holds — name the first one, not the last one. Because "that conversation was tense" gets you nowhere, and "I was drafting my defense instead of listening" gets you somewhere by Tuesday.
