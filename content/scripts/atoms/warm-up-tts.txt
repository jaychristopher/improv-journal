[teaching] You're changing brain states. There's fMRI work — Limb and Braun, 2008 — where they looked at jazz musicians improvising. That's self-expression, internally motivated behavior.

[curious] So improvising is literally a different brain configuration.

[matter-of-fact] Looks that way. Worth flagging that they studied pianists, not improvisers on a stage — the transfer to theatrical improv is an inference, not a proven finding. But the shape of it matches what everybody reports. And warm-ups accelerate the shift.

[teaching] You spend the first ten to fifteen minutes of your show still in daily-life brain. Monitoring. Judging. Planning your next line while your scene partner is talking. [short pause] The audience is watching you catch up to yourself.

[conversational] Which is a specific, recognizable badness. That first scene that never quite lands, and then the show finds its feet by scene three.

[teaching] Ensemble. Everyone arrives in a different state. One person just fought with their partner in the car, one just crushed a work presentation, one hasn't spoken to a human all day. [curious] Johnstone has a line about this, right?

[matter-of-fact] People who warm up together perform together. People who don't, perform next to each other.

[teaching] It's the difference between a group scene and four solo scenes happening at the same volume.

[conversational] So the shaking and the lip trills aren't superstition. They're removing specific obstacles.
