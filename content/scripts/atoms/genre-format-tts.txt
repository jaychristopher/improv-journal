[curious] So genre improv. Film noir, horror, rom-com, musical. Is that just... doing a bit? Like, we all agree to talk in a certain voice for thirty minutes?

[teaching] That's the version that goes wrong, yeah. But the reason genre exists as a form is more interesting than that. Think about what every other longform does. Harold, Deconstruction, an organic set — you walk out on an empty stage and you build the structure during the show. The audience is learning your rules while you're inventing them.

[curious] And genre skips that.

[teaching] Genre borrows a structure the audience already walked in with. They know what noir is. They know the detective has a past, the client is lying, and it rains. You don't have to teach any of that. You get to start on page forty.

[contemplative] [short pause] That's a real head start.

[teaching] It's an enormous head start. And it means the show isn't about establishing — it's about what you do with something already established.

[curious] Okay so what do you do with it? Because "the detective has a past" is not a scene.

[matter-of-fact] There are basically three moves, and every genre show is some mix of them. You can play the convention straight. You can turn away from it. Or you can push it past where it wants to stop.

[curious] Straight first. That sounds like the boring one.

[teaching] It's the one people underrate the most. Playing a trope straight, in real time, invented on the spot — audiences love that. There's a specific pleasure in watching someone nail the thing you were already expecting. The femme fatale walks in, and the moment she says the line you knew was coming, the room lights up. Not because it's surprising. Because it's *right*.

[curious] Recognition as a reward.

[teaching] Exactly. You're not fooling them. You're confirming them. And that's a legitimate thing for a show to do.

[curious] And subverting.

[matter-of-fact] Turning away from the convention once it's established. Key word: established. You can't subvert something the audience hasn't already been given. If your first move is the twist, there's nothing to twist. The detective has to be the detective for a while before it matters that he's afraid of the dark.

[emphatic] [short pause] That's the mistake, by the way. Groups who think genre is beneath them go straight for the subversion and there's nothing underneath it. You're commenting on a thing you never built.

[curious] And heightening?

[teaching] Take the trope to its logical extreme and keep going. Noir voiceover — fine. Noir voiceover where the detective narrates every single thing including his own narration. Horror where the group splits up, and then splits up again, and again, until there's one guy alone in a room going "I'll check the basement."

[conversational] The joke isn't that the trope is dumb. The joke is that the trope is *committed*.

[curious] Where does the actual form come from? Somebody made this a thing.

[teaching] The Movie came out of The Family, under Del Close — one of the three so-called mad rituals alongside Harold and Deconstruction. The cast scene-paints three locations using screenplay language, calls out a title, then rotates through those locations. And the backline is calling camera directions. Close-up. Pan. Split screen. Slow motion. Soundtrack cue.

[curious] So the backline is doing cinematography.

[warm] That's the part that makes it sing. Genre isn't just dialogue in a costume — it's the whole cinematic vocabulary as an improv tool. Somebody calls "slow motion" and the scene bends. Somebody hums a soundtrack sting and the room knows what's about to happen. Besser later taught it at UCB as Feature Feature.

[curious] And musicals. Baby Wants Candy.

[matter-of-fact] Chicago, four thousand-plus improvised musicals. Audience shouts a title for a musical that has never existed, and a cast plus a live band builds the whole thing — story, songs, lyrics, choreography. Their song architecture is simpler than you'd think. Verse-chorus, and lastline tagline.

[curious] Lastline meaning you take the last thing said in the scene and that becomes—

[teaching] That becomes the hook. The song is already sitting there in the dialogue; you just have to notice it. And BATS in San Francisco went the other direction with it — two-hour single-story genre shows with an intermission. An improvised play. Kung fu, noir, rom-com, Broadway.

[curious] Alright. Where does this go wrong?

[serious] Shared literacy. This is the whole ballgame. Genre only works if the ensemble actually knows the genre — not "I've seen a noir movie," but a real working sense of how noir moves. If half your cast has that and half doesn't, you don't get genre play. You get pastiche.

[curious] What's the difference, functionally?

[matter-of-fact] Pastiche is people signaling the genre to each other. Trench coat, cigarette, "it was a dark and stormy night." Genre play is people making decisions *from inside* the genre's logic. In noir, the detective takes the case he shouldn't take. Nobody has to announce that — everyone just knows that's what happens, and the scene moves.

[teaching] The tell is whether anyone can be surprised. If the ensemble shares the vocabulary, you can build on each other. If they don't, everyone's stuck restating the premise.

[curious] And the physical side?

[teaching] Underserved constantly. Genre worlds have to be built, not referenced. Space work, environmental specificity, actual physicality. You can say "we're in a dojo" or you can be in a dojo. One of those the audience can see.

[curious] There's a whole camp that thinks this form is a dead end, right?

[matter-of-fact] There is. The organic tradition — TJ and Dave, that lineage — argues genre is inherently referential. You're commenting on movies instead of finding truthful human moments. And the Johnstone side says genre rewards cleverness over story.

[contemplative] [pause] They're not wrong about the failure mode. They're describing bad genre accurately.

[curious] But?

[warm] But the frame cuts both ways. Genre is a set of expectations a group of strangers already shares. That's rare and it's worth something. What you do inside it can be as human as anything — the noir detective who can't say the thing he means, the rom-com couple missing each other by one beat. The genre is the container. It doesn't decide what you put in it.

[curious] And the musical barrier?

[serious] Real, and I won't soften it. Baby Wants Candy-style musical improv demands singing ability a lot of improvisers don't have, and the gap between trained and untrained is visible from the back row. It's the one genre where enthusiasm doesn't cover for you.

[contemplative] [short pause] Which is maybe the honest summary of the whole form. Genre gives you a structure you didn't have to build. It just doesn't give you the knowledge to fill it. That part you have to actually have.
