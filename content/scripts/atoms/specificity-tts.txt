[curious] Okay, specificity. Everybody nods at this one. Every coach says it. But I want it named precisely, because I think "be specific" has become wallpaper.

[teaching] Here's the precise version. Specificity is naming the particular instead of the category. Not a dog — a whippet with a limp. Not "my job" — the Tuesday shift at the garden centre. That's it. That's the whole move.

[curious] And that's cheap. That costs me nothing.

[matter-of-fact] Cheapest move available. And the one people skip most consistently.

[curious] So why does it work so hard for something so cheap?

[teaching] Because a specific detail does three things at once, and a general one does none of them. First — it gives your partner material. "A dog" offers nothing. There's no handle on it. "A whippet with a limp" arrives already carrying a temperament, a history, and a reason somebody cares. Every one of those is something your partner can grab.

[curious] Okay, that's the obvious one. What's the second?

[teaching] It characterises you. [short pause] Only a person who knows about whippets says whippet. The detail tells us as much about whoever chose it as it does about the dog. Which means specificity is doing relationship work while it looks like it's just doing description.

[contemplative] That's sneaky. You think you're painting the set and you're actually painting yourself.

[teaching] Every time. And the third thing — it settles instantly. Nobody can dispute a detail they've just been handed. The moment you say it, it's shared reality. No negotiation, no "wait, is it a dog or a wolf," none of that. It's the fastest, cheapest way to build base reality that exists.

[curious] So let's talk about the failure it fixes. Because I know the scene.

[emphatic] You know the scene. Two people agreeing pleasantly about nothing in particular. Nice location — no features. Nice subject — neither of them committed to it. Everything's fine and nothing is happening.

[curious] The scene that's not bad, it's just... vapour.

[matter-of-fact] Vapour. And the instinct when a scene goes to vapour is to add energy. Get louder. Introduce a plot. Somebody's got a gun now.

[teaching] Doesn't work. What works is one concrete detail. A scene that's drifted from specific to abstract gets pulled back faster by a single real noun than by anything else you can throw at it. Faster than energy. Much faster than a new plot.

[curious] Which is probably why "be more specific" is such a good note.

[confident] It's the best note a coach has. Because it's actionable in the very next sentence. The player doesn't have to go away and analyse what went wrong. They just say a truer noun and the scene comes back.

[curious] All right. Where do people get this wrong?

[teaching] Two ways. The first is confusing detail with specificity. They think it's a volume problem, so they pile adjectives on. "A big, sad, sort of scruffy, brownish dog."

[conversational] That's more words about less.

[emphatic] That's decoration. And it costs bandwidth — it makes the offer harder to catch, not easier. One exact noun beats three approximate ones. Every time.

[curious] And the second way?

[serious] Specificity aimed at the wrong thing. Elaborate backstory in the first minute. Deeply specific — dates, names, the whole family history — and almost always unhelpful, because it commits the scene to material nobody asked for. You've actually left less room than the vagueness you replaced.

[contemplative] So it's not just how specific. It's specific about what.

[matter-of-fact] Specific about what's in front of you. The thing on the table, the thing you're holding, the thing you just noticed about them.

[curious] Okay, the practical question. Where do I get these details from? Because in the moment, my brain goes: dog. Just — dog.

[teaching] Here's the reframe. You're not inventing. You're recalling. [pause] The reliable route to a specific detail is to use a real one. An actual street. An actual brand. An actual small annoyance you've genuinely had.

[curious] So the obvious choice is the specific choice.

[confident] The obvious choice produces specificity as a by-product. The first thing that occurs to you is usually remembered rather than constructed — and remembered things arrive with detail already attached. You don't have to build the whippet. You just have to remember the one from your street.

[contemplative] Which is a much smaller ask than "be creative."

[teaching] Infinitely smaller. And this is the mechanism underneath something people say a lot and rarely unpack — that honesty expresses itself as specificity.

[curious] Say more, because "be honest" on stage usually gets heard as "confess something."

[emphatic] It isn't asking you to confess. It's asking you to use what you actually know. [short pause] And what you actually know is specific. Nobody knows things in the general. You don't know "restaurants," you know the one with the bad lighting where the waiter never came. That's the material. It's already in you, already detailed, already true.

[contemplative] And off stage it's the same tell, isn't it. You can hear when someone's talking about a category versus talking about a thing that happened to them.

[confident] You can hear it instantly. Vague talk is almost always talk about something you haven't looked at closely. The person who says "we had some issues with the rollout" and the person who says "the invoices went out with last year's date on them" — one of them has actually been looking.
