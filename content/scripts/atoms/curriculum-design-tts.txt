[curious] You've got eight people in a room on a Monday night and eight weeks with them. What do you teach first?

[teaching] Not improv.

[curious] Not improv.

[teaching] The first thing you build is the room. Before a single exercise, before agreement, before anybody makes an offer. Because everything you're about to ask them to do is a small act of exposure, and if they don't trust the room to catch them, they'll spend eight weeks managing their image instead of learning anything.

[curious] That sounds like something people say and then skip.

[matter-of-fact] It is. Most curriculum failures aren't sequencing failures further down — they're this. You skipped the foundation and then spent week five confused about why nobody will commit.

[curious] Okay, so the room is safe. Then what? Is there an actual order, or does every school just make it up?

[teaching] There's an order. And what's interesting is that nobody agreed on it — UCB, iO, Second City, Annoyance all developed independently, with different aesthetics and different opinions about almost everything, and they converged on roughly the same five stages.

[curious] Which are.

[teaching] Foundation — listening, agreement, presence, making an offer. Then scene work — character, relationship, environment, emotional commitment. Then game — the pattern in the scene, heightening, discovery, editing. Then form — the Harold, or a montage, or whatever longform structure you're building toward. Then performance — pacing, the audience, ensemble.

[curious] Why that order and not some other one?

[emphatic] Because each stage is literally made out of the one before it. [short pause] You cannot play a pattern you keep denying. So yes-and comes before game — not because it's easier, but because game is impossible without it. You cannot edit a scene if you can't tell what a scene is doing. So scene work comes before editing. And a Harold is nine games plus the connections between them, which means Harold isn't a level, it's an audit of everything underneath it.

[contemplative] That's the part that reframes it for me. The Harold isn't the hardest thing on the list because it's complicated. It's the hardest thing because it's a test that includes every previous test.

[teaching] Right. That's why it's the standard culmination. It doesn't add a new skill. It asks for all of them at once, under time pressure, in front of people.

[curious] Here's what I want to poke at. These are prerequisites in the sense that a math class has prerequisites. But you're not teaching facts. You're teaching habits.

[emphatic] That's the whole thing, and it's where most training design goes wrong. The prerequisite isn't "they've heard about yes-and." The prerequisite is that yes-and has become automatic enough to stop consuming attention.

[curious] Say more.

[teaching] A student who is still actively fighting the urge to plan the scene cannot also track a game. There isn't room. The planning is using all the bandwidth. So if you introduce game to that group, they don't learn game — they learn a vocabulary word they'll use to describe scenes they still can't play. You've taught them to talk about it. You've taught them nothing.

[contemplative] Which is a very familiar failure outside a theater. Somebody sits through the workshop, learns the framework, can name all four quadrants, and behaves in the next meeting exactly the way they behaved in the last one.

[matter-of-fact] Because the workshop taught concepts on top of habits that weren't there yet. The sequence was violated and nobody noticed, because concepts are easy to deliver and easy to assess. Habits are neither.

[curious] So how do you know when a group is ready to move up?

[warm] You watch them. That's the honest answer. The curriculum is a map, not a timetable. Some groups need three more weeks on listening than the syllabus allows. Some groups are ready for game in week two and will get bored if you make them wait.

[curious] But you wrote a syllabus. You told people it's eight weeks.

[teaching] And that's the tension every teacher lives in. The phrase I'd hold onto is: it's a progression, not a schedule. The order is real. The pace is a judgment call you make in the room, on the night, with those eight people.

[curious] Isn't that a little convenient? "The framework is rigorous, except when I decide it isn't."

[confident] No, and here's the distinction. The prerequisites don't move. You never teach editing before scene work, no matter how sharp the group is — that's not flexibility, that's just skipping. What moves is dwell time. How long you stay. What you loop back to. Which exercise you use to get there.

[contemplative] The skeleton is fixed. The tempo is yours.

[teaching] And the vehicles are yours. This is the part people underrate — the exercises aren't illustrations of the curriculum. The progression through exercises is the curriculum. Spolin built an entire book that way. Nobody learns presence from a definition of presence. They learn it from a warm-up that punishes not paying attention, run forty times.

[curious] Let me raise the obvious objection. There are extraordinary improvisers who didn't go through any of this. TJ and Dave learned mostly by performing.

[matter-of-fact] True, and worth taking seriously rather than waving off. Self-directed learning has real advocates, and performance is a brutally efficient teacher — it gives feedback every four seconds and doesn't care about your feelings.

[curious] So why bother with structure at all?

[teaching] Because the research on creative teaching lands in a place that sounds like a dodge and isn't: good teaching is a balance between structure and improvisation. Structure gets a group of eight strangers to competence reliably, in eight weeks, without anyone getting hurt. Unstructured immersion produces some brilliant people and quietly loses the rest. You just never survey the ones who quit.

[curious] The survivors write the memoir.

[matter-of-fact] The survivors always write the memoir. If you're designing for a group, you don't get to design for the survivors.

[contemplative] So the meta-skill isn't knowing the five levels. It's knowing what a group can actually hold right now, and refusing to teach the thing they can name but can't do.

[warm] And being willing to spend week four on something you'd planned for week one. That's not falling behind. That's the job.
