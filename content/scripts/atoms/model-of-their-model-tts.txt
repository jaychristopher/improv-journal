[curious] I want to start with something I keep getting wrong. I'll be mid-scene, I'll think I know exactly what my partner is doing, and then they do something completely different. And my first reaction is always "they broke the scene."

[warm] When really, they were never in the scene you thought they were in.

[curious] Right. I had a whole movie playing in my head about what we were building together. And it turns out... that movie was mine. Alone. They had their own.

[teaching] You had your model of their model. Which is always what you have. You never have direct access to their mind. You have a sculpture of it that you built from what you noticed — what they said, how they paused, what they leaned into. And that sculpture is always at least a little wrong.

[short pause]

[contemplative] And the size of the wrongness is bigger than it feels.

[teaching] Always. Because the sculpture gets filled in by you — your priors, your assumptions, what you would have done. That's projection. Then role stereotyping. Then whatever signal arrived most recently weighted disproportionately. You're not sculpting from clean clay; you're sculpting from your defaults.

[curious] So when I'm doing that thing where I "know" what kind of character my partner is playing — I'm not really reading them, I'm reading me.

[matter-of-fact] You're reading yourself, applied to them. Which is fine. Which is unavoidable. The mistake isn't having a model. The mistake is forgetting the model is yours.

[short pause]

[curious] Okay so what changes when you do remember that?

[teaching] You hold the model loosely. You act on it — you have to, you can't wait for certainty — but every new signal is evidence that could update it. A held-loosely model gets sharper with each exchange. A held-firmly model gets defended against the very signals that would correct it.

[warm] That's the move. Defending your read of your partner instead of updating it. You can feel it happen — that little internal "no, they're definitely doing the thing I thought."

[curious] Yeah. That's the moment I stop listening, actually. Because I've decided what's happening.

[teaching] Which is why Del Close kept hammering "listen" at students who thought they already were. They were hearing the words. They weren't updating their model.

[short pause]

[contemplative] Johnstone's "be obvious" lives here too, doesn't it?

[teaching] It does. If you can't read your partner reliably, don't try to outguess them. Give them something obvious — something readable — and let their response tell you where they are. The obvious offer is a probe. Not a guess about what they want, but a clean question their answer will reveal.

[curious] So it's not "make a boring choice." It's "make a choice they can clearly respond to so I learn what they're actually thinking."

[warm] Exactly. It's epistemic, not aesthetic. The obvious offer collects data.

[long pause]

[curious] This isn't just stage stuff, is it.

[matter-of-fact] No. Every persuasive move you've ever made presupposed a model of the person you were trying to persuade. The quality of the move is bounded by the quality of the model. You can have brilliant tactics aimed at a phantom and still lose, because the phantom is not who you're talking to.

[contemplative] So when I'm in a hard conversation and I'm "sure" they're going to react badly to what I say next...

[teaching] You're sure your model is going to react badly. The actual person hasn't reacted yet. Sometimes they don't react how the model predicted — but you can't see it if you're committed to the model.

[curious] The fix?

[matter-of-fact] Three habits, in order of cost. One — name your model out loud. "I'm reading this as you being annoyed; is that right?" Let them correct it. Two — listen for the gap between what you predicted and what they actually did, and treat the gap as the real data. Three — before you press a point, ask whether you're pressing against their actual position or against your model of it.

[warm] That third one breaks more arguments than I can count. Half the time the disagreement was with a position I constructed.

[short pause]

[curious] Even saying that out loud — "you only have a model of their model" — feels useful. Like it gives me a place to stand that isn't certainty.

[teaching] It's the only honest place to stand. Not "I know what they're thinking" — that's the lie. Not "I have no idea" — that's the dodge. The honest version is "I have a model, and the model is mine, and I'm willing to update it."

[contemplative] Which is also what makes someone feel met. When you change your mind based on what they actually said, instead of arguing with the version of them in your head.

[warm] That feeling — of being received instead of predicted — is what people mean by "actually heard."

[long pause]

[curious] One last thing. Skilled empathy, cold reading — they do this better than most of us, right? They narrow the gap?

[teaching] They narrow it. They don't close it. The expert failure mode is overconfidence in a model that's gotten better but is still a model. The discipline applies most at the moment you're most sure you understand someone.

[emphatic] Especially then.
