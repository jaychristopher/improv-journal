[curious] So Theatresports. Two teams, a referee, an audience with scorecards. Is this actually improv, or is it improv wearing a sports jersey?

[teaching] It's improv that admitted something out loud. Johnstone started it at Loose Moose in Calgary in 1977, and his diagnosis was brutal. He said improv bored audiences because the performers weren't really trying. Not because they lacked skill. Because nothing was at stake, so nobody pushed.

[curious] And competition fixes that?

[teaching] Competition creates effort. That's the whole engine. You give two teams the same challenge and something in the body changes — you commit faster, you take the bigger swing, you stop hedging. Johnstone wasn't trying to find the best improviser. He was trying to build a night out that people would come back for.

[emphatic] And here's the part people miss. The scoring isn't for the performers. It's for the audience.

[curious] Say more.

[warm] Think about a normal improv show. The audience sits there being polite. They laugh, sure, but there's a wall — they're watching art happen. Now hand them a card with a number on it. Suddenly they're allowed to react. Loudly. They boo the referee, they groan, they cheer their team. [short pause] And that noise is the fuel. The performers ride it. It's a loop — the ref calls a challenge, the teams play, the audience scores, and the scoring energy comes right back at the stage.

[matter-of-fact] The points don't mean anything. The permission means everything.

[curious] Okay, so a match is what — ninety minutes of games?

[teaching] Roughly. Teams of four or five. The referee calls a challenge each round — scene in verse, genre replay, expert scene, whatever. Sometimes both teams take the same prompt head to head, sometimes one team showcases while the other watches. Scenes run three to seven minutes. You establish, you play, you finish. Fast.

[curious] What's the referee actually for, though? Beyond timekeeping.

[confident] The referee is Johnstone's pedagogy with a whistle. That's the foul system, and it's the most underrated thing in the format.

[curious] Fouls. In improv.

[teaching] Blocking — you refuse someone's offer, that's a foul. Gagging — you go for the cheap laugh and torch the scene to get it, foul. Wimping — you hedge, you won't commit, you stay safe, foul. Groaning — if the audience groans, you've offended or gone gratuitously crude, foul. And the big one, the sin above all sins in Johnstone's universe: being boring.

[emphatic] He took the things a teacher would give you a note about after the show and made them cost you points during it. In front of everyone. That's not decoration. That's a curriculum enforced in real time.

[contemplative] And it's honest about something most training rooms aren't. You can be told "don't block" for years and still block. But get whistled for it in front of two hundred people once, and your body remembers.

[curious] So what does the format actually demand from a player?

[teaching] Four things. Versatility — you'll hit six wildly different challenge types in one night, so you can't have one trick. Speed — three minutes means no ten-beat exploration, you land it or you don't. Showmanship — the competitive frame rewards entertaining, not just technically sound. And above all, audience awareness. More than any longform format, Theatresports asks you to read a room and ride it in real time.

[curious] That third one worries me. "Rewards entertaining, not just good." That sounds like it rewards mugging.

[serious] It does. That's the real critique, and it isn't unfair. The Chicago longform tradition — Del Close and everyone downstream — sees competition as fundamentally against ensemble. Close reportedly called shortform the fast food of improv. The argument is that when a number gets attached to your scene, you start optimizing for the number. You showboat. You play to the crowd instead of playing to your partner. You go for the laugh instead of the truth.

[curious] And Johnstone's answer is the fouls.

[matter-of-fact] Exactly. Gagging is a foul specifically because he saw this coming. The foul system is his pre-emptive strike against his own format's failure mode.

[contemplative] But you have to be honest — plenty of Theatresports shows still devolve into mugging. The mechanism exists; it doesn't run itself. It only works if the referee actually calls it, and a lot of referees don't want to be the person who kills the fun.

[curious] So where does it actually go wrong?

[teaching] Two places. One, a weak referee. If the ref is just a genial host announcing games, you've got a variety show with numbers stapled on and every incentive pointing toward cheap. The ref has to be willing to be unpopular.

[emphatic] Two — and this is the bigger one — treating the competition as real. The second a team wants to win more than they want to make a good show, it curdles. You get sabotage disguised as playing, you get players cutting each other off, you get the loudest person on stage eating every scene. The competition is a frame. Frames are supposed to be invisible. When the audience can feel the players actually competing, the format is broken.

[curious] Which means the underlying split with Close is about who's in charge.

[confident] That's it exactly. Johnstone believed the audience should be the final judge — that if they're not engaged, you've failed regardless of how pure the work was. Close believed the art should lead and the audience would follow it upward. [pause] Neither one is wrong. They just placed the bet differently.

[warm] And the thing about that split — it's not really about improv. Anyone who makes anything is standing in that same room. Do you serve what people respond to right now, or do you make the thing you believe in and trust them to come to it?

[contemplative] Johnstone's answer was to build a machine where an audience's response was the score. And whatever you think of that, more people on earth have seen Theatresports than any other improv format. It's the front door. For most of the world, it's what improv is.

[matter-of-fact] Which is why running it badly matters more than running any other format badly.
