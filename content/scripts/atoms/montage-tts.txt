[curious] So there's a format that's basically the opposite of the Harold. No opening, no structure, no group games. Just... scenes.

[teaching] Montage. Will Hines describes it as "suggestion and go." You take the suggestion, someone walks out, and you start playing. That's the whole spec.

[curious] That sounds less like a format and more like the absence of one.

[matter-of-fact] It kind of is. And that's exactly what makes it hard. The Harold gives you a to-do list. Opening, three scenes, group game, second beats, group game, third beats. You always know what comes next. Montage gives you nothing. You have to decide, live, what this show needs.

[curious] So what's it actually for? Because if you're not practicing structure, what are you practicing?

[teaching] You're practicing reading the room. Not the audience — the show. In a Harold, when the third beat comes around, you're connecting because the form says connect now. In a montage, if a connection happens, it's because someone was listening across the whole night and thought, oh, that's the same thing as the scene twelve minutes ago. The form doesn't prompt you. You have to notice.

[contemplative] There's something almost unfair about that. The Harold protects you from your own inattention.

[emphatic] Right, and that's the trade. Harold has low freedom and high scaffolding. Montage has high freedom and no scaffolding. Neither is better. They're doing different jobs.

[curious] Okay, so is montage one thing, or are there versions of it?

[teaching] Roughly three flavors, and I think naming them is genuinely useful because teams often think they're doing one and are actually doing another.

[teaching] Unconnected montage: scene after scene, each one standing alone. The only thing tying them together is the suggestion, and maybe not even that. Every ounce of skill goes into making individual scenes excellent. That's it. That's the whole game.

[curious] Which sounds simple but is probably brutal.

[matter-of-fact] Brutal, because you have nowhere to hide. There's no callback to bail you out, no group game to reset the energy. A weak scene is just a weak scene, sitting there.

[teaching] Second flavor: connected montage. Scenes start referencing each other. Echoing. Sometimes you revisit a scene for a second beat. And the key word is emerge — those connections aren't assigned, they arise because performers are tracking the whole show. The best montages leverage lessons learned in a Harold. You're doing Harold moves without Harold telling you to.

[curious] And the third?

[teaching] Thematic montage. Every scene explores one theme from a different angle. The theme becomes the game of the show. Suggestion is "keys" — you get a locksmith scene, a teenager finally getting the car keys, someone locked out of their own house at two in the morning. Different worlds, different characters, but all of them are about access. Permission. Who gets let in.

[contemplative] That's beautiful when it works.

[emphatic] And it's a disaster when it's forced. Which is where I want to spend a minute, because this is the main failure mode.

[curious] Go.

[teaching] The bad thematic montage is where somebody decides in scene two what the show is about, and then everyone starts servicing the idea instead of playing the scene. You get four scenes of people making thesis statements. "Trust is really about vulnerability." Nobody's in a relationship, nobody wants anything, everybody's illustrating.

[matter-of-fact] The theme should be visible to the audience on the walk to the car. Not announced in scene three.

[curious] What about the other direction — the montage that's just fine and never becomes anything?

[teaching] That's the more common failure, honestly. Freedom collapses into drift. Scenes end because someone got tired, not because the scene hit its peak. Nothing recurs because nobody was listening. It's twenty-five minutes of adequate.

[serious] And here's the uncomfortable part. Montage is what teams graduate to, and it's also what teams fall back into when discipline slips. Those look identical from the outside. Same format. The difference is entirely internal — whether the choices are choices or just defaults.

[curious] So the pedagogy hierarchy is real. Harold first, montage after.

[teaching] That's the standard line — UCB, iO, you train on Harold, you perform montage. And there's real logic to it. But I'd push back slightly on the framing, because "montage is the relaxed alternative" makes it sound like the lesser form. It isn't. It's a form where all the structural decisions moved from the format into your head. That's not less demanding. It's differently demanding.

[curious] Then why is Harold the training ground?

[matter-of-fact] Because Harold forces reps. You will practice second beats whether you feel like it or not. Montage lets you avoid every skill you're bad at, forever, and never notice.

[teaching] There's also a reason montage wins in performance. Audiences who've never seen improv can follow it instantly — no structural literacy required. They're not waiting for the pattern to pay off. They're just watching scenes. And scenes can breathe. In a Harold you sometimes edit a scene that's going great because the form needs to move on.

[curious] Where does the Armando sit in all this?

[teaching] It's a montage with an engine. A monologist tells a true story off the suggestion, the ensemble plays scenes inspired by it, then the monologist comes back with a new story — sometimes sparked by what they just watched. Repeat. That's ASSSSCAT. It solves montage's biggest problem, which is where does the next scene come from, by putting a person on stage whose whole job is generating raw material.

[contemplative] So it's freedom with a fuel source.

[emphatic] Exactly. And that's the thing I'd leave a team with. Montage isn't a format you perform. It's a format you steer. Everything that makes scenes work — game, heightening, relationship, editing, listening — none of it changed. What changed is that nothing is telling you when to do which one.

[teaching] The Harold makes you a good soldier. Montage asks whether you can command.
