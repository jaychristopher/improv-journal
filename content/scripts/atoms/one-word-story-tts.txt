[curious] Okay, so a circle of people, one word each, telling a story. That sounds like a warmup.

[teaching] It looks like a warmup. It's actually the most precise diagnostic tool in the building. Everyone stands in a circle. Going around, each person adds exactly one word to a single continuing story. No conferring, no skipping, no sneaking in two words. Punctuation you say out loud — "period," "comma" — or you just let a pause do it.

[curious] And you run it for what, a couple minutes?

[matter-of-fact] Two, three minutes. Or until the story ends. Which — if the group is actually listening — it will do on its own. Nobody decides to end it. It just arrives somewhere and stops.

[curious] Quick check, because I always mix these up. Is this the same as One-Word Scene?

[teaching] Different animal entirely. One-Word Scene is two players doing a scene where every line is a single word. That's compression — how much can you carry in one syllable. This is a whole circle narrating. That's surrender — giving up the plot. Groups ask for one and get the other constantly. Worth keeping straight.

[curious] So what makes the circle version work?

[emphatic] One word is not enough to steer with. [short pause] That's the whole engine. You can nudge the grammar. You can push toward a noun. But you cannot impose a direction. Which makes it the cleanest demonstration you'll ever see that a story can be built by people who each control almost none of it.

[contemplative] And it exposes steering faster than any scene work does.

[curious] Meaning?

[teaching] Say somebody decides the story should be about a dragon. They will keep trying to bend the sentence back toward dragons. And everyone hears it. The sentence starts fighting its own grammar — it gets lumpy, it tangles, someone downstream has to say "and" three words in a row just to keep it upright. You can *hear* a person trying.

[serious] In a scene, that exact same move is invisible. It just feels like the scene is going badly. Nobody can name it. You get in the car afterward and go, "that one didn't work," and you never find out why. Here, it has a sound.

[curious] What are you saying to them while it's running?

[matter-of-fact] Short things. "Take the word that finishes the sentence you were handed." "Don't be funny, be grammatical." [short pause] "You are allowed to say 'the.'"

[warm] And my favorite: "Say it before you decide it."

[curious] That last one's doing a lot of work.

[teaching] Because that's the actual discipline. Your first word is connected to the sentence in front of you. Your second thought is connected to your idea of a good story. And your idea of a good story is a fantasy you built in the two seconds while you waited for your turn — it has nothing to do with what nine other people just said.

[contemplative] The stories that work are almost always the ones where nobody tried. Groups don't believe that until they hear it. So you run it twice. Once where everyone reaches, once where everyone accepts. The accepting one is better. It's also funnier, which is the part that stings.

[curious] What are the ways it falls apart?

[matter-of-fact] Three, mostly. First: everybody ending the sentence. Each person drops a full stop because a complete sentence feels like a job well done. You end up with eleven short declarative statements and no story. Coach them to leave the sentence open. Hand the next person something unfinished.

[curious] That's generous, actually. Handing someone an incomplete thing.

[warm] It is. It's the whole job.

[teaching] Second failure: comedy tax. Somebody drops in a word chosen purely to get a laugh. "Banana." "Suddenly." Somebody's name from the room. And it *works* — it gets the laugh. And it costs the story its thread, and the next four players spend their turns cleaning it up. They pay for that laugh and the person who got it doesn't.

[curious] And the third?

[emphatic] Refusing to say small words. Players get "of" or "and" and feel like they wasted their turn. Like they got handed a dud.

[serious] Those turns are the ones holding the sentence together. A group that understands that is halfway to understanding support. Because that's what support actually is most of the time — it isn't the brilliant reframe, it's being the connective tissue so the thing someone else is building doesn't collapse.

[curious] Are there variations worth running?

[teaching] One sentence each instead of one word — easier, closer to how a Story Spine round moves. And with a stem: start from "once upon a time" and just see whether the circle finds the shape without being told.

[confident] But the interesting one is two words each.

[curious] That sounds like almost no change.

[emphatic] It changes the game completely. Two words is just barely enough to steer with. And you watch the group start steering — immediately, unconsciously, all of them. The dragon person can now actually get their dragon. The stories get worse and everyone can feel it getting worse and nobody can quite say why.

[contemplative] Nothing about the circle changed. Same people, same rules, same room. Only the size of a turn.

[teaching] Which is the thing I'd take off stage. The amount of control you have in a conversation is mostly a function of how big your turns are. Give yourself a long enough turn and you will steer — toward your point, your anecdote, the thing you were waiting to say. Not because you're a bad listener. Because the room made it possible.

[serious] One word at a time, you can't. You have to take what you were handed.

[warm] And the story ends up somewhere none of you would have gone alone.
