[curious] So this is the one everybody cites but nobody seems to have finished.

[teaching] That's about right. And the interesting part is that not finishing it is almost fine, because the sections don't build. Four of them, plus a memoir opening and an appendix, and they're close to independent. You can walk in through any door.

[curious] Okay, but if I've got one evening, which door?

[matter-of-fact] Status. Read that section, close the book, and you'll have gotten more than most people get from the whole thing. It's the reason "status" is a technical term in improv at all. Before that, status meant rank — who's the boss. Johnstone makes it a behaviour. Something you transact, moment to moment, in how you hold your head and whether you finish your sentences.

[curious] And that section's held up?

[emphatic] Remarkably. Sixty years of social psychology since, and none of it has embarrassed him. That's because he was describing, not explaining. Here's a thing people do, here's how to see it. Descriptions age well. Theories don't.

[curious] What about the section everyone skips?

[teaching] Two candidates. Narrative Skills is the least-read and probably the most useful — it's about how stories actually get assembled. Reincorporation. The break in normality. The mechanics of why a scene feels like it went somewhere instead of just going on.

[short pause]

[contemplative] And then Masks and Trance, which is the one people skip on purpose. It's the strangest part of the book and the most revealing about what Johnstone thought he was up to — which was closer to lifting inhibition than to teaching comedy.

[curious] That's the part where he gets a bit mystical, right? The claims about what's happening in your brain under a mask?

[matter-of-fact] Right, and that's where you have to bring your own qualification, because the book doesn't offer one. He asserts a lot about what's happening psychologically, and the assertions arrive with much more confidence than evidence. The generous read — and I think the correct one — is that he's a practitioner describing an effect he'd reliably produced. Not a theory of it. A field report.

[curious] So the observations survived and the explanations didn't.

[teaching] Same book. Same voice. Same confidence. That's the thing to hold onto, because the confidence gives you no signal about which is which. You have to sort it yourself.

[curious] What about the opening? The autobiographical stuff — is that just throat-clearing before he gets to the exercises?

[emphatic] That's the most common misread, and it costs you the whole argument. Notes on Myself is Johnstone on his own education and why he came to distrust it. And underneath it is the claim everything else depends on: the problem isn't that you lack imagination. The problem is that you have a censor.

[short pause]

[teaching] Skip that, and you read the rest of the book as techniques for being more creative. Which is exactly backwards. They're not techniques for adding anything. They're ways of getting out of your own way.

[contemplative] That reframe is the whole book, honestly. Spontaneity isn't something you summon. It's something you stop blocking.

[curious] Which connects to the bit everyone quotes secondhand — the cult of the obvious.

[warm] The observation that the improviser straining to be original produces the exact same tired ideas as every other person straining to be original. Your "weird" idea is everyone's weird idea. The obvious one is actually yours.

[matter-of-fact] And that travels off stage more cleanly than almost anything in improv. Every meeting where somebody's sitting on the plain, true, useful thing because it doesn't sound clever enough.

[curious] So what's not in the book? Because I came to improv through long form, and I assume he's got opinions.

[teaching] He's got none, in here. No long form, no Harold, almost nothing about ensembles bigger than a scene. The American traditions most readers arrive from either hadn't happened yet or were happening independently, elsewhere, at the same time.

[curious] Not even Theatresports? That's what he's famous for.

[matter-of-fact] Barely present. He invented it around the same period, but the book that documents it properly is the sequel. If you came to Impro wanting the format, you'll find the philosophy underneath the format and not the format.

[curious] Then what is the sequel, exactly? People treat it like a second edition.

[emphatic] That's the common mistake and it's worth correcting. Impro for Storytellers is a genuinely different book — practical where this one is discursive, organised around running a company and running a session. Read Impro for the argument. Read the sequel for the practice. If you only ever read one, ask yourself whether you need to be convinced or need to run a workshop on Thursday.

[curious] Anything else that dates it?

[matter-of-fact] The examples come from British drama schools in the sixties and seventies. And the tone about students is occasionally severe in a way no contemporary teacher would adopt. Neither undermines the content, but both are audible. You hear the room he was in.

[contemplative] Which was, notably, not a comedy venue. He built most of this at the Royal Court in London — a new-writing theatre. The exercises were designed to get actors and writers unstuck. That they became a comedy form happened later and somewhere else.

[curious] Somewhere else being?

[teaching] Calgary. He moved to Canada in the seventies, taught at the university, founded Loose Moose. The book sits exactly at that join — written in Britain out of studio work with actors, published just as the Canadian company that would carry the ideas outward was getting going.

[curious] So who should actually read it?

[warm] Almost anybody, because it doesn't read like a manual. It reads like a very opinionated person thinking out loud, which is why it's stayed in print for over forty years.

[matter-of-fact] The catch is retrieval. It's not organised for it. Exercises are buried inside arguments, the sections don't build, there's no route through it for someone who wants a curriculum. Teachers tend to use it alongside Spolin rather than instead of her — she supplies the structure, he supplies the reasons.

[curious] And the single line you'd take out of it.

[contemplative] "Improvisers usually try not to be altered. We need to be altered."

[short pause]

[serious] Which is not really about improv. That's about walking into any room already knowing who you'll be when you leave.
