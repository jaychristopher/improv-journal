[curious] I want to talk about the improv book that isn't an improv book.

[teaching] Improv Wisdom. Patricia Ryan Madson, 2005. Thirteen maxims, thirteen short chapters, and almost no theatre vocabulary anywhere in it. Say Yes. Just Show Up. Pay Attention. Start Anywhere. Be Average. Take Care of Each Other.

[teaching] It's the one that stops people. The claim is that when you reach for the brilliant answer, you get the strained one — the answer that's trying. Reach for the obvious answer and you get something a real person would actually say. Which, on stage, is almost always the better offer.

[matter-of-fact] Every player has been in the scene where somebody is visibly hunting for the clever line. You can watch them do it. The scene stops while they shop.

[curious] So this is Be Simple with a different name on it.

[teaching] Essentially, yes. She arrives at it from ordinary life rather than from scene work, but it's the same instruction. And that's the pattern of the whole book — these are improv principles with the improv sanded off.

[contemplative] Madson taught at Stanford for decades. The book lives in the d.school library as a design-thinking text, which tells you who found it and what they did with it. It's a small book. [curious] Okay, but the subtitle. Don't prepare, just show up. That's either provocative or just wrong.

[emphatic] It's the weakest thing on the cover and it invites exactly the objection you'd make. Taken literally, don't prepare is bad advice for most things people actually do. If you apply it to a surgery rota or a mortgage application, you've read a provocation as a rule.

[curious] Which is an argument about over-preparation, not against preparing.

[matter-of-fact] Right. And the book is stronger the moment you read it that way. She's consistently talking about attention, not laziness. The thing you're giving up is the script — not the competence.

[contemplative] Every player knows the version of this. You walk into a set with a character you've been polishing all week. Somebody hands you an offer that has nothing to do with it. [curious] That's a real failure, though. It's not just a book's framing device.

[teaching] It's one of the most common ones. And it doesn't stay on stage. It's the meeting where you've drafted your point so thoroughly that you deliver it after the conversation has moved past it. Everybody has done that.

[curious] So who should actually read this?

[matter-of-fact] Somebody who wants what improv teaches without any intention of performing. That's the honest answer.

[emphatic] If you're trying to get better at improv, there is almost nothing operational here. No exercises. No diagnosis. Nothing about why a scene died or how to find the game. It's not a craft book and it's not pretending to be one — it takes thirteen maxims out of improv training and points them at ordinary life. It's closer to a practical philosophy book that happens to have come from a theatre.

[curious] Which is either the achievement or the limitation.

[teaching] The achievement, I'd say. Somebody with no interest in performing can read it end to end without ever feeling they've wandered into someone else's hobby. That's hard to do. It's the clearest demonstration anybody has managed that these principles don't need a stage to make sense.

[contemplative] Where Johnstone uses theatre to illuminate life and lets you draw the line yourself, Madson just addresses life directly. She's the bridge text. If you've ever tried to explain to a non-improviser why this thing you do on Thursday nights changed how you handle your family, this is the book that does it for you.

[curious] The other objection is that it's slight. Thirteen maxims that sound like things you already knew.

[matter-of-fact] That's fair and I'd take it seriously. Pay Attention. Take Care of Each Other. Read as a list, those are fortune cookies.

Start Anywhere is aimed at a real problem: the inertia of waiting for the right action when any action would break it.

[emphatic] So it depends what you want from it. As a reference, it's thin. There's nothing to look up. But as something you run for a week — pick one maxim, live inside it, see what it does — it holds up considerably better than its length suggests.

[confident] That's the book's actual design. It's short because it wants to be carried, not consulted. Say Yes for a week and watch how often your first instinct was to decline something that would have been fine.

[contemplative] And that's the real claim under all thirteen of them. Not that improv is a useful metaphor for life. That the reason these principles work on stage is that they were always principles about being a person, and the stage is just where they're impossible to fake.
