[curious] Okay, I want to talk about the format everybody has an opinion about. Micetro.

[teaching] Johnstone's elimination show. Eight to twelve people start. The audience votes somebody out every round. Down to a final two or three, and then there's a winner.

[curious] A winner. In improv.

[matter-of-fact] A winner. In improv.

[curious] So the obvious objection first. Isn't this just the thing Chicago spent forty years telling us not to do?

[teaching] That's the fight, yeah. Del Close's line is that the ensemble succeeds or fails together. You put individual elimination in the room, and you've taught people to play for themselves instead of for the scene. Which — I don't think that's a dumb objection. It's the strongest objection there is.

[curious] But you're going to defend it.

[confident] I'm going to say Johnstone was solving a different problem. He watched ensemble shows and saw people coasting. Standing on the backline. Editing when it got hot. Supporting — quote unquote — instead of exposing themselves. And the thing about ensemble ethics is they give you perfect cover for that. You're not hiding, you're serving the group.

[short pause]

[teaching] Micetro removes the cover. His line was that it was designed to make improvisers take risks. Not to make them competitive — to make them *findable*.

[curious] Findable.

[matter-of-fact] The audience is looking at you specifically. Not the team. You.

[contemplative] And every improviser knows the difference between those two feelings. There's the show where you had a rough set but the team was great, so it's fine. And then there's the one where you know — you personally were the drag. Micetro just makes the second one public.

[curious] Okay so how does it actually run. Because I think people picture something more gladiatorial than it is.

[teaching] It's calmer than it sounds. There's a director — the Micetro, that's where the name comes from — who runs the evening. They assign scenes. Duos, trios, sometimes a solo challenge. And crucially they mix and match, so you're playing with different partners every round. Two to four scenes, then the audience votes. Applause, cards, hands, whatever. One person goes.

[curious] And there's a bit with the exits.

[warm] Johnstone loved a theatrical exit. Make a meal of it. Which is not a small detail — it's the pressure valve. If leaving is a performance, leaving isn't a failure. It's your last scene.

[curious] What does the show feel like as it shrinks?

[emphatic] That's the whole thing. That's the format's actual argument. You start with twelve, you end with two, and the narrowing *is* the story. A montage doesn't have that. A Harold doesn't have that. Great scenes, no arc. Micetro has rising tension baked into the structure — the field gets smaller, the stakes get louder, and there's a climax that isn't just "the lights went down."

[teaching] And the audience isn't watching. They're deciding. That's a completely different quality of attention.

[curious] So where does it go wrong?

[serious] Two ways, and they're mirror images. The first is showboating. You feel the votes coming, you decide the way to survive is to be the biggest thing on stage. So you steamroll. You take every offer and turn it into a bit about you.

[matter-of-fact] And it doesn't work.

[curious] It doesn't?

[confident] Audiences vote out the person who made the scene worse. Almost every time. They can't always articulate it — they don't have the vocabulary for "he didn't accept the offer" — but they *felt* the scene get worse when you did that. Which means the actual winning strategy in an elimination format is generosity. Make your partner look brilliant. That's the joke at the center of Micetro. The competition rewards the thing competition supposedly destroys.

[curious] And the other failure?

[teaching] Playing not to lose. Getting so small nobody notices you. And that gets you eliminated too, just more quietly. There's nowhere to stand in Micetro where you're safe.

[short pause]

[curious] What about the emotional side. Getting cut in front of a room.

[contemplative] That's the real demand. Not the courage to go out there — the self-management afterward. Because there's a version of you that gets voted out in round two and spends the rest of the night in the bar rewriting the scene. And that's not the format doing something to you. That's you finding out what you do with a public judgment.

[warm] Which, honestly, is worth knowing. You will be evaluated in front of people who don't owe you an explanation. Job interviews. Pitches. Somebody just deciding they don't want the second date. Micetro is a two-hour rehearsal for being assessed and not getting to argue.

[curious] So when do you actually program it?

[matter-of-fact] Big cast. That's the practical one. If you've got fourteen people and a standard format, half of them get two lines all night. Micetro gives everyone real stage time and then thins the herd honestly.

[teaching] Also: audiences who like stakes. Sports crowds, game show crowds, people who want to be *in* it. And when your company has gotten comfortable. When everyone's competent and nothing's dangerous. Micetro is a good way to find out who's actually been taking risks and who's been hiding inside the ensemble.

[curious] Last thing. Does the Chicago objection ever just — win?

[contemplative] I don't think either side wins. I think they make different improvisers. Johnstone's camp produces performers who can walk on cold, with a stranger, and be interesting immediately, because they've had to. Close's camp produces people who can build something over an hour with a group they trust. Those are both real skills and they are not the same skill.

[emphatic] But here's the thing the ensemble argument has to answer. When nobody's individually accountable, nobody's individually excellent. "We're a team" is true, and it's also the most comfortable place in the world to be mediocre.
