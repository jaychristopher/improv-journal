[curious] I want to talk about the book everyone cites and half of everyone finishes.

[teaching] The Viewpoints Book. Bogart and Landau, 2005. It's the reason you've heard the word Viewpoints at all. Before it, the method lived in a handful of companies and a few university programmes. After it, it was in hundreds.

[curious] So it's the standard source.

[matter-of-fact] It's the standard source, and it's not the original source. Those are different things, and the gap between them is most of what's interesting here.

[curious] Start with what's actually between the covers.

[teaching] Roughly the first third is vocabulary. Nine physical Viewpoints, sorted into Viewpoints of Time and Viewpoints of Space, with a vocal set introduced later. Each channel gets a definition and then an exercise sequence — how you open a session, how you introduce one channel at a time, what you say to a group that's stalled out in the middle of the floor.

[emphatic] And that's the unusual part. It promises a practical guide and it delivers one. Most theatre methodology books promise that and give you an argument. This one gives you instructions you can run on Thursday.

[curious] So it's a training manual.

[contemplative] The first half is. The second half is something else, and it's the part most readers don't expect and the part that makes the book worth owning.

[curious] Composition.

[teaching] Composition. You give a group a set of constraints — you have ninety seconds, you must include three entrances, nobody speaks — and they build a short piece that satisfies them. [short pause] The constraints are arbitrary on purpose. That's not a flaw in the exercise, that's the mechanism. A group that's been given ninety seconds and three entrances cannot deliberate its way to a result. There's no time to have the meeting.

[curious] Which is the same reason we do time limits in improv.

[warm] Exactly the same reason. And here's what Composition solves structurally. The commonest complaint about Viewpoints training is that people spend six weeks walking around a room changing their tempo and nothing ever gets made. That complaint is fair, and the answer to it is in the second half of the book. The training generates raw physical material. Composition is the structure that turns it into something with a shape.

[emphatic] Teachers who never get past the first half are the reason the objection keeps getting made.

[curious] Landau's half?

[matter-of-fact] Her hand is all over it. She's a director of new work, not a theorist, and the Composition material reads like it was built by somebody who had to generate stageable material on a deadline. It has that quality. Nothing in it is there to be admired.

[curious] What about the rest?

[teaching] There's a short section on Source Work — the research a company does into the world of a play before rehearsal starts. Images, music, historical material, anything that gives everybody a shared reference point.

[contemplative] It's the quietest useful thing in the book and it's also the fairest criticism of it. Source Work is the part of the method that connects the training to actually producing a production, and it gets a handful of pages. If you're coming from a devising practice you'll want considerably more than is there.

[curious] Who's this book actually for?

[matter-of-fact] It's a director's book and a teacher's book. Bogart taught directors at Columbia for years and you can feel who she's writing to — the person facilitating the room.

[emphatic] Which means if you're reading it to improve your own performing, you'll find the training described but almost no account of what it feels like from inside. That's not an oversight, it's the addressee. Somebody running a group will find sessions they can lead by the end of the week. Somebody looking to get better on stage will find a manual for a job they're not doing.

[curious] Is it argued or asserted?

[matter-of-fact] Asserted. They state what Viewpoints does and how to do it. They don't defend it against alternatives and they don't report what happens when it fails. If you want the critical case you have to supply it yourself.

[curious] Okay. The attribution thing.

[serious] Mary Overlie. Six Viewpoints, built in the seventies out of postmodern dance at NYU's Experimental Theater Wing. Bogart and Landau credit her clearly — the book is more honest about this than the book's reputation is. But the book is what travelled, and so the method travelled under Bogart's name. "Bogart Viewpoints" is now what most people mean by the word.

[teaching] And it's worth saying out loud every time you teach it: the nine channels are an adaptation, made for actors, of a framework that was made for dancers, by someone else.

[curious] Is the adaptation neutral?

[emphatic] No, and this is the thing. Overlie's six are presented as a way of seeing. They're deliberately resistant to being turned into a syllabus — that resistance is part of the content. Bogart and Landau's nine are a syllabus. The sorting into Time and Space is their organising decision, not something inherited, and it's precisely what makes the material teachable in a fixed order.

[contemplative] Something's gained. The method became teachable at scale — that's not nothing, that's why it exists in hundreds of programmes. And something's lost. [pause] If you only ever meet the nine, you don't know what the something was. Worse, meeting the nine first makes the six look like a reduction of them. Which reverses the actual order.

[curious] So what do you buy?

[matter-of-fact] The 2005 paperback is the only substantive edition. Later printings are unchanged, so a used copy is the same book — don't pay for new.

[teaching] Then read Overlie's own account, *Standing in Space*, which came out eleven years later and reads completely differently. More philosophical, less instructional, pointedly not a systematisation. Anyone using Viewpoints seriously should read both, in that order, because the nine are a translation and the original deserves to be met on its own terms.

[curious] And *A Director Prepares*?

[warm] Different animal entirely. Seven essays on the conditions for making work, no exercises in it at all. It's the better book if you want to understand why Bogart works the way she does, and the worse book if you need a session to run on Thursday night.

[curious] Bottom line for an improviser.

[teaching] Borrow the vocabulary — it's genuinely good. The value isn't the list, it's the separation. Telling somebody to be more interesting gives them nothing to do. Asking them to change only their spatial relationship to the other person is an instruction that can be carried out, and the interesting thing tends to follow anyway.

[matter-of-fact] The Composition half transfers to anyone who has to make something with other people under time pressure. That's most of us, most weeks.

[contemplative] But the ensemble training and the Source Work assume a rehearsal room, a company, and weeks of it. If you're hoping to drop this into a two-hour workshop, the fit is going to be awkward, and knowing that going in is worth more than finding it out on the floor.
