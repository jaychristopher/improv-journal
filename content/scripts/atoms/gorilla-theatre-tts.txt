[curious] Okay, so Gorilla Theatre. I've heard the name, I've seen the foam banana on a shelf in a theatre lobby somewhere. What actually is it?

[teaching] It's Johnstone's format where improvisers take turns directing each other. You step up, you pitch a scene to the audience — "two rival pastry chefs at a funeral" — and if they approve it, you cast your fellow performers and you direct it. Live. Out loud. You do not perform in your own scene.

[curious] So I'm standing at the side calling adjustments while other people play.

[teaching] Side-coaching, editing, letting it breathe when it needs air. And then the audience votes on whether the scene was good. If it was, you get a banana — a foam one, a trophy, a token, doesn't matter. If it wasn't, you do a forfeit. Something public and humiliating that the audience or the host picks.

[curious] Hang on. The audience votes on the scene. But the forfeit goes to the director.

[matter-of-fact] Only the director. The performers who actually played it walk away clean either way.

[contemplative] [pause] That's the whole format right there, isn't it.

[emphatic] That's the whole format. Everything else is scaffolding around that one asymmetry.

[curious] Why build it that way? It seems almost unfair. I could pitch something beautiful and get handed three people who bulldoze it.

[teaching] You could. And you'd still eat the forfeit. But think about what that does to you before you pitch. Johnstone noticed something about improvisers — we avoid directing. We'll happily play in anyone's scene, but ask us to shape somebody else's work and we get shy about it. Because the moment you direct, you're accountable for a result you don't fully control.

[curious] Which is exactly the thing nobody wants.

[teaching] Right, so the format just refuses to let you dodge it. It says: you are responsible for the whole show, not just your own performance. And then it puts your dignity on the line to make sure you mean it.

[conversational] I want to sit with the pitch for a second, because I think that's the part people underestimate.

[curious] It feels like the throat-clearing before the real thing.

[emphatic] It's a negotiation. The audience is telling you what they want to see, and you have to actually listen. If you pitch something they don't want, you have already failed. The scene hasn't started and you've lost.

[curious] So a bad pitch isn't a slow start, it's a finished loss.

[matter-of-fact] The vote at the end is often just the audience confirming what they told you at the top.

[contemplative] What makes a good pitch, then?

[teaching] Specific enough to direct, open enough to play. "Two rival pastry chefs at a funeral" gives you a status game, a location with rules, and a reason nobody can raise their voice. But it doesn't tell the performers who they are or what happens. Compare that to "a scene about grief" — nothing to direct — or a fully mapped-out premise where the performers are just reciting your outline. Both fail, from opposite directions.

[curious] And you find out which one you did in real time.

[warm] [short pause] Publicly. With a forfeit waiting.

[curious] So what actually goes wrong when a group runs this?

[teaching] The most common one: directors who won't direct. They pitch, they cast, and then they stand there and watch, hoping it goes well. That's the timid version — you've taken on all the risk and none of the control.

[curious] Which is the worst possible trade.

[matter-of-fact] It's the trade most people make the first time. The fix is to intervene early. Not politely, at the end, when it's already dead — sixty seconds in, when it's still shapeable.

[curious] What's the other failure?

[teaching] The opposite. The director who's really performing through other people. Constant instructions, puppeteering, so much calling out that the audience is watching you instead of the scene. That reads as ego, and audiences vote it down hard.

[contemplative] So the skill is knowing which one you're doing.

[emphatic] The skill is knowing when to intervene and when to shut up. That's not a rule you can write down, that's a thing you learn by getting it wrong in front of people who then make you sing an opera about your worst date.

[warm] [pause] Which, honestly, is the fastest teacher I know of.

[curious] And there's a real argument against all of this, isn't there. The Chicago tradition would hate this.

[teaching] They would say directing is antithetical to improv. In a Harold, in a montage, the ensemble collectively discovers what the show is about. Nobody controls it. One person imposing a vision is a violation of the whole premise — the ensemble *is* the director.

[curious] And Johnstone's answer?

[matter-of-fact] That ensembles without individual accountability produce timid scenes. Nobody makes a bold choice because nobody's on the hook for it. Everyone's being supportive and nothing is happening.

[contemplative] So both traditions are staring at the same problem.

[emphatic] The exact same problem — how do you get performers to actually commit. And they built opposite machines to solve it. Chicago distributes the responsibility until everyone owns it. Johnstone concentrates it until one person can't escape it. Neither is wrong. They're two answers to "who pays if this is bad."

[curious] [short pause] That question is not confined to a stage.

[warm] It really isn't. Think about the last meeting where an idea died. Somebody floated something half-formed, the room made agreeable noises, nobody picked it up, and it evaporated. No one failed. That's the tell. When a bad outcome has no owner, you don't get safety, you get vagueness — everyone hedging, nobody committing, and afterward it's genuinely unclear what happened or whose it was.

[teaching] Gorilla Theatre's move is to name the owner before anything starts. You pitched it, so it's yours. And what's interesting is that people pitch *better* when that's true. The risk doesn't make them cautious — it makes them specific.

[contemplative] Because vagueness stops being a safe place to hide.

[matter-of-fact] It's the least safe place to hide. You can't direct a vague idea, and you can't defend one in front of a voting audience.

[curious] So if you're running this with a group — what do you actually want out of it?

[teaching] Directors. That's the point. You run it when you want performers who can shape other people's work and stay in relationship with an audience while they do it. The variety is a bonus — every scene is different because every director has a different sensibility. But the thing you're building is the willingness to be accountable for something you can't fully control.

[contemplative] And you're teaching that by making it cost something.

[emphatic] By making it cost something in public, in front of the people you're trying to please, with a foam banana as the only consolation. [pause] That's not a gimmick. That's the curriculum.
