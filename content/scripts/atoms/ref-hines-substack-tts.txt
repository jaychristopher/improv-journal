[curious] So there's this Substack that keeps coming up whenever improvisers argue about pedagogy online. Improv Nonsense. Will Hines. What is it, actually?

[teaching] It's the closest thing this form has to a living textbook. And I want to be precise about "living," because that's the whole thing. It's not a book. It's not a course. It started as a Tumblr, moved to Substack, and what exists now is over a decade of posts written as they occurred to him. No syllabus. No order. Off-the-cuff observations sitting right next to essays he clearly sweated over.

[curious] That sounds like it could be a mess.

[teaching] It is a mess. [short pause] That's also why it's the best record we have of a teacher arguing with his own tradition in public.

[curious] Say more about that.

[teaching] Hines has taught at UCB since 2005. Performed there the whole time. So he's not a theorist writing about improv from outside — he's a practitioner who has watched thousands of students hit the same walls. And over ten years of writing, he changes his mind. Repeatedly. In public. There are posts in that archive that flatly contradict other posts in that archive.

[curious] And nothing tells you which one he still believes?

[matter-of-fact] Nothing tells you. And the page is more useful for it.

[curious] Okay, but if I open it tomorrow, where do I even start? Because "ten years of unordered posts" is a great way to read nothing.

[teaching] Don't start at the beginning. That's the single most important thing. It's a working archive, not a course — there's no sequence being built toward. Start with whichever post names a problem you currently have.

[curious] And if nothing's currently on fire?

[teaching] Then here's an order. Craft first, arguments second. [pause] The craft posts are where he gives you tools. "Accepting Offers" draws a distinction most classes skip entirely — facts have to be accepted, opinions can be argued with. That's a far more usable rule than "yes, and."

[curious] Wait, that's genuinely different. Because "yes, and" tells you to agree with everything, and half the scenes I've seen die from two people agreeing pleasantly forever.

[emphatic] Right. Your scene partner says you're brothers — that's a fact, you're brothers now. Your scene partner says Mom always liked you best — that's an opinion. You can fight about it. And the fight is the scene.

[curious] What else in the craft pile?

[teaching] "Heightening: Do Both Vertical and Horizontal" — that's a four-quadrant model, and it's the thing that finally makes heightening teachable instead of just "make it bigger." "Relationship vs Game" refuses the either-or that people love to argue about and says you need both. And then "Rewrite History," which is the sharpest thing written anywhere on changeability.

[curious] Changeability meaning...?

[teaching] Most of us learn it as accepting what's arriving. Something happens, you let it change you. Hines pushes further — you can rewrite what already happened. Go back and revise the past of the scene. It's a bigger permission than people realize they have.

[curious] And then the arguments.

[confident] Then the arguments. And these are the reason to read him rather than a manual. "The Harold Has Too Many Scenes" compares the form to a restaurant that only serves nine-course meals, every single night. "Do Openings Ruin Improv?" argues that an opening manufactures false certainty about where a scene is going. "Let's Get Rid of the Pattern Game" is exactly as blunt as it sounds.

[curious] Why craft first, though? Why not lead with the fun stuff?

[matter-of-fact] Because read in that order, the disagreements make sense — you have something to disagree from. Read the arguments cold and it's just a man complaining about improv.

[curious] What about the map? Like, what forms even exist beyond the Harold?

[teaching] "What Forms Are There?" It's the survey, and it's the fastest way to find out how much is out there. If you've only ever done Harolds, it's a little vertiginous.

[curious] Let me ask the obvious one. If I don't train at UCB — if I came up somewhere patient, or organic, or European — is this for me?

[contemplative] Yes, with a caveat. The vocabulary assumes a game-based frame. Game of the scene, heightening — those are used as settled terms, not defended. So occasionally you'll be reading an argument whose premises you don't share.

[curious] Which is annoying.

[teaching] Which is annoying. But the value survives it, because the recurring subject isn't really UCB. The recurring subject is what actually happens to people learning this. Plateaus. The fear of being bad. Why notes don't land. None of that is house-specific. Everyone who has ever stalled out in year three recognizes those posts.

[curious] What's aged badly?

[matter-of-fact] The house-specific stuff assumes an institution that has since come apart. And honestly, some of the posts are just a guy having a thought on a Tuesday. Not everything is load-bearing.

[curious] What's aged well?

[warm] The honesty about not knowing. A lot of improv writing presents a finished theory — here is the system, apply the system. Hines frequently works a problem on the page and doesn't resolve it. He'll write a post and leave the ending open because he genuinely hasn't figured it out.

[curious] That feels risky for a teacher.

[emphatic] It's the opposite of risky, it's the whole credential. He's also, informally, the UCB community's historian. A fair amount of that Substack is the only written record of how that institution taught, changed its mind, and fell apart. Nobody else was writing it down.

[curious] So who is it for?

[teaching] The player who's past the beginner stage and has started noticing that the rules they were given don't cover everything. If you're still learning what a base reality is, this will overwhelm you. If you've been doing this three, five, ten years and you've quietly stopped believing one of the things you were taught — this is a person with standing inside the tradition saying which parts of it he no longer believes.

[contemplative] And that's rarer than either defending the tradition or dismissing it. Defence is easy. Dismissal is easy. Staying inside something for twenty years while publicly disagreeing with it — that takes a kind of stamina.

[curious] Does the same thinking exist in a tidier form?

[matter-of-fact] It does — he wrote a book, and the book is the organized version. But the book has conclusions. The Substack has the arguing. If you want to watch someone think, you want the arguing.
