[curious] Okay, so a scene starts. Two people, nothing but a suggestion. What's the first thing that actually makes it a scene instead of two people talking?

[teaching] Somebody has to want something from the other person. Not want something in general — want something from *them*. That's the whole engine. Meisner built his entire training around one question: what do you want from the other person?

[curious] Why does the "from the other person" part matter so much? Isn't wanting something enough?

[matter-of-fact] No, and you've watched this fail. "I want to be happy" — nothing happens. "I want to be a better person" — nothing happens. Those are states, not actions. But "I want you to admit you were wrong"? Now I have something to do. Now every line I say is a move toward getting it, and every line you say either gives it to me or doesn't.

[teaching] The specificity is the whole thing. A want has to be aimed at a human being who can grant it or withhold it. That's what turns dialogue into pressure.

[curious] So do I decide this before I walk out? Because that feels like planning, and we're told not to plan.

[warm] You almost never decide it. You discover it. You do three lines, and by line four you notice — oh, I keep asking her if she's okay. I keep checking. I want her to tell me she's fine so I can stop worrying. You didn't choose that. Your behavior chose it, and you're just the first person to read it.

[conversational] Stanislavski asked "what do I want?" Improv flips it: what am I already doing, and what does that tell me about what I want?

[curious] There's a second word that gets thrown around next to this one. Need. Are those the same thing wearing different clothes?

[teaching] They're opposites, usually. Want is conscious, external, specific — the character knows it and chases it. Need is unconscious, internal, and frequently the exact thing the want is preventing.

[emphatic] The character who wants control needs to learn to trust. The character who wants approval needs to find their own worth. They're pulling in opposite directions, and that gap is where the whole thing gets interesting.

[curious] So the want is the engine and the need is —

[contemplative] The destination. The want generates the scene. The need is the arc. And when they don't line up, your character has internal conflict without you having to act internal conflict. It's just there, structurally.

[curious] Give me the mechanics. How does want actually shape what happens beat to beat?

[matter-of-fact] Four things. Two people with competing wants — that's conflict, automatically, no one has to argue about it. A want that gets threatened escalates, which is your heightening. A want that gets satisfied kills the engine, which is often your ending.

[teaching] And the fourth: want ties into game. UCB has this line — the game sticks best when it's tied to fear, desire, shame, or love. That's not decoration. That's saying the pattern lands harder when there's something the character can lose.

[curious] Okay, here's what I want to push on. I've been in a lot of scenes where somebody wants to escape a sinking ship, or find the treasure, or stop the bomb. High stakes, right? Big want.

[emphatic] Big want, wrong location. That's want in the situation, not in the relationship. "I want to escape the burning building" is a plot. "I want you to say you love me before we escape the burning building" is a scene.

[teaching] The building doesn't respond to you. Your partner does. Every want you plant in the environment is a want your scene partner can't play with.

[curious] So the fire is fine as long as it's not what I want.

[warm] The fire is great. Let it burn. Just make sure what you're actually after is standing across from you.

[curious] One more thing. Where does this show up when I'm not on stage?

[contemplative] Every conversation you've had where you left frustrated and couldn't name why. Usually you wanted something from that person — an acknowledgment, a thank you, permission — and you never said it. You circled it. You made small talk about the thing next to it.

[matter-of-fact] Same failure mode as the scene where nothing happens. You had a want. You just kept it off the table.

[curious] Last thing — is this even ours? It feels borrowed.

[matter-of-fact] It is borrowed. Want comes in from Stanislavski and Meisner, from text-based acting. Johnstone was deliberately building an alternative to that — he found objectives limiting and went off toward narrative skills and status instead. There's an appendix in his storytelling book literally called "Fast-Food Stanislavsky Lists."

[teaching] Which is a useful tell. He didn't think objectives were wrong, he thought they were shorthand. A tool you reach for, not a foundation you build on.

[contemplative] So hold it loosely. But when a scene is dying and you can't tell why, the question still works: what do I want from this person, right now, that I haven't asked for yet?
