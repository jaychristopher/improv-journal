[curious] So there's this term that gets thrown around in every note session I've ever been in. Find the game. Play the game. What's the game? And I've nodded along for years without being totally sure I could define it.

[teaching] The game of the scene is the repeatable, heightenable pattern that gives the scene structure and momentum. That's it. Repeatable. Heightenable. Pattern. It's what the scene is about behaviorally — not what it's about topically.

[curious] Say more about that distinction. Behaviorally versus topically.

[teaching] Two people are in an elevator. That's the topic. That's the setup. Now: one of them is claustrophobic, and the other one keeps unconsciously making the space feel smaller. Takes off his backpack, sets it down. Stretches. Mentions how the cables sometimes stick. That second thing — that's the game. The premise is static. The game moves.

[matter-of-fact] UCB draws that line hardest. Premise is the situation, game is the engine inside it. Other traditions don't split it as cleanly, but they're all after the same thing.

[curious] Okay, so how do you find it? Because the version I was taught is the first unusual thing.

[teaching] That's one door. A character reacts unexpectedly to something, you point at that reaction, and then you ask: if this is true, what else is true? If this, then what. It's a great door. It's not the only one.

[curious] What are the others?

[teaching] Behavioral — a character who apologizes for everything. The tic is the game. Relational — the dynamic itself is the pattern. One always defers, one always leads, and that transaction repeats. TJ Jagodowski builds practically his whole practice out of that door. Emotional — someone inappropriately calm, or someone completely devastated by minor inconvenience. Status — the power exchange is the game, who has it and how it moves. Environmental — every room they walk into is smaller than the last one. And mapping, which is treating one thing as another. A breakup played as a hostage negotiation.

[contemplative] Six doors into one room. The one your school taught you first is a line on the map, not the map.

[curious] Right, but finding it is the easy part. I find games all the time and then the scene just... sits there.

[emphatic] Yes. That's the actual work. Finding is the beginning. Playing it is the craft.

[teaching] Heightening first — same pattern, higher stakes. The apologizer starts by apologizing for bumping a chair. Then for the weather. Then for the concept of chairs existing. Same pattern, escalated.

[teaching] But here's the part people skip. Rest beats. You have to come back to base reality between the heightens. If every line is bigger than the last, there's no contrast, and heightening becomes noise. The pattern needs quiet around it to read as a pattern.

[curious] That's the thing I get wrong constantly. I hit the gas and never let up.

[warm] Everybody does. It feels like generosity. It's actually erasure — you're stepping on your own pattern.

[teaching] Then lateral exploration. Instead of bigger, you go sideways. Same pattern, new context. The apologizer at work. At a wedding. At his own trial. You're not escalating, you're mapping the pattern across the world.

[curious] What about the other person in the scene? Because sometimes I feel like I'm just standing there.

[emphatic] You're not standing there. You're doing the harder job. One player explores the pattern, the other one holds the reality — reacts like a human being would, notices that this is unusual. That's the straight-man function, and without it the audience has nothing to measure the weirdness against. Two people playing the game is two people shouting.

[teaching] And then, eventually, the button. The moment the pattern's been fully explored. It breaks, or transcends, or hits one final escalation that resolves the tension. Scenes don't end because time ran out. They end because the game finished.

[serious] Mick Napier would push back on this whole conversation. His warning is that "find the game" turns into a performance block — one more thing you're searching for in your head while your scene partner is right there in front of you, talking. You're not present, you're diagnosing.

[curious] And his alternative?

[teaching] Do something. Fully. Commit hard to a choice, and the pattern reveals itself through the commitment. In his frame the game is a retrospective description of what happened, not a prescription for what to do.

[matter-of-fact] Johnstone won't even use the word. His engine is narrative — reincorporation, tilts, story moving forward and transforming. He'd say game turns improvisers into joke machines instead of storytellers.

[contemplative] And they're not wrong, exactly. UCB sees pattern-and-escalation as the engine of comedy. Johnstone sees story-and-transformation as the engine of drama. Both produce work that's genuinely good.

[curious] So which is it?

[teaching] The tension is real and I don't think you resolve it by picking. Game-awareness sharpens scenes when it's a lens and paralyzes them when it's a mandate. If you're onstage running a checklist — is this the game, is this the game — you've stopped listening, and there's nothing to find anyway.

[emphatic] But both sides land in the same place. The pattern isn't something you retrieve. It's something you notice. Napier says notice by committing. UCB says notice by tracking the unusual. Either way, the material is already in the room. It got there because someone said something and someone else was actually paying attention.

[contemplative] And that's the reason game works on an audience at all. It's cumulative. Each beat builds on the one before it, the pattern gets richer, and somewhere around the third iteration the audience figures it out. Now they're ahead of you. They know what's coming and they're leaning forward waiting to see how you'll deliver it.

[warm] That lean-forward moment — that's not a joke landing. That's a room full of people who've been let in on the structure. You built something they can predict, and then you made the prediction more fun than the surprise would have been.
