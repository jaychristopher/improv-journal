[curious] There's a warm-up where you stand in a circle, keep a beat, and say two numbers. That's the whole game. And somehow it destroys people.

[teaching] It destroys people because the numbers move. Everyone's in a circle, one position is at the top, the rest are numbered around from one. You slap your thighs, clap, slap, clap — steady beat. On the beat you say your own number and then somebody else's. "Number four, number nine." And nine has to answer on the very next beat.

[curious] So it's a call and response.

[teaching] It's a call and response until somebody misses. Then they go to the end of the line — and here's the part that matters — everybody between them and the end shifts up a number. So you were seven. Now you're six. Nobody announced it. The beat didn't stop for you.

[matter-of-fact] So the answer I got ready is wrong.

[teaching] The answer you got ready is always wrong. That's the design. You spend the whole round preparing to say "number seven" and by the time somebody calls you, you're five, and the person who was five is out, and the beat is still going.

[curious] I want to sit in that for a second, because I think that's the actual mechanism. It's not that the game is fast. Plenty of games are fast.

[teaching] Right. Speed alone you can beat with preparation. You can get faster at retrieving a fixed answer. This one takes the fixed answer away. Your own identity in the game keeps changing underneath you, so rehearsal doesn't just cost you time — it produces the wrong output. It's not inefficient. It's actively wrong.

[contemplative] And the rhythm is what makes that true. Without the beat you'd just count. You'd look around, find your position, work it out, answer correctly every time. It'd be a puzzle, and you'd solve it.

[teaching] The beat turns the puzzle into a moving target. That's the entire game. Everything else is decoration.

[curious] So what's it actually training? Because it's not numbers.

[emphatic] Attention is a pool. There's a finite amount, and the part of you doing internal computation is drawing from the same pool as the part of you listening to the room. Every warm-up in the world tells players to stop planning. Stop being in your head. Be present. And players nod, and then go right back into their heads, because nobody ever showed them what the planning was costing.

[teaching] This game shows them. Within four or five rounds, the only people still in are the ones who stopped tracking and started listening. Not because they're better at math. Because they gave up on math.

[curious] Is that visible from outside?

[warm] It's one of the most visible things in improv. You can watch it happen to a specific person. They've been staring at the floor, lips moving slightly, doing the arithmetic. Then they lose a round, and something goes out of them — the shoulders drop, and they look up. They look at the circle instead of the floor.

[warm] And their timing gets better. Immediately. Right after they stop trying to be ready.

[contemplative] That's a rough thing to learn about yourself.

[matter-of-fact] It's a rough thing to learn and it's completely undeniable, which is why it works. Nobody told them. The game told them.

[curious] Okay, but I've seen this go badly. What breaks it?

[teaching] Three things. First, letting the beat drop. If you let players take as long as they want, the constraint's gone and you're running a very boring counting exercise. The rhythm isn't atmosphere, it's the whole load.

[teaching] Second, playing to win. Getting to the top position feels like the goal, so groups start treating it as a competition. And competitive groups get slower, not faster — because competing means caring about the outcome, and caring about the outcome means calculating.

[curious] The thing that makes you want to win is the thing that makes you lose.

[matter-of-fact] Every time.

[teaching] Third one's the serious one. Nobody's eliminated, but sliding down the line is a small public failure, and it happens to you over and over in front of people. In a room that hasn't built any trust yet, that's the wrong first experience. It reads as humiliation instead of play.

[curious] So not week one.

[serious] Not the first ten minutes of week one, definitely. Week three, when people know each other's names and have already been bad at something together. Then it's funny. Then it's a group of people failing in rhythm, which is a completely different event.

[curious] There's also the name.

[matter-of-fact] There's also the name. Traditionally it's called Big Booty, and the top position is Big Booty. In a classroom, a corporate room, a group you met twenty minutes ago — that name is a liability, and nothing in the game depends on it. Big Fish, Zoom Boom, whatever you want. It costs you nothing to change.

[teaching] And honestly, knowing when to change it is the same skill the game claims to teach. Reading the room and responding to what's actually there instead of running the version you rehearsed.

[curious] Give me the variations worth knowing.

[teaching] Names instead of numbers, if the group doesn't know each other. Slower, but you get people learning names under pressure, which sticks better than a go-around. Two beats between calls for a new room, tightening to one as they settle. And a no-elimination version where the numbering stays fixed and you just play through mistakes — you lose the renumbering pressure, but you keep the rhythm work.

[curious] Which half transfers?

That's a collection of individuals taking turns, and they're going to play scenes the same way.
