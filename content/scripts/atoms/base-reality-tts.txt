[teaching] So the first unusual thing has something to be unusual against. Two people at the kitchen table doing their taxes. One of them flinches at a receipt. Everybody clocks the flinch — the audience, the other player, the person who did it — because everything around it was flat.

[curious] Go on.

[teaching] Base reality is the UCB frame, and it says: ground the scene so you can spot the unusual thing already inside it. The odd detail is in there. Your job is to find it. Platform is Johnstone's term, and it points the other direction — the platform exists so it can be knocked over. Somebody tilts it, and the story is whatever happens after.

[curious] So one is hunting and one is waiting.

[emphatic] Which is exactly the misfire in a mixed room. The platform player establishes normality and waits for someone to disturb it. [pause] And the scene just sits there while each one waits for the other.

[curious] Okay, but there's a whole school that thinks this entire conversation is a trap. Napier. Annoyance.

[curious] So which is it?

[teaching] The disagreement shrinks the moment you separate *how* it gets established from *whether*. What Napier is attacking is base reality announced. "Honey, we've been married eleven years, and here we are, in the kitchen." That's a caption, not a scene.

[warm] Base reality demonstrated costs nothing and stalls nothing. Same information, arriving through behavior — how you handle the object, how you say the other person's name, what you assume you're allowed to touch. The behavior *is* the bold choice. [emphatic] So the rule isn't skip the platform. It's stop narrating it. If the first thirty seconds of your scene could be replaced with a caption, the objection is about you.
