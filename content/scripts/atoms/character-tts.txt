[curious] So a friend of mine, first year of classes, keeps saying she "did a character" in a scene. And I never know what she means.

[teaching] She probably doesn't either, and that's not an insult. Character is one of those words that gets used to cover about four different things. Let's name it precisely. A character is a distinct identity you're inhabiting — its own way of seeing the world, its own wants, its own body. The line that matters: the performer observes and responds. The character lives.

[curious] Okay, but that's still abstract. What's actually in there?

[matter-of-fact] Four things. Point of view — how they see everything, their filter on reality. Want — what they need from the other person in this scene. Physicality — posture, gesture, voice, how they take up space. And status default — where they habitually sit in the pecking order.

[curious] And you run through those before you go out?

[emphatic] No. God, no. That's the trap. They emerge through action. Napier's version is "do something, anything, for yourself first." A limp. A tilt of the head. A specific way of sitting down. A physical choice generates a character faster than any amount of planning ever will. The body leads and the character shows up behind it.

[curious] Here's what I actually want to know. Of those four — which one goes missing?

[contemplative] [short pause] Want. Every time.

[curious] Why that one?

[teaching] Because three of them are visible and one isn't. Point of view shows. Physicality shows. Status shows — you can watch a person defer or take up room. A performer who's chosen those can feel themselves doing it. An audience can see it happening. Want produces nothing visible on its own. So it's the component performers most often believe they have and most often don't.

[curious] What does that look like from the audience?

[matter-of-fact] A scene that looks completely populated and does not move. Two vivid people. Strong physicalities, clear attitudes, talking for two minutes, going absolutely nowhere. Because neither of them needs anything from the other, and so there is nothing that can change.

[conversational] You've watched that scene. You've maybe been in it and thought the problem was you weren't being funny enough.

[curious] So how do you check for it if it doesn't show?

[teaching] You can't watch for it. It has to be a question you ask yourself mid-scene. Not deciding a want in advance — that contradicts everything about character emerging through action. Just: is there anything I would be disappointed not to get from the person across from me? If the honest answer is no, that's the hole. And you can fill it right there without stopping.

[curious] Let's talk about the failures. Because I think there are versions of "doing a character" that are just... not.

[confident] Four of them, and none of them looks like bad acting from the inside. That's what makes them stick around.

[curious] Go.

[teaching] The voice with nobody behind it. An accent, a pitch, a speech rhythm — adopted and then not connected to anything. It reads as a character for about fifteen seconds, which is roughly how long it takes an audience to notice that this person is not being affected by anything happening to them.

[curious] Second?

[matter-of-fact] The job played as an identity. "I am a dentist." That's a circumstance, not a person. An occupation is where the scene happens, not who's in it. And the tell is that once someone's picked a job, they usually stop choosing anything else.

[curious] Third.

[teaching] The quirk that never costs anything. A trait that produces behaviour and no consequence. The character who's terribly anxious, and nothing anxiety-producing ever happens to them. Test it — if you could delete the trait and the scene would survive unchanged, it was decoration.

[curious] And the fourth?

[serious] The alibi taken too far. [pause] So — the useful version first. A character gives you an alibi. It's a psychological permission slip. You'll take risks as the character that you'd never take as yourself, because it isn't you doing it. That mechanism is real and it's most of why character works.

[curious] And past a point it flips.

[serious] Past a point the character becomes somewhere to hide. You're now protected from exactly the exposure the scene was supposed to involve. And the tell is very specific: a character who is enormous and reveals nothing about anybody.

[contemplative] Which is the one that fools teachers, too. It looks like commitment.

[curious] Is there one thing underneath all four?

[teaching] Yeah. In every case the character has been specified rather than inhabited. Someone decided what it was instead of becoming it. And the fix is the same each time, and it's never more detail — it's letting the other person's behaviour land on whoever you've already become.

[curious] The traditions don't agree on where character comes from though, right?

[matter-of-fact] They violently disagree. UCB builds it from point of view plus game — the unusual perspective generates the pattern. Johnstone builds it from status and masks — you yield to the character, it arrives from outside, you don't construct it. Napier: one strong initial choice, committed to fully, and the rest fills in. And Meisner — living truthfully under imaginary circumstances. Character isn't pre-built, it emerges from genuine reaction to the other person.

[curious] Four incompatible processes.

[warm] Four incompatible processes, all producing real characters. But Meisner's the bridge, because that's the one that's true of improv no matter which room you trained in. The character is discovered through interaction. Not designed in advance.

[contemplative] [short pause] And it's the same off stage, honestly. The version of you in a job interview, the version at your parents' table — you didn't design those. You found out who you were by finding out who you were talking to.
