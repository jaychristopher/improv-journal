[curious] Everybody knows this one. First class, first exercise. Somebody says "let's paint the fence," the whole room yells "yes, let's!" and starts painting. Four minutes and you're done.

[teaching] And most people file it under "warm-up." Something to get the blood moving before the real work. But look at what's actually being installed. Not the yelling. The yelling is a formality. What you're drilling is the physical habit of beginning something on somebody else's word instead of your own.

[curious] Say more about that. Because on the surface it just looks like enthusiastic agreement.

[teaching] Think about what usually happens between hearing a proposal and acting on it. There's a gap. You evaluate. Is that a good idea, do I want to do that, does it fit what I had in mind. This exercise removes the gap. The word arrives, your body moves. You don't get to check first.

[conversational] And there's a second thing happening that nobody mentions. It removes the cost of proposing.

[curious] How do you mean?

[matter-of-fact] In a scene, an offer nobody takes is a small public failure. You put something out there, it dies, everybody saw. Beginners learn very fast to stop doing that. It's not a conscious decision — you just get quieter.

[emphatic] Here, every single proposal gets accepted by an entire room within half a second. That's the most emphatic possible demonstration that offering is safe. And it holds. A group that's done this is measurably more willing to start things ten minutes later. Something in them got unlocked.

[curious] So what goes wrong?

[teaching] Three things, and you'll recognize all of them. First — the room goes quiet. Nobody proposes.

[curious] Because they're being polite?

[matter-of-fact] Because everybody is auditioning proposals in their head. Running a little internal quality check. "Is 'let's stand up' good enough?" And the answer is yes. The correct proposal is the first one. That's the whole rule. The silence is the exercise's target sitting right there in the room.

[conversational] Second failure — the same three people propose every time. And you can fix that with one line, which I'll get to.

[teaching] Third, and this is the sneaky one. Miming nothing. Somebody yells "yes, let's!" and then does vague hand-waving. That's the entire failure of the exercise in miniature. Enthusiastic acceptance with no content in it. Whatever was proposed, do it specifically enough that a stranger walking in could name what you're doing.

[curious] You said there are lines for these. What are you actually calling from the side?

[matter-of-fact] Four, and the rule is you call them while the room is moving. Never by stopping it. A halt reintroduces exactly the deliberation you're trying to remove.

[teaching] "Take the first one." That's the most useful line in the exercise, and you aim it at the silence.

[teaching] "Specifically." For the hand-waving. Faster than explaining why specificity matters, and it works immediately.

[teaching] "Somebody who hasn't gone yet." Say it to the room, not to a person. Nobody gets singled out, and the quiet people get an opening they didn't have to claim. Which matters — the quiet members are who this exercise exists for.

[teaching] And "keep going." Rounds die from politeness. Everybody finishes painting and then waits to see whether it's finished. It's finished when somebody proposes the next thing.

[curious] Okay. But I want to push on something. Is this exercise actually teaching what people say it teaches?

[serious] No. And this is worth being honest about, because a lot of teachers run it and call it accepting the offer, and those aren't the same thing.

[curious] What's the gap?

[serious] Nothing in this exercise requires anybody to add. You say yes, you copy, you wait for the next proposal. And that shape — accept, contribute nothing, wait — is structurally wimping. The politest way there is to stop a scene. You're drilling it in as a positive habit.

[contemplative] Groups that run this a lot produce students who are enthusiastic and add nothing. And they're genuinely puzzled about it. Their scenes go nowhere and they did every single thing they were told. Yes, and — they can hear it, they can say it. But they've only ever practiced the first half.

[curious] So do you drop it?

[teaching] No. You fix it, and it doesn't take a different exercise. Just a small change to this one.

[conversational] Require the addition. Each person says "yes, let's" and adds one detail as they begin. The fence is wet. The fence belongs to somebody who's watching us. The paint is the wrong color. Now acceptance carries an obligation to contribute — which is precisely the thing the plain version quietly leaves out.

[teaching] Or make the proposals build. The next suggestion has to extend what the room is already doing instead of replacing it. Painting the fence becomes painting the gate to match — not climbing the fence. That trains extension against substitution, which is the exact failure wimping names and the one the plain version otherwise rehearses.

[curious] What does the variant cost?

[matter-of-fact] Real money. Both versions slow the round down and reintroduce a little evaluation — which is the exact pressure the plain version exists to remove. You can't have both at full strength.

[emphatic] So sequence it. Run it plain first, until proposing is visibly cheap — until nobody hesitates before opening their mouth. Then add the requirement. Not before. If you front-load the addition, you lose the one thing this exercise does better than anything else in the form.

[contemplative] And notice where that lands you off stage. Most people have the opposite problem from what they think. They believe they need better ideas. What they actually need is to stop auditioning the ones they have. The meeting where nobody speaks isn't a room without ideas — it's a room full of people running quality checks on themselves.
