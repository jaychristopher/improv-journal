[curious] I want to talk about scene structure, because it's one of those things where everybody nods along and then nobody agrees on what it means.

[teaching] Start with what's actually observable. Watch a scene that works, and you can point to three things that became true. You knew who those people were to each other. Something distinctive surfaced and got played with. And it went somewhere — the thing at the end wasn't the thing at the start.

[curious] Establish, discover, resolve.

[teaching] Right. And the establish part is smaller than people think. One to three lines and a shared physical activity. Carrane's version is start in the middle — walk on and say the thing that addresses what's already happening between these two people. Not "hey, how was your weekend." The line that only makes sense if you've known each other for eleven years.

[conversational] Then something unusual surfaces, and both players clock it. That's the shift. You stop building the world and start exploring the strange thing inside it. And the middle isn't one event — it's a cycle. Heighten, look around at the new reality, heighten again.

[curious] And the ending?

[matter-of-fact] Chosen, not written. A button — some late reveal that recontextualizes everything, and you can hear the audience reprocessing the last two minutes. Or an edit on the laugh, sweeping at the peak. Or the rule of three, because a game that's hit three beats has usually said what it has to say.

[emphatic] And the note nobody wants: replace "someone should edit this" with "I should edit this." The best moment to edit is almost always before you think it is. A scene cut slightly early leaves energy in the room. A scene that runs long decays in front of you.

[curious] Okay, but there are two whole traditions here that describe the middle differently.

[teaching] They do. UCB is game-first — base reality, first unusual thing, if-this-then-what, heighten, button. Structure comes out of the pattern. Johnstone is narrative-first — platform, tilt, reincorporation. Structure comes out of consequence. His line is my favorite instruction in the whole art form: set a firm platform, expand it, and reincorporate like mad. You don't know where you're going, just where you've been.

[contemplative] Same shape, both times. Establish, explore, resolve. They just disagree about what's driving the middle — is it a pattern escalating, or is it a story consequence-ing.

[curious] Then what about the shows that don't do any of this? An hour of TJ and Dave has no phases.

[serious] It doesn't. No edits, scenes bleed into each other, threads come back forty minutes later, and resolution — if it arrives — arrives across the whole evening, not at the end of any particular scene. Napier's just do something at the top points the same direction. The structure isn't a plan you execute.

[curious] So does the model survive that?

[teaching] It survives, but it has to change what it's claiming. The three phases aren't a sequence. They're conditions. They describe what has to become true for a scene to be legible — that the audience knows where they are and who these people are to each other, that something distinctive has surfaced, that it's gone somewhere. They say nothing about order, or duration, or a moment where each one is finished.

[emphatic] And that distinction has teeth. Hold them as stages and you start resolving because the scene feels like it's been going a while. That's the single most common way a good scene gets closed early — a clock in your head, not a read of the room.

[conversational] Hold them as conditions and you're asking something different. Has the audience got what it needs to follow this. And that question has a real answer at any point in the scene. Two minutes in, ten minutes in, doesn't matter.

[curious] Which is also just... a way of paying attention.

[warm] It is. You notice this off stage the second you look for it. The meeting that ends because forty-five minutes elapsed versus the meeting that ends because the thing got decided. One is a clock. One is a condition. Almost everything we do runs on the clock by default.

[matter-of-fact] One more thing about the middle, since it's where scenes actually die. A scene where nothing shifts isn't a scene, it's a premise. The relationship has to be different at the end — in status, in feeling, in what these two now know about each other. Without movement, it decays. You can watch it happen in real time: two people confirming the same fact to each other with escalating volume.

[contemplative] Which is why the three phases are worth holding at all. Not because a scene must contain them in order. Because when a scene is dying, they tell you which one isn't true yet.
