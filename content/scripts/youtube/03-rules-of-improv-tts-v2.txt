[curious] You searched "rules of improv." You got the same five rules everywhere. Yes And. Don't ask questions. Make your partner look good. Don't negate. There are no mistakes.

[emphatic] About half of these are misleading. Some are flat-out wrong. And every single one is trying to point at the same thing — which I'll show you in seven minutes.

[short pause]

[teaching] Rule one. Yes, And.

[curious] What people think it means: agree with everything, add something.

[contemplative] What it actually means in three different traditions.

[teaching] Keith Johnstone, the founder of modern improv, never uses the phrase Yes And. His version is — quote — "be changed by what happens." UCB teaches it as a specific technique with structure: accept the offer, then add to it. Mick Napier from the Annoyance Theatre argues that obsessing over Yes And produces wimpy, compliant, passionless scenes. So which is it?

[emphatic] Yes And is not about agreement. Characters can disagree. Characters can argue, lie, refuse, fight. What can't happen is the performer refusing the performer's offer. If your scene partner says we're in a hospital, you don't have to love the hospital. You just can't say no, we're in a restaurant.

[contemplative] The real principle: accept the shared reality, bring something of yourself to it. The acceptance is non-negotiable. The "and" is where your personality, your point of view, your creative contribution lives.

[short pause]

[teaching] Rule two. Don't ask questions.

[emphatic] This is mostly wrong.

[curious] The rule was invented to solve a specific beginner problem — improvisers asking questions to avoid making choices. "What do you do for a living?" is an attempt to make your partner do the creative work.

[teaching] But questions are a normal part of how humans talk. Banning them entirely creates stilted, unnatural dialogue. The real issue isn't questions — it's empty questions.

[contemplative] "What do you do?" is empty. "Are you seriously wearing that to Dad's funeral?" is loaded. There's a funeral. There's a father. There's a relationship. There's a judgment baked in. That's not avoiding a choice — it's making four at once.

[short pause]

[teaching] Rule three. Make your partner look good.

[warm] This is the only rule all five major improv traditions essentially agree on.

[teaching] What it actually means: create conditions where your partner can succeed.

Johnstone talks about lowering your status so your partner's offers can shine. Del Close and Charna Halpern frame it as ensemble responsibility. UCB builds it into game structure — if you've found the game, your job is to set up your partner to heighten it.

[emphatic] Making your partner look good doesn't mean avoiding conflict, being agreeable, or suppressing your own contributions. It means tracking what your partner needs to succeed and providing it. Sometimes that means setting up their joke. Sometimes it means giving them space. Sometimes it means playing a character who challenges them, because the scene needs the challenge.

[contemplative] The scene is a shared creation. Your success and your partner's success are the same thing.

[short pause]

[teaching] Rule four. Don't negate.

[curious] What people think: never say no.

[emphatic] Oversimplified. Negation in improv is a specific term — destroying the established reality. "We're not in a hospital" when your partner established a hospital — that's negation. "I refuse to go to the hospital" — that's drama. Different.

[teaching] Johnstone identified an entire taxonomy of blocking behaviors that go far beyond saying no. Five named types. Wimping — accepting without adding. Cancelling — making an offer and immediately retracting it. Bridging — delaying real action with stalling moves. Hedging — making vague, uncommitted choices. Pimping — forcing your partner to do the creative work.

[contemplative] Meanwhile, Napier argues that the fear of negation can be worse than negation itself. Performers so terrified of saying no that they agree with everything produce scenes with no tension, no stakes, and no point of view.

[warm] The real principle: don't destroy the shared reality. But within that reality, conflict, disagreement, and refusal are not just allowed — they're necessary.

[short pause]

[teaching] Rule five. There are no mistakes.

[curious] What people think: everything is fine, don't worry about quality.

[contemplative] What it actually means: unplanned moments are material, not errors.

[emphatic] Del Close put it precisely — quote — "fall, then figure out what to do on the way down." An accidental word, a weird physical choice, a moment of confusion — these aren't mistakes to be ignored. They're offers from your unconscious. The best scenes often come from what happened by accident, not what anyone planned.

[teaching] But this doesn't mean quality doesn't matter. The standard of quality shifts. From "did I execute my plan?" to "did I use what happened?" A mistake you ignore is a missed offer. A mistake you build on is a gift.

[short pause]

[contemplative] Now strip away every specific instruction. Five rules. What are they all trying to solve?

[emphatic] Get out of your head and into the scene.

[teaching] Yes And — stop evaluating, start participating. Don't ask questions — stop deflecting, start committing. Make your partner look good — stop competing, start collaborating. Don't negate — stop controlling, start building. There are no mistakes — stop judging, start using.

[contemplative] The rules of improv aren't a checklist. They're training wheels for one skill: being present enough to respond to what's actually happening instead of what you planned. Once you can do that, the rules become unnecessary.
