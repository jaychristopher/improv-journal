[contemplative] In 1981, Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman published a paper in the journal Science. They gave research subjects identical statistical outcomes — described once in terms of lives saved, and once in terms of lives lost.

[short pause]

Same numbers. Same outcomes. Different angle.

[emphatic] Subjects' preferences reversed.

That's the framing effect. And it's usually taught as a cognitive bias to defend against. Humans are irrational, beware. But there's a second reading. If the angle of presentation is part of the signal, then choosing the angle is part of the work. The framing effect isn't only a bias. It's also a skill.

[short pause]

[contemplative] George Lakoff put the mechanism most clearly in his 2004 book Don't Think of an Elephant. Frames don't just decorate content — they activate inferences. Try not to think of an elephant. You can't. The frame is already in play.

[short pause]

[emphatic] You can't argue against a frame from inside the frame. You have to provide a different frame, and let the new web of inferences do the work.

[short pause]

Three moves.

[teaching] One. Lead with the frame, not the conclusion.

The most common mistake is stating the conclusion first, inside the wrong frame. The listener evaluates the conclusion before the frame arrives. Defense fires. The framing, when it shows up a sentence later, reads as rationalization. Instead — establish the frame first. Then let the conclusion land inside it. "What we're really negotiating here is X, not Y" — before the position. "This is a learning conversation, not a performance review" — before the feedback. The frame primes the listener. Conclusions that fit the frame feel like discovery, not invasion.

[teaching] Two. Use their vocabulary.

Words carry frames. Their words carry their frames. When you reach for the term you would use, you're signaling you're outside the listener's architecture. The boundary-defense reflex fires before any reasoning happens. The fix is small — swap your in-group word for theirs — but the effect is structural. The listener stays inside the conversation instead of leaving to defend a frame.

[teaching] Three. Ask, don't assert.

A question lets the listener do the framing. An assertion forces them to evaluate yours. Socrates was working this 2,400 years ago. When you ask a question that points at the conclusion, the listener reasons their way there using premises they already hold. The conclusion arrives as their discovery, not your invasion.

[short pause]

Try this. "How would you handle this if it were a customer issue instead of a personnel one?" The listener answers. Their answer often resolves the original disagreement — without anyone having argued.

[short pause]

[warm] One anti-move.

Framing slides into spin the moment the angle hides something the listener would want to know. The honesty test is simple. Would you be comfortable with them seeing the framing move you just made? If yes, it's craft. If no, it's manipulation. The line is real. Stay on the craft side.

[short pause]

[contemplative] Here's the deeper point. The framing effect is usually presented as a bias to defend against, because the audience is treated as the problem. The actual finding is structural. Communication doesn't transfer content intact. Meaning is constituted in reception. The frame is part of the signal because it has to be.

[short pause]

[emphatic] Which means the choice isn't whether to frame. The choice is whether to frame deliberately, or by accident.

[short pause]

People who refuse to think about framing aren't avoiding it. They're framing badly. Frame on purpose. Use their words. Ask the question. And when the frame itself is the issue — name it.
