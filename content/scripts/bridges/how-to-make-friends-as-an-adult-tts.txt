[curious] I want to start with something that sounds like a complaint but I think is actually a diagnosis. People say they've forgotten how to make friends.

[teaching] Right, and that's the wrong story. You didn't forget. Think about how you learned to improvise — you didn't get good because someone explained "yes, and" to you. You got good because you were in a room with the same eleven people every Tuesday for eight weeks, failing at something together. That's not a skill you acquired. That's a set of conditions you were placed inside.

[curious] And the conditions went away.

[teaching] The conditions went away. Adult life removes them one at a time and nobody announces it.

[conversational] So what are they? What does a friendship actually need?

[teaching] Three things. First, repeated unplanned contact with the same people. School gives you that by force. Work sometimes. But adult socialising is scheduled — which means every single meeting has to be individually justified, and anything that requires justification happens less.

[curious] That's the whole problem with "we should grab a drink sometime."

[teaching] That's the whole problem. Second thing: a shared activity that is not the friendship itself. Talking is a bad medium for getting close, because it makes the relationship the subject. Doing something together lets closeness accumulate as a by-product — which is the only way it reliably accumulates.

[curious] Like how you never once had a conversation about becoming friends with your Level Two class.

[teaching] Never. It just happened while you were both trying to figure out an object work exercise. And that's the third condition — stakes low enough that being unimpressive is survivable. A room where you have to be interesting produces performance. And performance is the exact thing that prevents friendship.

[matter-of-fact] Most adults still have the first one at work. Almost nobody has the second and third together.

[contemplative] Which explains why improv classes produce friends at a rate that seems unreasonable.

[emphatic] It's not that improvisers are more sociable. The format accidentally solves all three. Same twelve people, weekly, eight weeks — repetition you didn't have to negotiate. A shared task that isn't each other. And a room where everyone is visibly bad at something.

[curious] That last one gets underrated.

[teaching] Massively. Being watched failing at something harmless, alongside other people failing at the same thing — that produces closeness fast and cheap. And adults almost never arrange to be bad at anything in public. It's a real loss.

[short pause]

[teaching] Think about trust as an accumulated estimate. Many small exchanges that tell you whether it's safe to be exposed around these specific people. Eight weeks of a shared task generates more of those exchanges than two years of occasional drinks.

[curious] Okay. There's a distinction I want you to draw, because I think it's the useful one. Ensemble versus... a group of people.

[teaching] An ensemble is a group operating as an interdependent system, not a collection of individuals taking turns. And that distinction is exactly the difference between having a friendship group and having a set of people who each know you separately.

[conversational] And most adult social lives are the second thing.

[serious] Most adult social lives are the second thing. A series of one-to-one relationships, each maintained individually, each requiring you to initiate. It's exhausting and it's fragile, because every connection depends on one person's ongoing effort. Usually yours.

[teaching] An ensemble maintains itself. When several people know each other independently of you, contact stops requiring organisation — which removes the exact friction that killed everything else.

[curious] So the move is —

[emphatic] Introduce your friends to each other. That's worth more than acquiring new ones. It's the ensemble move.

[curious] What else actually works?

[matter-of-fact] Choose repetition over variety. One thing weekly with the same people beats four different things once each. Almost every "put yourself out there" recommendation optimises for the wrong variable — it maximises exposure to new people, when what makes friendship is exposure to the same people repeatedly.

[teaching] Choose something with a task in it. Classes, teams, choirs, volunteering — anywhere your attention has somewhere to go that isn't each other. Pure social events are the hardest possible setting to make a friend in, which is why they feel like work.

[curious] And the going-first thing.

[teaching] Be first to be unimpressive. Fastest route through the polite phase is being visibly imperfect before anyone else is. A real opinion, an admission, an obvious question. It gives everyone else permission, and permission is what the polite phase is sitting there waiting for.

[conversational] There's a version of this problem I think is more common than starting from zero. People who have acquaintances that never convert.

[teaching] Right — colleagues, gym people, the parent at the school gate you always enjoy talking to. That's a different problem with a specific cause. Those relationships already have the repetition. What they're missing is any exchange that would update the estimate.

[curious] Because the context supplies the script.

[emphatic] Because the context supplies the script. You can see someone weekly for three years in a setting that only ever calls for the pleasant version of both of you. So two moves. [teaching] And say one true thing slightly earlier than is comfortable. Not a confession. A real opinion, an actual difficulty, an admission that you found something hard. Both people are waiting for evidence that the other won't use it against them, and someone has to supply it first.

[contemplative] The failure mode being the person who wants deeper friendship and waits to be invited into it.
