[curious] Okay, theatre games. Everyone's played them. But I want to start with the thing I think most people have wrong about them.

[teaching] They were never meant to be funny.

[curious] Say more.

[teaching] Viola Spolin invented the form in Chicago in the forties and fifties, teaching improvisation to people who weren't actors. Kids. Immigrants learning English. She needed something that didn't depend on talent or training or even fluency in the language. So she built games. Give someone a clear objective and a constraint, and the skill shows up as a side effect of playing.

[matter-of-fact] The laugh was never the point. Her son took the method to Second City and that's where the comedy grew out of it. But the root is a teaching instrument.

[curious] So when we say theatre games and improv games interchangeably —

[teaching] We're blurring two different things. Theatre games are training instruments. The goal is a skill — attention, physical awareness, ensemble responsiveness. Improv games are performance structures. Freeze Tag, Scenes from a Hat, Theatresports. Those are built to generate entertainment in front of an audience.

[emphatic] And here's the practical consequence. If you run a theatre game and the room starts performing for each other, the exercise has stopped working. That's not a discipline problem. That's a diagnostic.

[curious] A diagnostic of what?

[teaching] The point of concentration is gone. [short pause] That's Spolin's whole mechanic. Every game gives you one thing to attend to. Only one. Mirror your partner's movement. Build the physical environment. Say one word at a time.

[contemplative] And the constraint is doing the work. With one clear object of attention, there's no spare capacity left over for self-monitoring — and self-monitoring is what makes people stiff. You cannot simultaneously worry about looking foolish and track your partner's hand precisely. There isn't room.

[warm] The game occupies the part of you that would otherwise be judging.

[curious] Which is why "just relax and have fun" never works.

[teaching] Right. One is a request. The other is a structure. [pause] You can't request your way out of self-consciousness. You can only crowd it out.

[curious] So the circle games. Zip Zap Zop. Everyone's done it. Is it actually training something, or is it just a way to start?

[matter-of-fact] It's the most played warm-up in existence and the most often played badly. The game is the eye contact. Not the speed.

[curious] Oh.

[teaching] Rooms turn it into a race. Fastest round wins. But what it actually trains is attention that can be redirected instantly — you have to genuinely find a person and land on them before you send. Speed without contact is just noise arriving on time.

[warm] Pass the Clap is the one I love for a different reason. Two neighbours clap simultaneously, aiming for one sound instead of two close together. It's the simplest demonstration there is that a group can do something no individual is doing. Nobody makes that single clap. The room does.

[curious] And One Word Story?

[teaching] Hardest thing on the beginner list, and people don't realize it. A story travels around the circle at one word each. No conferring, nobody's allowed to pass. What it trains is giving up authorship. [short pause] Which is the reason the story always goes somewhere nobody planned — and the reason people fight it. You feel the sentence you wanted forming, and then someone hands you a word that kills it.

[contemplative] That feeling — that's the actual lesson. That's the whole thing.

[curious] Where does that show up off stage for you?

[teaching] Any conversation where you're waiting to say your thing. You've stopped listening; you're holding a sentence. One Word Story makes holding a sentence structurally impossible, and you discover the conversation was better without it.

[curious] Say you're planning a session. How do you decide what to run?

[matter-of-fact] Not alphabetically. The only question that matters is what the group needs. Attention and listening first — that's foundational and it's where most groups need the most time. Mirroring, last word response, blind offer. Then ensemble, physicality, spontaneity, structure, in whatever order the room in front of you is asking for.

[serious] Emotional range last. Emotional honesty scenes, status transfer — those are exposing. Run them before trust exists and you get compliance on the surface and withdrawal underneath. Which looks like it worked, and didn't.

[curious] And running them well?

[teaching] Side-coach. Don't stop the game. Spolin's signature move — you call short instructions from outside while play continues. "Follow the follower." "Slow it down." "Use the whole space." Stopping to explain breaks the exact state the game exists to produce.

[emphatic] And give one instruction, not three. If a game needs a paragraph of setup, your point of concentration isn't clear enough yet. That's on you, not the game.

[curious] What about when it's just... boring? Nothing entertaining happens.

[warm] Let it be boring. A mirroring exercise that produces nothing funny but genuine attention has succeeded completely. Judge the game by whether people were actually present, not by whether the room laughed.

[contemplative] That's the discipline, honestly. The laugh is such a clean signal — you know immediately whether you got one. Presence isn't like that. You have to be willing to run something, watch it produce nothing, and trust it landed.

[curious] Last one. Somebody won't join in. What do you do?

[matter-of-fact] Carry on without them and leave the door open. Refusal in a room like that is rarely obstinacy — it's a reasonable estimate that they're about to be embarrassed in front of people they know. And the estimate is often correct.

[teaching] Someone who watches two or three rounds usually takes the fourth. Because by then they've seen everybody else be visibly bad at it and survive. That's the information they were waiting for.

[serious] Insisting converts the game into a test of compliance. Which is precisely the one thing it cannot be and still work.

[contemplative] Spolin taught these as play among equals. Not an adult directing from outside the circle. The moment you're standing outside giving notes, you've made it a performance for your approval — and that reflex is the exact thing the games exist to dissolve.
