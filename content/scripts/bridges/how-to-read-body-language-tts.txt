[curious] Everyone says improvisers are good at reading people. But I've been doing this for years and nobody has ever taught me what crossed arms mean.

[teaching] Because they don't mean anything. That's the whole thing. The crossed-arms school — arms crossed is defensive, eye contact is engaged, leaning in is interested — it's basically astrology with better posture. Your partner might have crossed their arms because the studio is freezing.

[curious] So what are we actually doing up there? Because something is happening. I can feel when a scene partner shifts.

[teaching] You're reading the shift. Not the position. That's principle one and it reorganizes everything. A frozen posture tells you almost nothing — it's their resting state. But arms that cross *right now*, mid-scene, in response to the line you just said? That's an offer.

[curious] And the offer isn't "I'm defensive."

[teaching] [emphatic] No. The offer is "something just changed." That's all you get, and that's plenty — because you also know exactly what it changed in response to. You said the thing about their mother, and their breathing went shallow. You don't know what that means. You know when it happened. That's a scene.

[curious] I want to push on that, because it feels like less information than the books promise.

[matter-of-fact] It's less information and it's true, which beats more information that's invented. The books promise a dictionary. There is no dictionary. What there is, is timing.

[curious] Okay. Principle two.

[teaching] Read the whole person, not individual signals. Spolin called it seeing with the whole body. Your partner walks on and you take in one impression — tension, energy, weight, breath, how much space they're claiming — as a single thing. Not a checklist.

[conversational] Which is the part that sounds mystical and isn't.

[teaching] Not remotely. Look across the room right now at someone you're not talking to. Are they relaxed or tense? Open or closed? Charged or depleted? [short pause] You had an answer before I finished the question. That answer is more accurate than any gesture-by-gesture analysis you could run in five minutes.

[curious] So why don't we trust it?

[teaching] Because analysis feels like work and feeling doesn't. But analysis is slow and often wrong. Feeling is fast and weirdly reliable, because your body has been reading other bodies since before you could speak. You just started talking over it.

[curious] Principle three is the one I always forget.

[emphatic] And it's the biggest. You're not reading a person. You're reading the space between two people. Status.

[curious] Johnstone.

[teaching] Johnstone. And here's the part that makes it usable: status is complementary. One person raises, the other feels pressure to drop. It's a seesaw. Which means the same body produces completely different signals depending who's standing opposite it.

[contemplative] So somebody isn't a fidgety person. They're fidgeting *at* someone.

[teaching] [warm] Yes. Exactly that. And once you see it that way, four channels carry most of the information. Who breaks eye contact first — not how much, who yields. Stillness against adjustment — who's resettling and who isn't. Who fills the silence, which is the most reliable tell in ordinary conversation and the one people are least aware of producing. And share of space — how much of the table each person took without anyone negotiating it.

[curious] Two people both playing high?

[matter-of-fact] Competition. Both playing low and nothing gets decided and everyone leaves the meeting vaguely unsatisfied and can't say why.

[curious] There's a version of this I hadn't thought about — my own default is in the measurement.

[teaching] It's the correction factor on everything. If you default low, people read higher to you than they do to anyone else in the room. They didn't change. The seesaw pivots around you. Knowing which way you lean is the difference between reading a room and reading your own tilt.

[curious] Can we do the myths? Because I have heard the ninety-three percent thing at every corporate workshop I've ever been dragged to.

[emphatic] It's wrong and it's the most confidently repeated wrong thing in the field. Mehrabian, sixties, tiny studies — subjects heard a *single word* delivered in a tone that contradicted it, and got asked which one they believed. Seven, thirty-eight, fifty-five describe how people resolved *that mismatch*. Not communication. He spent years saying so.

[curious] Why does it matter beyond being annoying?

[serious] Because if you believe words are seven percent, you'll discount what somebody actually told you in favour of a hunch about their shoulders. The words are usually your most reliable channel. The body is where you look when the words and the delivery disagree.

[curious] And lie detection.

[matter-of-fact] Fifty-four percent. Pooled across hundreds of studies. That's a coin toss with a slight limp. Trained professionals score the same and are considerably more confident, which is worse.

[teaching] The tells people cite — nose touching, broken eye contact — index discomfort. And being suspected of lying is uncomfortable whether or not you're guilty. You're reading stress, and stress points at nothing.

[curious] But something is readable.

[warm] Emotional state. Not the content of someone's thoughts — how they feel about what's happening. Which is far more useful anyway. On stage you're never asking "is my partner being honest." You're asking "what's happening inside them right now, and what does it want from me."

[curious] So how do you actually get better at this? Because it isn't by memorizing more.

[teaching] Mirroring. Ten minutes. That's the shortcut and it's almost insultingly simple. You can't mirror someone while thinking about yourself. You can't mirror someone while running analysis on their left eyebrow. You have to take in the whole physical state, continuously, and answer it in real time.

[contemplative] And it turns something on.

[teaching] It was already on. You'd been drowning it out. [short pause] The irony is beautiful — the way to read body language better is to stop reading and start feeling.

[curious] One thing still bothers me. Reading is only half of it, right? I can be exquisitely perceptive and still be wrong for two years.

[emphatic] That's the failure mode, and it's not a perception failure. It's a reasonable assumption made early and never checked. A read is a hypothesis. Blind offer trains exactly this — respond to what they actually did, not what you decided it meant, then watch what comes back.

[curious] And the check is cheap.

[teaching] Absurdly. Soften your own status and see if they expand into the space you left. Or name it lightly — "you went quiet, what are you thinking?" [pause] Offers are what people hand you on purpose. That question gets a better answer than any amount of inference, for a fraction of the attention.

[contemplative] The reading is for the moment before you can ask.

[warm] And for deciding what to ask. It was never meant to replace the asking. People forget that, and go quietly, confidently wrong about somebody they see every single day.
