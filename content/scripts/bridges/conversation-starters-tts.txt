[curious] I want to talk about the thing that everybody thinks is their problem and isn't. The opener. Somebody's standing at a party, mentally flipping through questions, trying to find the good one.

[teaching] And the opener is almost never what killed it. Think about how you'd diagnose a scene that died. You don't blame the initiation. The initiation was fine. What happened was the second line — somebody didn't build on it. Conversations are exactly the same, and people mis-diagnose them in exactly the same way, because the opener is the last thing they remember choosing.

[curious] So a great opener buys you what, exactly?

[matter-of-fact] One exchange. That's it. They answer, and now you're both standing in the same silence you were in before — except worse, because you've spent your material.

[contemplative] There's something almost funny about that. The list of two hundred conversation starters is the improv equivalent of a really good opening line you've been sitting on all class. It gets you into the scene. It doesn't get you through it.

[teaching] Right. And there is a genuine craft to a good opener, it's just not the craft people think. Three things, and none of them is being interesting.

[curious] Go on.

[teaching] First — it has to be answerable without effort. An opener is an offer. And you judge an offer by what your partner can do with it, not by how good it sounded coming out of your mouth. "What's the most interesting thing that's happened to you this year?" — that's a beautiful question and a terrible offer. Answering it well is work. Answering it badly is embarrassing. You've handed them a bag of bricks.

[curious] Whereas "how was the drive over?"

[warm] Whereas "how was the drive over" has an answer sitting right there. Which is the second thing — specific beats general, always. "How's it going" gets you "yeah, good." That's a wall. "How was the drive over" gets you an actual sentence, because there's something to describe. Detail is the raw material. You want them handing you detail.

[curious] And the third?

[emphatic] The obvious one is the good one. Your first thought is connected to the situation. Your second thought is connected to your ego.

[short pause]

[teaching] The question you reach for when you stop trying to be memorable is nearly always the better question. And here's the part that stings — the other person can tell. They can hear the difference between a question and an audition. When you ask something clever, you've asked them to be clever back. You've made it a performance, and now they're managing that instead of talking to you.

[contemplative] That's the small-talk thing too, isn't it. People treat small talk as the boring bit to be skipped.

[teaching] Small talk is a handshake. It's not supposed to be interesting — it's establishing, cheaply and at no risk to either party, that an exchange is safe and both of you are willing. Nobody decides whether they like you based on the weather question. They decide based on whether the weather question got answered warmly. That's the whole function.

[emphatic] Which is why skipping it backfires. Open with something substantial and you've asked for a level of exposure they have no basis to give you yet. The polite version of that is a short answer, and then a closed door.

[curious] Okay. So what happens at exchange two? What's the actual failure?

[serious] Four of them, and every one is a scene note.

[matter-of-fact] One — they answer, and you drop it. They say something, you say "oh, nice," and you both start hunting for the next question. Two rounds of that and it's a quiz.

[teaching] Two — you answer with your own version. They mention a holiday, you mention yours. And it feels like participating! It really does. But that's steering. Accepting on the surface while redirecting to your own material. The tell is that they never get back to what they were saying.

[curious] Three?

[emphatic] Three is the one this whole genre of advice causes. You reach for the next starter. Having questions in reserve makes it tempting to fire the next one rather than sit in what was just said. And a sequence of unrelated good questions is an interview.

[teaching] And four — you never offer anything back. A question with no disclosure attached is a request. Two or three in a row and they feel the imbalance, even if they couldn't name it.

[curious] So what's the move instead?

[teaching] Build from their last words. Whatever they just said, your next line comes out of it. Not a new topic, not your equivalent story — an extension of theirs. There's an exercise for exactly this, last-word-response, where every reply has to start from your partner's final word. Two minutes of that and you notice how violently your instinct is to change the subject.

[curious] It's just yes-and with the volume turned down.

[warm] It's yes-and at conversational volume, yeah. And the other half of it — ask about the detail, not the headline. Someone says "we finally moved out of my mum's place in March." Most people ask about the move. The move is the headline. The material is the mum, or the March, or that "finally." Which is the same instinct as in a scene — you don't take the big thing, you take the specific thing.

[contemplative] And then say something back. One sentence about yourself, roughly the depth they just offered. Evidence that this is an exchange and not a survey.

[teaching] And let the pause run one beat past comfortable. People fill it with the second half of what they were saying, and the second half is the half worth having. Be thankful applies to silence too — it's an offer, not a gap you have to plug.

[curious] So the two hundred questions are —

[matter-of-fact] Useful. Genuinely. Ask about now, not about them — "tell me about yourself" is the worst opener in common use because it demands a self-assessment before anybody's said anything. Ask what today's been like. Ask what they were doing before this. Ask what got them into that, which is the cheapest depth there is.

[emphatic] But every answer you get already contains two or three next topics. The skill is taking one instead of reaching for a fresh subject. Nobody who's actually listening ever needs the list.
