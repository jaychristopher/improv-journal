[curious] Okay, so Del Close. Everyone in improv knows the name. Half of us can quote him. But if I asked you what he actually taught — like, the content — I'm not sure I could answer.

[teaching] Most people can't. Which is a strange thing to say about the most influential improv teacher in America. What people know is the list. Belushi, Murray, Farley, Fey, Poehler, Colbert. And the list isn't the point. The list is what happened *because* of the point.

[curious] So what's the point?

[teaching] That improv is theatre. Full stop. Not a warm-up for sketch, not a writers' room with an audience watching — a form, judged as a form. Structure, weight, an ending it earned. That was a genuinely contested claim in 1970 and it's the assumption of every serious training program now. He won by attrition.

[curious] And everything else comes out of that?

[teaching] Pretty much. If it's theatre, then chasing a laugh is a failure of nerve — because a laugh is always available and always cheaper than the alternative. If it's theatre, you work at the top of your intelligence instead of playing dumb, because an audience will follow anything if the people on stage genuinely believe it. And condescension reads from the back row. It always reads.

[matter-of-fact] Then the truth is funny. Which sounds like a claim about comedy but is actually an instruction about where to look. Invented material is thin because the person inventing it is guessing. Observed material is dense because reality is more specific than anybody's guess.

[contemplative] That's the one that hits me off stage. You can always tell when someone's telling you a story they've told a hundred times versus something they just noticed.

[warm] Same mechanism. And then the fourth one — the ensemble is the star. Your job is to make the other people look good. That's not manners. A scene is a shared object. Anyone protecting their own position in it is degrading it.

[curious] Let's do the famous one. Follow the fear.

[teaching] Most misused sentence in improv. And what it actually meant in the room was narrow and technical.

[short pause]

[teaching] At any moment a scene offers you several available moves. And performers reliably pick the safest one. The joke instead of the admission. The new topic instead of the uncomfortable one. Going for a glass of water instead of staying in the chair. The instruction was: notice that flinch, and take the other option. Because the reluctance is data. You're avoiding it because it matters.

[emphatic] That's a claim about choice-making inside a scene. It is *not* a claim that discomfort is good for people. And the gap between those two readings is where an enormous amount of damage has been done by people quoting him.

[curious] Which gets us to the part nobody puts on the poster.

[serious] He was, by many accounts — including admiring ones — frequently cruel. Contemptuous of students he decided were lazy. Capable of humiliating someone in front of a class. The standard telling files that under "uncompromising" and points at the results.

[emphatic] But the results aren't evidence. The students who thrived are the ones you've heard of. Nobody counts the people who quit. And that anecdote — brilliant teacher destroys a student who had it coming — has licensed a staggering amount of ordinary bullying by people with none of his ability.

[matter-of-fact] Same with the drugs. Told as colour, occasionally as a source of insight. It cost him jobs and years, and the romantic version of it has not helped the improvisers who inherited the idea that self-destruction and creative seriousness are somehow related.

[curious] What about the mysticism? He talked about the Harold as a spiritual thing.

[contemplative] And that one's subtler, because group mind is real. It's observable. It has identifiable conditions you can actually train. But describing it in terms that can't be examined made it harder to teach and easier to gatekeep. If the thing you're training is ineffable, then whoever holds the room decides whether you have it.

[curious] Here's what I want to know. Which of the ideas actually survived?

[teaching] Most of them. Ensemble-first, honesty over cleverness, playing at full intelligence — every school since has kept those, including the ones that define themselves against him.

[matter-of-fact] One got corrected. The UCB generation — taught largely by *his* students — put comedy back as the explicit goal and put game at the center. And that's a real correction, not a betrayal. Close's suspicion of laughs protected you from hackery, but it left you without a usable account of why a scene was working.

[curious] And the Harold itself?

[matter-of-fact] Widely acknowledged now, even by its advocates, that the structure costs more than it returns. Will Hines's objection is the cleanest version — the form just contains too many scenes. Plenty of strong teams use its lessons without its scaffolding.

[contemplative] And the thing that got quietly abandoned is the teaching culture. Contemporary training has more or less concluded that the room has to be safe for the work to be honest. Which is close to the opposite of the environment he's celebrated for running — and is arguably a better route to the exact thing he wanted.

[curious] There's a detail I love, though. He didn't invent the Harold.

[warm] He developed it. First one was The Committee, 1967, Concord, California — an improvised piece about Vietnam, performed for a high school. The name came later, in the van home. Somebody called out "Harold" as a joke about A Hard Day's Night, where a reporter asks George Harrison what he calls his haircut and he says "Arthur."

[curious] It meant nothing.

[warm] It meant nothing. It stuck anyway. And what Close did over the next twenty years — decisively with Charna Halpern at ImprovOlympic — was turn a loose experiment into something teachable. Inventing a form and making it transmissible are different achievements, and the second one is why anyone outside that van has heard of it.

[curious] There's the skull story too. He willed his skull to the Goodman so he could keep playing Yorick.

[matter-of-fact] Great story. Appears to be untrue, at least in the particulars. Halpern later admitted she couldn't get his skull after he died and sourced one from somewhere else. The Goodman got a skull. It was probably not his.

[contemplative] Which is a fitting ending for a teacher whose entire subject was the difference between an appealing invention and an honest observation.

[short pause]

[warm] And improv has gone right on telling the better version anyway.
