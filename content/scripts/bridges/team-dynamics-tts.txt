[curious] Let's talk about safety, because that word has been beaten to death in corporate contexts.

[emphatic] It's infrastructure. And you can see the mechanics on stage.

[teaching] Right — a performer who doesn't feel safe hedges. They plan ahead instead of responding. They go for the safe choice instead of the honest one. [contemplative] Same loop that kills teams anywhere. People withhold, avoid conflict, get predictable. Meetings become performances of agreement.

[teaching] And Edmondson's finding is the one that surprises people. Teams with high safety don't make fewer mistakes. They report more of them, and learn faster.

[curious] Here's my real question. Almost everything written about team dynamics assumes you can restructure the team. Most people can't. They're a member, not the manager.

[emphatic] That's the honest gap, and there are exactly two things that are genuinely unilateral.

[teaching] First: who gets heard. Woolley's finding is about a distribution, and one person can shift a distribution. "What do you think?" aimed at whoever hasn't spoken costs nothing and requires no standing. Do it twice in a meeting and the shape of the discussion changes.

[curious] And the second?

[teaching] What happens to a contribution after it's made. An idea that gets built on becomes the group's idea. An idea met with silence teaches everyone watching what offering costs. One consistent builder in a room of six changes what people are willing to put forward — because everyone calculates the risk of speaking from what happened to the last person who did.

[conversational] Two. Name a pattern once, neutrally — "we moved past that quite fast." Costs almost nothing the first time. [matter-of-fact] So if that's your situation, the honest options are: protect the people below you from it, raise it with someone who can change it, or leave.

[contemplative] Practicing better turn-taking underneath a manager who punishes dissent isn't a fix. It's a way of being tired.
