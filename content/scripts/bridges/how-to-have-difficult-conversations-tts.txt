[curious] I want to ask about the thing everybody does before a hard conversation. You know the one. You're in the shower, you're driving, and you're running the whole thing. Your opening line. Their comeback. Your comeback to their comeback.

[teaching] Right. And you get good at it. By the time you sit down, you've got a script that works beautifully — against the version of them you invented.

[curious] Which is the problem.

[teaching] Which is the entire problem. They didn't get a copy. They say something you didn't write. And now you're standing at a fork: respond to what they actually said, or get back to your point.

[short pause]

[teaching] Almost everybody takes the point. And the second you do that, you've stopped listening. They feel it. They get defensive. The conversation gets harder — because of the preparation that was supposed to make it easier.

[curious] So what, don't prepare?

[matter-of-fact] Prepare your state. Not your lines. You can't prepare your way through something you don't control. What you can do is show up present, listening, honest — and then deal with whatever's actually in the room.

[conversational] Which, if you've done any improv at all, should sound extremely familiar.

[teaching] It's the same skill. We do this for a living — scenes with conflict, tension, somebody's crying, somebody's furious, no script, people watching. That's a difficult conversation with a lighting grid.

[curious] Okay so break it down. What actually transfers?

[teaching] Three things. First one: receive before you respond.

[emphatic] When your scene partner speaks, your only job is to take in what they gave you. Not evaluate it. Not decide if it's a good offer. Not start building your response underneath it. Just — see it. Completely. Words, tone, the thing under the tone.

[curious] And in a real conversation?

[teaching] When they say the thing that lights you up — let the trigger happen. Notice it. Then, before you say anything, check that you actually heard them. Not the version you were expecting.

[conversational] The move is: reflect before you redirect. "What I'm hearing is you feel overlooked when I make calls without checking in."

[curious] That's not agreeing with them.

[matter-of-fact] Not remotely. It's verification. But it does something almost unfair — it makes them feel heard, their defenses drop, and now the rest of the conversation is possible. You didn't concede anything. You just proved you were listening.

[curious] Second one.

[teaching] Say the true thing, not the tactical one.

[conversational] Most people in a hard conversation are running a strategy. Careful words. Managed tone. Controlled disclosure. It feels safe. It also feels completely fake — to both of you.

[emphatic] "I'm scared this conversation is going to damage us" — vulnerable, true, lands. "I think we need to align on expectations" — perfectly composed, and it bounces right off. People can hear the difference between a person and a performance.

[curious] Even when the tactical version is well-written?

[matter-of-fact] Especially then. Polish is a tell.

[curious] And the third.

[serious] Stay in the room.

[teaching] The hardest part of a difficult conversation isn't finding the right words. It's not leaving. And I don't mean walking out — I mean the version where your body stays and you go somewhere else. Silent. Defensive. Already drafting the exit.

[conversational] Onstage that's commitment. Playing the emotion that's happening instead of deflecting to a joke or bailing into a new topic. Which is what everyone does when a scene gets genuinely uncomfortable.

[teaching] Off stage it looks like: noticing the urge to minimize, and choosing not to. "This is hard. I want to get it right. Can we slow down?" That's staying. The joke, the subject change, the shrug — all leaving.

[short pause]

[curious] Alright, but all three of those assume the other person is playing along. What about when they're not?

[teaching] Good. Three failure modes, three moves.

[matter-of-fact] They get defensive — that's a block. Your offer got refused. And the instinct with a block is to repeat the offer, louder. Never works. The improv answer is to accept what they actually gave you, including the defensiveness. "You're hearing this as an accusation. Let's sort that out before anything else."

[curious] You're accepting the refusal itself as the offer.

[teaching] Because it is one. It's information about where they are.

[conversational] Second: they escalate. Remember status is complementary — you raise, they drop, and they feel it. A spiral is just two people raising in turn. Lowering deliberately — slower, quieter, less certain — breaks it faster than matching them.

[curious] Isn't that just backing down?

[emphatic] No. That's the part people miss. You can lower your status and hold your content. Those are separate dials. Play low, say the hard thing anyway.

[teaching] Third: you lose the thread entirely. Two people in two different conversations. That's a fracture. And the fix isn't restarting from the top — it's naming where you actually are. "I've lost what we're deciding. Can we say it out loud?" Same move as onstage. Re-establish the shared reality instead of continuing in separate ones.

[curious] What about when it just... doesn't resolve?

[contemplative] Then it doesn't resolve. Most of them don't, in one sitting. And treating that as failure is exactly what makes people avoid the next one. A conversation that ends with the disagreement clearly stated and the relationship intact — that worked. Nothing got settled and it still worked.

[curious] Can you rehearse any of this? Because you can't exactly practise on the person.

[teaching] No, and that's why everybody defaults to scripting. But the moves drill fine on their own.

[conversational] Last word response — your line has to build from your partner's final words. Makes it physically impossible to compose while they're talking. That's principle one, as an exercise.

[teaching] No backspace scene — nothing can be retracted or softened. Which teaches you that most leaving isn't walking out. It's the qualification. It's "I mean, it's not a big deal" three seconds after you finally said the big deal.

[curious] The take-back.

[matter-of-fact] The take-back is leaving. It just looks like politeness.

[curious] One more. When's the right time?

[teaching] Sooner than is comfortable, earlier in the day, and never right after they've performed something. And if you've already sat on it for months — say that first. One sentence, no explanation. What kills you is the accumulated list, because delivering a list tells them you've been keeping score. Take the most recent instance. Leave the rest.

[contemplative] If the pattern's real, it'll come up on its own.

[teaching] Here's what's underneath all of it. These conversations go badly when both people are performing — managing words, controlling tone, hiding the actual reaction. They go well when both people are present.

[emphatic] And that's the whole thing improv teaches. Being real, in real time, with no script, across from someone who's also being real. It isn't comfortable and it isn't smooth. It's just the only thing that works.
