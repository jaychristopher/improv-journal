[curious] Somebody's suggested an improv workshop for the team. Half the room lights up, half of them look like they've been asked to give blood. Who's right?

[teaching] Both, a bit. Some of it transfers really well. Some of it doesn't transfer at all. And the industry selling it isn't always clear about which is which.

[curious] Start with the uncomfortable part. What does the evidence actually say?

[matter-of-fact] Not much. Reviews of workplace improvisation training keep landing on the same two observations — there isn't much rigorous research, and the studies that do exist define "improvisation training" so differently that comparing them is nearly meaningless.

[curious] So it doesn't work?

[emphatic] No — that's not the finding. The finding is that nobody's measured it well enough to make the confident claims you see in the marketing. Which means when a provider quotes you a percentage improvement in collaboration, ask what was measured, against what baseline, and how long afterwards. [short pause] The honest version of the claim is smaller: the mechanisms are plausible, practitioners report them consistently, the controlled evidence is thin.

[conversational] Okay. So what does transfer?

[teaching] Four things, and they're all structural rather than performative. First — building on what was already said. Every contribution is something the next person can extend or discard. Most unproductive meetings are a sequence of unconnected proposals, each one quietly replacing the last, and the people making them can't see it happening.

[curious] And improv makes it visible.

[confident] Because on stage it fails immediately and audibly. You feel the scene die. In a meeting the same move just... slides past.

[curious] Second thing?

[teaching] Making being wrong survivable. Which isn't an outcome of improv, it's a precondition — but the exercises rehearse it directly. Small, low-stakes public failures where nothing bad follows. [pause] A team that has watched everybody in it be visibly rubbish at a game together has a slightly different relationship to a bad idea in a meeting.

[conversational] Third.

[teaching] Status. It's the transaction running underneath every exchange, and it matters at work because a manager's raised position is structural — they didn't choose it, they can't switch it off. Improv gives people a vocabulary for something they were already reading and couldn't name.

[curious] And the fourth?

[matter-of-fact] Attention is a limited resource. You cannot compose your reply and hear the other person at the same time. That's not a metaphor and it doesn't need a stage to demonstrate.

[conversational] Now the other list. What doesn't transfer?

[emphatic] Being funny on demand. Nothing in a workshop makes a team funnier at work, and the ones promising a more playful culture are selling a mood, not a skill.

[curious] What about "yes, and"? That's the one that escaped into business language.

[serious] That's the most damaging import, and it's precisely because it escaped. In a scene, accepting an offer is the discipline that makes the form possible — you can't build anything on a denial. In a meeting? An organisation that can't say no to a proposal isn't collaborative, it's ungoverned.

[curious] So what's the transferable part of it?

[teaching] The noticing. How often a reply accepts the words and discards the content. [short pause] Somebody says "yes, absolutely, and what if we came at it from a totally different angle" — that's a denial wearing a yes. Improv trains you to hear that. It does not train you to agree with everything.

[curious] And confidence? That's on every workshop brochure.

[matter-of-fact] Least reliable outcome on the list. What actually improves is a person's willingness to start speaking before they have the whole sentence. Which is narrower, and honestly more useful.

[curious] Right — the away-day. Why does it fail?

[teaching] Not because it's badly run. Because of what it is. A one-off workshop produces a real effect in the room and almost nothing that survives to the following week. The mechanism is practice, and practice means repetition. Nobody learns an instrument at a very good three-hour seminar about instruments.

[conversational] So the away-day generates goodwill.

[confident] And goodwill decays in days. [pause] But there's a second failure and it's more serious. Technique can't substitute for conditions. A team that's under-resourced, or whose manager has no authority upward, or where disagreeing has previously cost somebody something — that team doesn't have a collaboration-skills problem.

[curious] And running games inside that is...

[serious] Being seen to do something. And people know the difference. That's when the workshop does active harm, because it demonstrates the organisation would rather buy an afternoon than change the thing.

[curious] So what should they run instead?

[teaching] Short, repeated, attached to the actual work. Ten minutes at the start of a regular meeting, every week, beats a day once a year — and it's cheaper. A yes-and chain, where each person accepts the previous contribution and adds one thing, no reframing allowed. Mirroring — two people, slow movement, no speaking, the fastest demonstration that attention is physical. Gift giving. One-word story, where nobody can steer it and no individual is exposed.

[curious] None of those require anybody to be funny.

[emphatic] That's the selection criterion. No wit, no solo performance, no personal disclosure. Which is what makes them survivable for the half of the room that was appalled.

[curious] What do you do about that half, actually?

[warm] Take the objection seriously, because it's usually accurate. Most of it is a reasonable response to having been made to perform in front of colleagues with no way to decline. Run formats where everybody moves at once, keep them under ten minutes, never single anybody out, and let people sit out visibly and without explanation. [short pause] The resistance is to exposure, not to improv.

[curious] And if they do bring somebody in?

[teaching] Ask what the session is for, in terms of a behaviour that would change. A good facilitator names something narrow — how a meeting opens, what happens after somebody disagrees. A weak one describes an experience.

[conversational] Anything to decline outright?

[matter-of-fact] Three. Anything promising measurable culture improvements, because that evidence doesn't exist. Anything competitive, because the moment there's a winner people play not to lose. And anything requiring individuals to perform in front of colleagues with no ability to pass — in a room with a power gap, that isn't a game.

[curious] One last thing. Should the boss run it?

[confident] Never. Status guarantees that whatever they do reads as an instruction. [pause] Which is, when you think about it, the whole lesson arriving before the workshop starts.
