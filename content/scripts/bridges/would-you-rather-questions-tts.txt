[curious] Would you rather. It's the oldest game there is, and most of the time it dies in the first two seconds.

[teaching] It dies in one of exactly two ways. Either one option is obviously better — would you rather be rich or poor — which isn't a question, it's a quiz with one right answer. Or neither option is real. A hundred duck-sized horses, one horse-sized duck.

[curious] That one always gets a laugh, though.

[matter-of-fact] Once. It gets a laugh once and then the room is finished with it, because nobody has a stake in an outcome that can't happen to them. It's the same reason the spaceship scene dies. Nothing in it costs anybody anything.

[curious] So what's left when you strip both of those out?

[teaching] Something narrow, and much better. A pair where the room genuinely divides, and where people can say why. [short pause] And here's the thing every list of these gets backwards — the answer is worthless. Nobody has ever learned a single thing from knowing that six people picked flying and four picked invisibility.

[curious] Then what's the game?

[emphatic] The reason. The reason is the whole game. A good pair forces somebody to expose what they're actually optimising for. Safety. Status. Freedom. Being liked. Not being caught. And they usually don't know they're revealing it, which is precisely why it works where a direct question wouldn't.

[contemplative] Because if you ask somebody what they value, you get a considered answer.

[teaching] You get their press release. Make them pick between two things and defend it, and you get the truth by accident. Which is exactly what we're doing when we build a character — you don't discover who somebody is by having them announce it. You put them in a doorway with two exits and watch which one they take.

[curious] So how do you know if a pair is any good before you say it out loud?

[confident] One test. Does it split? If you can predict how the room will answer, it's dead. Eight-two is mildly interesting. Five-five is the point — an even split means the room contains two defensible positions, and the second somebody has to argue for theirs, it stops being a game and becomes a conversation.

[curious] Is there a recipe for the even split, or is it luck?

[teaching] Three things do it reliably. Both options have to cost something — not one good and one bad, but two goods with different prices. The trade-off has to run along a real axis: time against money, certainty against possibility, being known against being left alone. And it has to be answerable from an actual life. If a pair makes you imagine a fantasy self, you'll get a fantasy answer.

[curious] Give me one that works.

[matter-of-fact] Would you rather be slightly underestimated forever, or slightly overestimated forever. [short pause] Watch what happens in a room of improvisers with that one.

[curious] Half of them want the cover and half of them want the room.

[warm] And every one of them will tell you why without being asked. That's the whole machine.

[curious] Okay. What breaks it?

[serious] Two words. It depends.

[curious] That's just somebody being thoughtful, surely.

[emphatic] It's blocking. It's exactly blocking. Somebody hands you a premise and you decline it — you don't take the offer, you interrogate the offer. And it's blocking in its most respectable costume, because it sounds like nuance. One person does it, everybody else has permission, and now you're not playing anything.

[teaching] The opposite move is the actual skill. Choose without full information and back it. That's the thing improvisers train specifically, because a scene where somebody hedges is a scene that has stopped moving. Take the constraint seriously. Pick the worse option if you have to. Defend it anyway.

[contemplative] There's a version of this in the no-backspace exercise, isn't there. Nothing you say can be taken back.

[teaching] Which leaves you exactly one available direction. Forward. And the reflex that builds isn't just useful on stage — it's the thing that makes somebody easy to talk to. The person who commits to an opinion is more fun to disagree with than the person who keeps everything provisional.

[curious] Is there a way to run it badly, though? Beyond the obvious.

[serious] Yes, and this is the part worth being careful about. This format is safe because nothing is true. Nobody's being asked about themselves, both options are hypothetical, and that safety is doing all the work. There are four reliable ways to remove it without noticing.

[curious] Go.

[matter-of-fact] The pair that's secretly a values test. Save one person you love or five strangers — anything from that family stops being a game the moment somebody answers, because now a person in the room has said out loud that they'd let five people die. That follows them out of the evening.

[curious] And the second?

[teaching] The pair with somebody's actual life inside it. Sight or hearing. More time or more money. Know the day you die. All fine in the abstract and completely different if somebody in that room is losing their hearing or buried a parent last month. You often won't know, which is the argument for reading the room instead of the list.

[curious] Third?

[serious] Pressing somebody who picked lightly. The defence is the game, but it only works volunteered. "Why?" is an invitation. "No, but *why*?" is an interrogation. Somebody who chose in half a second with no reason ready is now justifying themselves in front of everyone.

[curious] And the last one?

[matter-of-fact] The pair aimed at one person. Built so that somebody's known situation is the answer. Everybody can tell, including them, and the game becomes a delivery mechanism for a point somebody wanted to make anyway.

[contemplative] There's a slower failure too. When one person's answers keep revealing the same thing — always the money, always the safe one, always the option that avoids being seen — the game has quietly started profiling them.

[teaching] And they notice before anybody else does. Change category before somebody says it out loud.

[curious] So if you only take one thing from the whole format —

[emphatic] Take this. Your first answer is connected to the situation. Your second answer is connected to how you want to appear. [pause] Everyone at that table knows which one they gave you.
