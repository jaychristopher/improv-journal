[curious] I want to start somewhere uncomfortable. Most people who are bad at friendship — they care. That's the thing. So what's actually going wrong?

[teaching] They're blocking. And they're blocking in the exact way that's hardest to see, because every version of it looks like helping.

[curious] Okay, unpack that. On stage blocking is obvious — someone says "we're on a submarine" and you say "no we're not."

[teaching] Right, and everyone in the room clocks it. But in a conversation? Your friend says "work's been really hard," and you say "have you thought about talking to your manager?" That's not a block, that's care. Except — watch what happened to their offer. It's gone. You accepted it on the surface and steered it toward your outcome, which is the conversation ending with the problem solved.

[curious] Because you want to help.

[teaching] Because you want to help. But most people share a problem to be accompanied in it, not to have it closed. The fix arrives as a door shutting.

[matter-of-fact] There are four of these and they're all the same move wearing different coats. Solving, which we just did. Redirecting — "that happened to me too" — which feels like empathy and functions as bulldozing. The tell is that they never get back to what they were saying.

[curious] Ohh. The scene about their thing became a scene about your thing.

[matter-of-fact] Attention left their offer and landed on yours. Third one: reassuring. "You're being too hard on yourself." "It'll be fine."

[curious] That's a block? That's the kindest sentence in the language.

[matter-of-fact] It's contradicting someone's account of their own experience. However kindly meant, it tells them the thing they said wasn't acceptable as stated. And the fourth is silver-lining — "at least you learned something" — which resolves your discomfort at their expense.

[contemplative] Which is the common thread in all four, actually. Every one of them is you managing how their difficulty feels to you.

[curious] So what's the alternative? Because "just listen" is the advice everyone's already failing at.

[teaching] Think about be-supportive. The instruction on stage is: make your partner's choices look brilliant. Optimise for the ensemble, not for yourself. And that's a stranger instruction than it sounds, because it means your job isn't to contribute your best material. It's to make theirs work.

[curious] Which in a conversation means what, concretely?

[teaching] Your next line comes out of theirs. "What was the worst part?" keeps the thread. "Same thing happened to me" starts a new one. That's the whole distinction — extending versus replacing.

[warm] And then the hard one. Let it be unresolved. Sitting with something unfixed is a real contribution, and it feels like doing absolutely nothing, which is precisely why people reach for advice instead. The doing is not the point, and that's unbearable for most of us.

[curious] What about advice, though? It's not banned.

[confident] Not banned. Just worth understanding why it goes wrong. Status is complementary — when you give advice you go up and they go down, momentarily positioned as the person who couldn't work it out. If they asked, that trade is completely fine. If they didn't, it costs something, and the cost gets paid later in what they stop bringing you.

[curious] So you ask.

[confident] "Do you want thoughts, or do you want to be annoyed about it for a bit?" That's not a technique, it's finding out which scene you're in. Most people, asked directly, know.

[curious] Let me push into the serious stuff. Grief, illness, someone's marriage ending. That's where people most want to be good at this.

[serious] And most reliably aren't — not for lack of effort. Every failure mode we just named gets stronger, because the discomfort is larger and the urge to resolve it is proportional.

[serious] "Let me know if you need anything" is the big one. Sounds generous. What it actually does is transfer the work to the person with the least capacity — they now have to identify a task, ask for it, and manage your involvement.

[curious] What's the version that isn't that?

[serious] "I'm bringing food Tuesday, tell me if that's a bad day." A specific offer is refusable, and that's exactly what makes it easy to accept.

[emphatic] And say the awkward thing rather than nothing. People go silent around grief because they're frightened of getting it wrong, and the silence is worse than almost any wrong thing. "I don't know what to say and I'm not going anywhere" is enough. The absence is what gets remembered.

[contemplative] Then show up after the first fortnight. Everyone appears immediately and most vanish within a month — which is roughly when it stops being acute and starts being long. Being the person still asking in week six costs very little and is rarer than any amount of early intensity.

[curious] Repair. Because friendships rupture.

[teaching] All of them. Every long friendship contains several — the ones that last are the ones where somebody repaired them. The improv version is fracture-recovery: you're in different scenes and neither of you can work out why it feels off. The move isn't to relitigate who was right. It's to name the state plainly and rebuild shared reality from there.

[emphatic] Two things make it possible. Go first, without a ledger — waiting for them to apologise first is the position that ends friendships, because both people can hold it forever and both feel justified.

[emphatic] And don't defend before you've understood. Defensiveness is a block on an offer that happens to be critical. You can receive the complaint fully and disagree with it afterwards — doing it in that order is what makes the disagreement survivable. Reversing it is what turns a small rupture into a permanent one.

[curious] Last thing. How do you know if you're any good at this?

[contemplative] Don't look at what you do for them. Look at what they tell you. Effort's a bad indicator because it's the part you can see.

[warm] The signal is whether they bring you things while they're still unresolved. A problem in progress, not a story with the ending already attached. People route unfinished things to whoever handles them well, and quietly stop routing them to whoever solves, minimises or redirects.

[contemplative] So if a friend now only tells you about difficulties after they're over — that's information. And it usually arrived long before anybody noticed.

[matter-of-fact] Which is why the boring part does most of the work. Trust is an accumulated estimate built from a lot of small exchanges. Answering the message. Remembering the interview they mentioned last month. Turning up predictably.

[teaching] The dramatic version — where you appear at the crisis and are magnificent — is the part people plan for and the part that matters least. By the time the crisis arrives, whether you're someone they call has already been decided by two years of very small moments.
