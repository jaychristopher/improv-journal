[curious] Okay, I want to start with a confession. I have taught this thing wrong. Out loud. To a room.

[warm] You and everyone else. Which four words did you mangle?

[curious] "Yes, and." I told a group of beginners it means agree with everything. Never say no. Stay positive.

[teaching] Right. And what you get from that room is scenes where nothing happens, very pleasantly. Two people confirming each other's existence for four minutes. Nobody blocked anything. Nobody built anything either.

[curious] So where's the actual line? Because I've heard "you can disagree" but I've never heard it explained in a way that stuck.

[teaching] The line is between the fiction and the people making it. Your characters can fight, lie, storm out, hate each other. All of that is on the table. What can't happen is one performer declining what the other performer put into the room.

[curious] Give me the hospital version.

[teaching] Your partner says we're in a hospital. You are not required to say "yes, and I love hospitals." You're required to be in a hospital. Be furious about it. Want to leave. Refuse to sit down. What you can't do is say "no, we're in a restaurant," because now there's no shared world to build in.

[matter-of-fact] So the test isn't whether you're being agreeable. It's whether the thing your partner added is still true one line later.

[curious] That reframes the whole thing. [short pause] But I want to push on the "yes" part, because I think I've been treating it as a decision.

[teaching] It isn't. It's perception before it's cooperation. You cannot accept an offer you didn't notice, and most failures here aren't failures of generosity — they're failures of attention. The person was composing their next line while the world was being built around them.

[emphatic] And offers are so much wider than dialogue. A sigh. A hesitation before answering. Where somebody chose to stand in relation to you. All of that adds to the shared reality, and all of it can be accepted or ignored. The word "yes" is doing a lot of work for something that's mostly listening.

[curious] Okay. So the "yes" is attention. What's the "and"?

[teaching] The half people drop. And honestly, dropping the "and" is the more common failure among people who've learned the phrase. They're so busy not-blocking that they forget to bring anything.

[conversational] Let me do this with one offer and four replies. Somebody says: "I have been standing out here for twenty minutes."

[matter-of-fact] Reply one: "No you haven't, you just got here." That's a block. The reality is denied, and now the scene is an argument about whose account is true.

[matter-of-fact] Reply two: "Have you? Why were you waiting?" Nothing denied. Nothing supplied. The work gets handed straight back.

[matter-of-fact] Reply three: "Twenty minutes. Wow." Yes with no and. Warm, agreeable, and the scene is exactly where it was before anybody spoke.

[emphatic] Reply four: "I know. I watched you park, walk past the door twice, and come back."

[curious] Oh, that's a whole scene now.

[teaching] It's a scene about somebody avoiding going in. And notice how little it cost — one fact accepted, one fact added. Only that last reply leaves the other person with more than they started with. That's the actual test. Not the tone of the reply. What the other person is left holding.

[curious] Does that survive off stage? Because this is where it usually gets gross.

[contemplative] It survives if you're precise. Try this one. "We should ship it Friday." And the reply: "Friday is not going to work, and I think the reason is the migration nobody has scoped."

[curious] That's a no.

[confident] That's a yes-and. What's accepted is that a date has been proposed and a decision is now live. What follows is a flat refusal of the date and a real addition — a reason, and information the other person didn't have. Set it beside "Friday doesn't work," which accepts and adds nothing. And beside "Sure, Friday," which is agreement, and is the thing this principle gets mistaken for constantly.

[curious] So what's the actual opposite? Blocking?

[teaching] That's the textbook answer and the least useful one. Anybody flatly contradicting people gets told within a fortnight of starting. The thing that actually happens is quieter, and its distinguishing feature is that it's polite.

[serious] "Yes, but." "Totally — although what if instead." The words are received warmly and the substance gets swapped out underneath them. Nobody refused anything, so there's no moment anyone can object to.

[curious] How do you even catch that?

[matter-of-fact] Look at what happened to the last thing somebody offered. If it was genuinely built on, the idea moved forward carrying their contribution. If it was negated, the conversation is now somewhere else, and the person who offered has gone quiet. [pause] That diagnostic needs no vocabulary at all. It works in a meeting as well as a scene.

[curious] There's a case against teaching this first, isn't there.

[teaching] Napier's, and it's serious. His argument is that "yes, and" as an opening instruction produces compliant, passionless scenes. Give a beginner a rule and they'll monitor themselves for compliance with it — and self-monitoring is exactly the state that makes people stiff and unspecific. They become careful instead of present.

[contemplative] His position is that the phrase describes good scene work better than it teaches it. A lens for looking back at a scene that worked, not a directive to run in real time.

[emphatic] And here's the practical version of that critique — you can say the word "yes" while denying the reality entirely. It lives in behaviour, not vocabulary. A room can be scrupulously verbal about it while nobody is genuinely building. When it's actually working, nobody on stage is thinking about it.

[curious] Last thing. Where does it stop working?

[serious] Four places, and the first one nobody in a business context mentions. In a room with a hierarchy, "we're a yes-and culture" can function as an instruction not to object to the senior person's idea. Improv assumes two performers with equal standing and a shared interest in the scene. A meeting is often neither.

[matter-of-fact] Second — some things should be blocked. A proposal that will hurt somebody, work that's genuinely a bad idea. Improv's version is bounded by a stage and four minutes. Decisions aren't.

[matter-of-fact] Third, it's generative, and generative moves are the wrong tool for converging. A group that yes-ands indefinitely produces an enormous pile of accepted ideas and no choice. The improv version has an editor and a running time. A project usually has neither unless somebody provides them.

[serious] And fourth. Applied to a person who isn't doing it back, it's just absorption. Two performers building is a system. One person accepting and extending while the other redirects everything is not a partnership with better manners — it's one person doing all the work, and the phrase gives that arrangement a flattering name.

[curious] So what do I actually practise?

[teaching] Two things off the mat. Run one meeting where your first response to any proposal has to name something usable in it before you say anything else. You'll find out fast how much of your normal contribution was redirection.

[warm] And watch one conversation you're not in. Track only whether offers come back. [pause] It is the fastest way to stop mistaking politeness for building.
