[curious] Okay, warm-ups. Everyone has a list. Pass the Clap, Zip Zap Zop, whatever. What's the thing people get wrong?

[teaching] They're asking which game. That's the wrong question. The question is what order you run three of them in.

[curious] Order. Why does that matter more than the games themselves?

[teaching] Because the people walking into that room can't do improv yet. Not because they're bad. Because they're still running the mode that got them through the day. Plan ahead. Monitor how you're coming across. Don't look stupid in front of people who know you.

[emphatic] Every single one of those is fatal on stage.

[curious] So the warm-up isn't about energy.

[matter-of-fact] The warm-up is twenty minutes of dismantling those three habits. And dismantling them has an order, because they don't come off at the same speed.

[curious] What happens if you just grab a game off a list?

[conversational] You often get a room that's louder and no more able to play than when it walked in. Which feels like it worked. Everybody's sweaty, everybody laughed.

[teaching] The reason is that most warm-up lists sort games by what they *are*. Circle games. Name games. Energy games. Nobody sorts them by what they *do to the person playing them*. A nervous group doesn't need a game that requires invention. A loud, scattered group doesn't need more energy. Run the wrong stage first and you've entertained people instead of preparing them.

[curious] Give me the three stages.

[teaching] Stage one. Move before you ask anybody to think. Four to six minutes. No ideas required, nothing verbal, impossible to be bad at.

[curious] Impossible to be bad at is doing a lot of work in that sentence.

[confident] It's the whole thing. Pass the Clap — a clap travels round the circle on eye contact. No words. No ideas. Just attention to one person at a time. It's the best opening game there is for exactly that reason. Bippity Bippity Bop is the other one I'd name, because it fails people constantly and cheerfully. Which is precisely what you want in the first five minutes.

[curious] Failing people is the goal?

[matter-of-fact] Watch for the opposite. If everyone's getting the pattern right every time, the game is too easy to be doing anything. Speed it up until the mistakes start.

[curious] Okay. Stage two.

[teaching] Stage two is where you take their attention off themselves and put it on each other. Five to eight minutes. Still no invention — but now it's shared.

[conversational] Stage one gets an individual out of their own head. That's not the same as a group. A set of individuals who are each relaxed is still a set of individuals. Ensemble doesn't arrive because you asked for it.

[curious] What does that look like?

[teaching] Sound Ball — you throw an imaginary ball with a sound, someone catches it with the same sound. And the entire discipline is returning exactly what you were given instead of improving it. [short pause] Which is harder than it sounds, and tells you something about the person who keeps improving it.

[warm] But the diagnostic one is counting to twenty. One voice at a time, no system, no turn-taking, restart whenever two people speak at once.

[curious] And that tells you what?

[matter-of-fact] A room that gets to twenty on the second try is ready for anything. A room that keeps colliding at four has people in it who are waiting for a turn rather than listening. [pause] Name that out loud before you move on. It's the most useful thing you'll say all session.

[curious] Stage three.

[emphatic] Stage three is the one everybody skips. And skipping it is exactly why a group can finish a lively, sweaty, hilarious warm-up and then produce a first scene that dies on the floor.

[teaching] The last game before the work should be a small, safe version of the exact thing you're about to ask for. If the session is about accepting offers, finish on Yes, Let's — somebody proposes, everyone answers in unison and does it. Four minutes, and it installs the physical habit of starting on somebody else's word rather than your own. If it's about listening, finish on Last Word Response — your first word has to be your partner's last. Makes not-listening structurally impossible.

[curious] So the first two games are about state and the last one is about content.

[confident] That's the whole argument in one line.

[curious] Does the shape change depending on who's in front of you?

[teaching] The stages hold. The weighting shifts. Strangers need longer in stage one than feels necessary — they need to see failure survived before they'll risk inventing anything. That's safety, not energy.

[contemplative] But here's the one that surprises people. A group that's played together for years? Compress stage one to a single game. What they actually need is stage two.

[curious] Why would experienced players need the listening one more?

[matter-of-fact] Because familiarity makes people confident. And confidence quietly erodes listening. You stop attending because you think you already know what they're going to do.

[curious] How do you know it worked?

[teaching] Not laughter. Everybody uses laughter and it's the wrong test. The test is whether they're looking at each other. A warm room makes eye contact without being told to, responds before it's composed a response, and has stopped checking who's watching.

[conversational] A room that's loud but still playing to the teacher has had a nice time. It isn't ready.

[warm] The reliable tell: nobody asks what the next game is. They're still in the last one.

[curious] There are good improvisers who don't warm up at all.

[matter-of-fact] Serious ones, not lazy ones. The claim is that what a warm-up manufactures — attention, trust, shared baseline — is already there in a group that's played together for years. And they're largely right about ensembles. And largely wrong about classes, because a room of people who met twenty minutes ago has nothing else to build a baseline out of.

[contemplative] Spolin's position cuts underneath both of those, though. She'd say the games aren't preliminary to the work. The games *are* the methodology. Each one sets a point of concentration that occupies the analytical mind and leaves the spontaneous one free.

[curious] So the warm-up isn't a runway.

[emphatic] It's a short version of the thing itself.
