[curious] Every question game I can think of is about the person answering. This one isn't.

[teaching] That's the whole thing. Most likely to is the only one where the answer is about somebody else in the room — and that person doesn't get a vote. They just receive it.

[curious] Which sounds like a small structural detail.

[matter-of-fact] It's not. It explains everything about how the game goes well and every way it goes wrong. Think about what you're actually doing. You're standing in a circle and the group is assembling a portrait of each person, out loud, without asking permission.

[contemplative] So the pleasure isn't the joke.

[warm] The pleasure is being recognised. "Most likely to reply at 2am" means somebody has been paying attention to you. That's the gift. The laugh is a side effect of the accuracy.

[curious] Okay, but I've been in the version where it turns. Where somebody gets named and the room goes a little quiet.

[teaching] Right, and there's a mechanism for that worth naming. Status. Every interaction has an invisible negotiation of who's up and who's down, and most likely to is a status game whether anyone intends it or not — because each round assigns a label to a specific person by group vote.

[emphatic] And when the label names a deficiency, the group has now voted on somebody's standing.

[serious] With no comfortable reply available. That's the part. If you object, you can't take a joke. So the cost is invisible to everybody except the person paying it, and it shows up three weeks later, not in the room.

[curious] So what's the line? Because "always late" and "unreliable" describe the same behaviour.

[confident] That's exactly the test, and it's a good one. Name habits, not shortcomings. Always late is a habit — it's a thing you do. Unreliable is a character judgement. Identical behaviour. Completely different landing.

[curious] Does being named feel like being seen, or like being caught.

[warm] That's the whole page in one sentence. Those two are near neighbours and the game lives entirely on one side of the line.

[curious] Here's what strikes me as an improv thing. Making somebody else look good, fast, in public, without it sounding like flattery — that's an exercise.

[teaching] It's gift-giving. You hand your scene partner something that makes them more interesting than you. Most likely to is the same reflex in a social format. "Most likely to make friends with the staff" is a gift. You've endowed somebody with warmth, and now the room sees them that way for the rest of the night.

[contemplative] Endowment. It's just endowment with a vote attached.

[emphatic] And like endowment on stage, the specific one is always better than the general one. "Most likely to be funny" is nothing. "Most likely to argue with a self-checkout machine" is a whole character.

[short pause]

[curious] What about the ones that just don't work at all?

[matter-of-fact] Anything naming a failure. Most likely to get fired, most likely to be single forever. You've voted on somebody's future in front of them. Anything about appearance or money — there's no good version, there's no reply that doesn't cost them something. And anything one person has actually done. If the room is thinking of a specific incident, that's not a game, that's a reminder with a format.

[curious] And playing it with strangers?

[serious] Worst case. Nobody can be recognised for something they've had no time to demonstrate, so the answers become guesses based on appearance. Which is the worst possible first impression to hand somebody.

[curious] How does that compare to never have I ever? Because those feel like the same genre.

[teaching] Opposite direction of disclosure. Never have I ever is self-disclosure — you reveal something about yourself and you control how much. Most likely to is other-disclosure. The group reveals what it thinks about you, and you can't decline the round.

[contemplative] So one is safe with strangers and gets riskier as the night goes on.

[confident] And the other wants a group that already likes each other. It's the wrong game for a room where anybody is still working out where they stand.

[curious] Which is a lot like scene work, actually. You can't endow somebody heavily on their first class.

[warm] You can't. Endowment lands in proportion to trust. Same rule.

[short pause]

[curious] Give me the mechanics. How do you actually run it well.

[matter-of-fact] Everybody points at once, on three. Simultaneous, so later votes don't follow earlier ones — that drift is what turns a round into a pile-on.

[curious] Ah. Herding.

[teaching] Exactly that. Then the named person gets one sentence. Not a defence — a reply. And that's where most of the laughs actually live, because they know something about themselves that the group only half-guessed.

[curious] Anything else?

[emphatic] Rotate who reads. Whoever holds the list steers the game, and one person reading all evening means the whole thing reflects one sense of humour. And stop before the good ones run out. Fifteen strong prompts beats forty where the last twenty were reaching.

[curious] That last one is just editing.

[warm] It's the same instinct as getting out of a scene at the top of the laugh. Nobody has ever regretted stopping early.

[curious] What do you do when somebody takes it badly?

[serious] Move on immediately. Do not repair it in public.

[curious] Really? The instinct is to explain.

[matter-of-fact] The instinct is to explain that it was a joke, nobody meant it, they're being oversensitive. Every one of those keeps them at the centre of the room's attention, which is the actual injury. The prompt was ten seconds. The discussion of whether they should mind is five minutes, and the five minutes is what they'll remember.

[contemplative] So you read the next one.

[warm] You read the next one. And later, privately, you say the true version of what you think about them. Not an apology for the game — that just invites them to insist they're fine. The specific thing the vote got wrong.

[teaching] Safety in a room is built out of small exchanges that go unpunished. The group is watching what happens to somebody who was uncomfortable, and learning from it. The recovery teaches more than the incident did.

[short pause]

[curious] One more. Group size.

[confident] Five is the floor, and it's arithmetic rather than atmosphere. With three or four the votes concentrate — too few candidates, so the same person gets named for everything, and being named four times running stops reading as recognition and starts reading as a verdict.

[curious] And at the top end?

[matter-of-fact] Past about twelve, the quiet ones stop getting named at all. Which is its own problem. An ensemble is a group where everybody's contribution is load-bearing, and a game that names the same four extroverts for an hour is describing a smaller group than the one actually in the room.

[contemplative] That's the note, isn't it. It's not really about the prompts.

[warm] It's about whether everybody in the room gets to be the answer to something. That's the same job as ensemble awareness on stage — noticing who hasn't been given anything yet, and giving them something. The game just makes it explicit.
