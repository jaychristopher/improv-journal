[curious] Everybody's seen the list. Five rules, same five, every website. Say yes and, don't ask questions, make your partner look good, don't negate, there are no mistakes. What's your problem with it?

[teaching] My problem is that nobody who wrote that list was in the room when any of it was said. Those five are a compression. They come mostly from Tina Fey summarizing her Second City training in a memoir, which was summarizing Del Close, who wasn't really giving rules in the first place. And Johnstone — who's arguably the most influential teacher in the form — isn't on that list at all. He was suspicious of lists.

[curious] So the rules contradict each other because they're answers to different questions.

[matter-of-fact] Right. Stack them into one list and you get something that looks coherent and isn't. Let's take them one at a time.

[curious] Yes, and. Start there, because that's the one that escaped improv entirely.

[teaching] And got mangled on the way out. People hear "agree with everything." That's not it. Your characters can disagree, lie, refuse, storm out. What can't happen is the performer refusing the performer's offer. Your partner says "we're in a hospital" — you don't have to love the hospital. You just can't say "no, we're in a restaurant."

[emphatic] The acceptance is non-negotiable. The and is where your point of view lives. Those are two different jobs and the phrase smashes them together.

[curious] Johnstone doesn't even use the phrase, does he.

[contemplative] Never. His version is "be changed by what happens," which I think is the better sentence. It puts the burden on you rather than on your response. And Napier at the Annoyance goes further — he argues that obsessing over yes-and produces wimpy, compliant, passionless scenes. Which, if you've watched a beginner show recently, you've seen. Everyone agreeing pleasantly for four minutes.

[curious] Okay. Don't ask questions.

[matter-of-fact] This one I'd throw out. Not soften — throw out.

[curious] Strong.

[teaching] It was invented to solve one specific beginner problem: people asking questions to avoid making a choice. "What do you do for a living?" is you handing your partner the creative work. Fair diagnosis. Terrible prescription, because it bans a normal feature of human speech and you get these stilted scenes where nobody can ask anything.

[emphatic] The issue was never questions. It's empty questions. "What do you do?" — empty. "Are you seriously wearing that to Dad's funeral?" — there's a funeral, there's a father, there's a relationship, there's a judgment, all inside a question mark. That's not dodging a choice, that's four choices at once.

[curious] So the rule should be: don't use questions to avoid committing.

[warm] And do use them when they carry offers. That's the whole correction.

[curious] Make your partner look good.

[teaching] This is the one all five traditions actually agree on, and it's the least argued about, which probably means it's the truest. Johnstone frames it as status — lower yours so their offer can land. Close and Halpern call it ensemble responsibility. UCB builds it into game structure.

[matter-of-fact] What it doesn't mean is be nice. It means track what your partner needs to succeed and hand it to them. Sometimes that's setting up their joke. Sometimes it's shutting up. Sometimes it's playing a character who makes their life very difficult, because the scene needs the pressure.

[curious] Don't negate.

[teaching] Negation is a technical term and people hear it as "never say no." Negation is destroying the established reality. "We're not in a hospital" — negation. "I refuse to go to the hospital" — that's drama. That's a scene.

[curious] And Johnstone's taxonomy goes way past the word no.

[matter-of-fact] Much further. Wimping — accepting without adding anything. Cancelling — making an offer and immediately pulling it back. Hedging — vague choices so you can't be pinned to one. Pimping — forcing your partner to do the work. All negation. None of them involve saying no.

[contemplative] And Napier's counterpoint again: the fear of negating does more damage than negating. A performer so scared of saying no that they agree with everything gives you a scene with no tension, no stakes, no person in it.

[curious] Last one. There are no mistakes.

[teaching] Heard as: quality doesn't matter, everything's fine. Close's version is sharper — fall, then figure out what to do on the way down. The accidental word, the weird physical thing your body did before your brain approved it, the moment of genuine confusion. Those aren't errors. They're offers from a part of you that isn't managing the scene.

[emphatic] But the standard doesn't disappear. It moves. It stops being "did I execute my plan" and becomes "did I use what happened." A mistake you ignore is a missed offer. A mistake you build on is a gift.

[curious] Now — these get taken to work constantly. Which ones survive the trip?

[serious] Yes-and survives and gets abused worst. In a meeting it turns into never disagree, which is the inversion of what it means on stage. Accepting an offer means accepting that a thing has been said and is now real in the room. Not agreeing to it. "Yes, we're behind, and I think the deadline is the problem" — that's a yes-and. A room where nobody can say "that won't work" hasn't got psychological safety. It's got silence. And from the front of the room those look identical.

[curious] And don't-ask-questions at work?

[matter-of-fact] Actively harmful. On stage it's shaky. At work it's dangerous, because questions are how you find out the thing nobody told you. The most common failure in a meeting isn't excessive curiosity — it's somebody acting on an assumption they never checked.

[warm] Make your partner look good transfers cleanest and gets discussed least. It's the entire mechanism behind why some teams hear about problems early. If flagging an issue makes you look bad and the person who caused it look bad, nobody flags anything. Credit the person who raised it and you get told about the next one sooner.

[curious] Is there a pattern to which ones travel?

[contemplative] A rule travels when it was solving a human problem — evaluating instead of participating, competing instead of building. It fails when it was solving a stage problem specific to beginners. That's also your test for whatever new rule someone hands you in a workshop.

[curious] So what's underneath all five?

[teaching] Same thing, five ways. Yes-and: stop evaluating, start participating. Don't ask questions: stop deflecting, start committing. Make your partner look good: stop competing, start collaborating. Don't negate: stop controlling, start building. No mistakes: stop judging, start using.

[emphatic] Every one of them is trying to get you out of your head and into the scene. They're training wheels for a single skill — being present enough to respond to what's actually happening instead of what you planned.

[curious] And once you can do that?

[contemplative] You stop consulting them. Which isn't the same as stopping. The behaviour becomes automatic and the rule turns into a description of what you already do. You can see it under pressure — someone running a checklist gets slower as the stakes rise, because checking costs the attention the scene needs. Someone who's absorbed the same material gets quieter. More present. Both of them would tell you they believe in yes-and. Only one of them is thinking about it.
