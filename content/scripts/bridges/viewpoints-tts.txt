[curious] Okay, here's a phrase that comes up in workshops and I've never gotten a straight answer on it. Viewpoints. Nine of them. What are they actually for?

[teaching] They're a way of splitting your physical awareness into channels you can pay attention to one at a time. That's the whole thing. Tempo, duration, kinesthetic response, repetition — those are the time ones. Shape, gesture, architecture, spatial relationship, topography — those are the space ones.

[curious] Nine feels like a lot of things to track at once.

[matter-of-fact] You don't track them at once. That's the point of naming them. Think about the notes you've gotten your whole training. Be present. Fill the space. Listen with your body. Commit.

[curious] [short pause] Every one of those, yeah.

[emphatic] None of them tell you what to do. If you can already do it, you didn't need the note. If you can't, you've been handed nothing. Viewpoints names nine things somebody outside your body can see, and you can change on purpose. That's the entire trick.

[curious] So give me one. The one that actually shows up in scenes.

[teaching] Spatial relationship. The distance between two bodies and how it changes. Take a conversation — any conversation — and play it four feet apart. Then play the same words at eighteen inches. Then across a twenty-foot room.

[curious] Three different scenes.

[warm] Three different scenes, and nobody changed a word. Distance was carrying the relationship the whole time. It always is. The only question is whether anyone chose it, or whether you're standing wherever your feet happened to stop.

[contemplative] [pause] That's most two-handers I've been in, honestly. The ones that die standing up.

[matter-of-fact] The talking-heads scene is a spatial-relationship failure before it's a content failure. Two people at a fixed distance, facing out, have switched off the channel an audience reads most reliably. And the fix isn't moving. Everybody thinks the fix is moving.

[curious] It's not?

[emphatic] It's *changing* the distance. You can pace around all night at a constant six feet and nothing reads. Close it once, on purpose, and the scene turns over.

[curious] Okay, what about the time ones? Tempo I get. Duration sounds like nothing.

[teaching] Duration is the one everybody skips. How long something goes on. Untrained performers end things at roughly the length that feels comfortable — and comfortable is short. Almost every piece of staging you've ever seen and thought, that was bold, is somebody doing something ordinary and holding it past the point where they wanted to stop.

[contemplative] Which is the same reason silence is terrifying.

[warm] Same muscle exactly. And it's not just stage. Watch what happens in a meeting when somebody asks a real question and then doesn't fill the gap. The whole room reorganises around that. They're playing duration, whether they'd call it that or not.

[curious] Where does this come from? Bogart invented it?

[matter-of-fact] Adapted it. Mary Overlie built six Viewpoints out of postmodern dance in the seventies — space, shape, time, emotion, movement, story. Her argument was that those six are *equal*. Conventional theatre makes everything serve story. Movement serves the story, space serves the story. Overlie refused that hierarchy, and the refusal was the whole point.

[curious] And Bogart's nine?

[teaching] Bogart and Landau pulled it into the rehearsal room and cut Overlie's single category of Space into five finer ones — shape, gesture, architecture, spatial relationship, topography. Dance could work with a broad category. Actors negotiating furniture and doors and each other needed the finer grain.

[curious] So what got lost?

[serious] Emotion and story didn't survive the translation. The nine are more usable and a lot less pointed. It's a training vocabulary now, not a claim about what theatre is. Overlie eventually wrote her own book, partly because the version everybody had learned wasn't hers.

[curious] Fair. So what does a session look like?

[matter-of-fact] Empty floor, a group, somebody names a channel out loud, then shuts up. No material, no agreed outcome. Twenty minutes is short. An hour is normal.

[curious] An hour of walking around a room.

[warm] The length is doing something. The first stretch is people making choices — everyone deciding what to contribute. Somewhere in there the choosing runs out of gas, and what's left is responding.

[curious] And then the magic-sounding part where everyone starts agreeing on stuff nobody said.

[matter-of-fact] Which is completely unmagical once you say the mechanism. Ten people told to be aware of each other will each track something different. Ten people tracking *tempo* will clock the same acceleration within a beat of each other, and act on it. A named channel gives everyone the same thing to look at. Shared attention, synchronised noticing.

[curious] [short pause] I want to push on something. I've been in Viewpoints sessions that felt incredible and produced nothing.

[serious] Yeah. That's the main failure and it's worth naming. It produces atmosphere and calls it work. A room of people moving thoughtfully at varied tempos *looks* like something's happening. The test is whether anybody can name what changed — a tempo that shifted, a gesture that got picked up. If the only answer is that it felt good, you had a mood.

[curious] What's the fix?

[teaching] Composition — which is the half that gets dropped whenever the method travels. Composition is building short pieces from tight, strange assignments. Two minutes, three entrances, one repeated gesture, a moment of unison. Viewpoints generates the material. Composition is where it gets selected and judged.

[emphatic] Without it there's no evaluative half. Nothing in an open session ends it, decides it worked, or tells a strong session from a long one. That's why people run Viewpoints for years with no idea whether they're improving.

[curious] What else goes wrong?

[serious] It rewards the already-comfortable. No words, no task, sustained attention to your own body in a room of people watching — that's easy for physically confident performers and genuinely punishing for everyone else. Bring it in too early and you teach self-conscious people they're bad at something nobody's taught them yet. The nine channels are the cure for that, not the cause — but only if you name one and work it, instead of opening the floor and hoping.

[curious] Alright. Sell me on it as an improviser. Where does this actually pay?

[teaching] Four places. Initiations first. The standard note is bring a strong choice, which in practice means walk on with an idea and announce it. A Viewpoints-trained player can start from tempo — walk in slow, or fast, and let the pace be the offer.

[curious] Before you've committed to anything.

[warm] Before either of you has. Your partner has something to match or break, and no premise has been spent. That's exactly what a first line usually fails to do.

[teaching] Second, group scenes. Six people onstage is where most teams fall apart, because everyone's individually deciding whether to enter. Repetition and kinesthetic response are the trained answer — enter on somebody else's movement, echo a shape already in the space. It stops being six soloists and starts being a room.

[curious] And edits? Because edits are always taught as taste.

[emphatic] Topography and spatial relationship make it observable. When the floor pattern has stopped changing and the distance between two people has been fixed for a while — the scene has stopped moving. The dialogue can keep going for another ninety seconds, but it's done. And that's a cue you can read from the back line.

[contemplative] [pause] So you're not guessing anymore. You're looking at something.

[warm] That's what all of it is. Nine things you can see, in a craft that mostly hands you adjectives.

[curious] Where do I start if I don't have a studio full of people?

[matter-of-fact] Take one channel into a rehearsal you're already having. Run a scene you know. Attend only to spatial relationship. Change nothing else.

[curious] And?

[warm] The scene changes anyway. Every time. Because distance was doing work the whole run and nobody had noticed.
