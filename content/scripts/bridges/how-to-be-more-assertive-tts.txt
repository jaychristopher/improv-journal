[curious] Okay, here's a thing I keep noticing. Somebody gives you the assertiveness advice — speak up, don't apologize, set your boundary. And you nod. And then the moment comes and none of it happens.

[teaching] Because it was never a knowledge problem. You knew the sentence. You had it drafted. What happened is your body said something different from what your mouth said, and the room went with the body.

[curious] Every time?

[matter-of-fact] Every time. You walk in to ask for the raise, your boss looks at you, and your voice drops a little, your shoulders come in, and you hear yourself go "it's not a big deal, but I was wondering if maybe—" You didn't forget what to say. You got outvoted.

[curious] So what's actually being read there?

[teaching] Status. Johnstone's whole territory. And the useful part of his framing is that status isn't who has power on the org chart. It's who's behaving right now like they have it. It's postural. It's negotiated in the first three seconds, before anybody says a word.

[conversational] Which improvisers know in their bones, because we establish character physically before dialogue. You walk on, you take a stance, and the audience has already decided who you are.

[emphatic] And here's the line that actually changes something. Status is a behavior, not an identity. You are not a low-status person. You are a person who, in this specific room, defaults to certain behaviors. Behaviors are trainable.

[curious] So the standard advice targets the words.

[matter-of-fact] The words are maybe twenty percent of the signal. You can say "I deserve this raise" while breaking eye contact and touching your face, and what lands is "I don't think I deserve this raise." The body doesn't lose that argument. It never loses that argument.

[curious] Give me the actual levers.

[teaching] Three. First one is stillness. The single most status-raising thing available to you is not moving when you don't need to move. Foot tapping, pen clicking, weight shifting, hair — all of it reads as discomfort with the space you're in.

[conversational] Not rigidity, though. Economy. When you move, you meant it. When you're still, you're present. That combination reads as somebody who belongs in the room.

[curious] Second?

[teaching] The pause. [short pause] Low-status speakers rush, because silence feels like an opening someone will take. High-status speakers let it sit. Before answering. Between sentences. After the point lands.

[emphatic] Two seconds. Try it. Somebody asks you something, wait two full seconds. It will feel like an eternity to you and completely normal to them. That asymmetry is the whole trick.

[curious] And the third.

[teaching] Eye contact on your terms. Low-status eye contact is reactive — you look at them when they talk, and away when they look back at you. High-status is initiatory. You look when you choose, hold as long as you choose, release when you're ready.

[curious] Okay, but this is where people get nervous. There's a version of all that which is just... being kind of a jerk.

[serious] Right, and "don't go too far" is useless as advice, so here's the mechanism instead. Status is complementary. It's a property of the relationship, not of you. When one person raises, the other feels pressure to lower. That pressure is real and it's why assertiveness feels dangerous.

[teaching] So — aggression raises your status by lowering theirs. Interrupting, correcting them in front of people, holding the eye contact past the point where they've yielded. The gain is extracted from somebody.

[emphatic] Assertiveness raises yours and leaves theirs alone. You finish your sentence. You hold your pause. You say the thing without the disclaimer. Nothing was taken from anyone.

[curious] So the test isn't how it felt.

[matter-of-fact] The test is whether they got smaller. If your becoming clearer required them to get quieter, that was the other thing.

[curious] Let's do the one everybody actually came for. Saying no.

[teaching] The refusal almost never fails on content. It fails on delivery. "No" is not the problem — the problem is everything wrapped around it. The apology, the three reasons, the trailing "unless that's a problem?"

[conversational] All of that is low-status signal, and here's why it matters practically: a hedged no reads as an opening position. You've told them it's negotiable. So they negotiate.

[emphatic] Three fixes. Say it and stop — the silence after the refusal is the entire thing, and filling it is where you hand the ground back. Give one reason, not three, because three reasons is a case and a case can be argued with. And do not soften it retroactively. That impulse to circle back with "sorry, I know that's annoying" is the same reflex, arriving late.

[curious] None of which requires being cold.

[warm] None of it. Warmth is a completely separate channel from status. The most effective thing in the world is a clear refusal delivered warmly — and that combination is unavailable to anyone who thinks assertiveness means getting harder.

[curious] How do you actually practice this?

[teaching] Status transfer. Run a scene high, run it low, then shift it mid-scene. Producing status deliberately is what makes your own default visible — and the default is the thing that's actually limiting you. You can't adjust a setting you can't see.

[conversational] The no-backspace scene is the other one. Nothing can be retracted or qualified. Because most under-assertiveness isn't silence — it's the softening that arrives one beat after you've already said the true thing.

[curious] What about when they push back?

[matter-of-fact] Repeat the same sentence. Don't produce a new one.

[curious] That feels stubborn.

[serious] It reads as settled, not stubborn — and it's the opposite of what your instinct wants. Your instinct wants to justify, and every new justification is a fresh surface to attack. Three more things to disagree with, and the original point is now buried under your own explaining.

[teaching] What it costs is commitment. You have to tolerate a few seconds where you're offering nothing new and they have to respond to what's already sitting there. Most pushback isn't an argument. It's a test of whether the position was real.

[curious] Last thing. Is this always the goal — get higher?

[emphatic] No, and that's the part that makes it more useful than the advice it replaces. The most socially skilled people adjust fluidly. Sometimes you raise: negotiation, presentation, holding ground. Sometimes you lower deliberately — building trust, making someone comfortable, letting yourself be the one who doesn't know. More movement, quicker responses, reactive gaze. Those are tools too.

[contemplative] A scene where both players go high is a power struggle. Both low is a puddle. The art was never in the altitude, it's in the movement.

[teaching] So the goal isn't to be assertive. It's to choose your level instead of having it chosen for you by a default you never examined.

[matter-of-fact] You're not stuck with the default. You just haven't rehearsed the alternative.
