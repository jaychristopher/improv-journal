[curious] Here's something I've noticed about improvisers at parties. We're often the worst people in the room at party games.

[teaching] Because we run them like a set. We announce the game, explain the format, put ourselves at the front of the kitchen, and start calling on people. Which is a show. We've just turned somebody's living room into a show, and nobody bought a ticket.

[curious] So what's the alternative? You just... don't play?

[matter-of-fact] You start without saying you've started. That's the whole trick. The word "game" is the problem — the moment you say it, everybody in earshot begins privately calculating whether they can get to the kitchen before their turn comes. [short pause] Ask two people a would-you-rather in a completely ordinary voice and let the circle grow on its own. Nothing was announced, so nothing can fail.

[contemplative] That's just an offer, isn't it. A casual one.

[warm] It's exactly an offer. Everything you say is something somebody else can build on, and a question asked sideways is a low-status offer — easy to take, cheap to ignore. If it lands, you have a game. If it doesn't, you had a conversation. Nobody had to be a good sport about anything.

[curious] Okay, but there are hundreds of these games. How do you tell a good one from one that's about to make the evening worse?

[teaching] Four tests. And almost every party game failure is one of these four. [pause] First: nobody can lose. The second a game produces a loser, it produces a person who now has to publicly perform being fine about it, and that's work. The good ones have no score, or a score so obviously arbitrary that nobody minds.

[curious] What's the second?

[emphatic] Nobody has to be funny on demand. This is the one improvisers get wrong constantly, because being funny on demand is our whole job and we forget it's a specialised skill. A game that requires it excludes everyone but the confident three — and the confident three have a great time while the rest of the room watches and quietly resents them.

[contemplative] That's a status problem more than a fun problem.

[matter-of-fact] It's the same thing a cast needs before anybody will risk anything. Safety in the room. A party is thirty people silently deciding whether it's safe here to be silly, and one visibly bad turn answers that question for everyone at once. [short pause] Third test: it has to survive being joined late. Parties aren't synchronised. People arrive, drift off for a drink, come back. Anything with fixed teams or accumulated score locks out whoever walks in at nine.

[curious] And the fourth?

[teaching] It can stop without ceremony. If ending it requires a winner, it will overstay. The best games just quietly stop and nobody notices.

[curious] Give me the actual games. If I've got fifteen minutes and no plan.

[confident] Depends entirely on the temperature of the room, and this is the part people get wrong. The commonest mistake isn't picking a bad game — it's picking a good one at the wrong moment, which makes it look bad. [pause] Cold room, everybody's just arrived, nothing with turns. You want simultaneous answers. Stand Up If — somebody calls a statement, anyone it applies to stands. No turns at all, so it scales to thirty. Or split the room physically: call two options, people walk to a side. The only goal at this stage is that every person has spoken or moved once. That single fact changes a room more than anything else you can do.

[curious] And once it's warm but everyone's talking in pairs?

[warm] Now would-you-rather earns its keep. Two options that both cost something. The pleasure isn't choosing — it's being made to defend it. That produces a disagreement the whole room wants in on, which pulls the separate conversations back into one. Most Likely To does the same, and it's the warmest of all of them when the labels are kind, and the sharpest when they aren't.

[curious] What about late, when it's down to six people?

[contemplative] Best and Worst. Everyone gives the best and worst thing about their week. That's it, that's the game. And it reliably becomes the conversation people remember, rather than the loud thing you did at nine.

[curious] Are any of these secretly improv exercises?

[teaching] Half of them. Story One Word Each is one-word story with a drink in your hand. Fortunately, Unfortunately is a two-move status engine. Count to Twenty — group counts out loud, anyone may speak, nobody may plan, two voices at once and you start over — is a pure group-mind exercise, and a room that pulls it off is absurdly pleased with itself. [pause] What Are You Doing? is a workshop warm-up that civilians will play for twenty minutes without ever knowing they were training anything.

[curious] So they train the same skill.

[emphatic] Every single one. Producing something without first checking whether it's any good. That's it, that's the entire curriculum. And spontaneity is trainable — it isn't a personality you were issued at birth, it's a habit of not pre-screening. A nine-year-old wins these games because they haven't yet learned to work out how an answer will look.

[curious] What do you do about the person who won't play?

[serious] Leave them alone, genuinely, and make sure the room can see you leaving them alone. [pause] Pressure on one reluctant person is the fastest way to make the whole room self-conscious, because everybody else is now watching to learn what happens to somebody who declines. If declining is cheap and public, the room relaxes. If it costs something, the next three undecided people also say no.

[contemplative] And most of them aren't refusing the game.

[warm] They're refusing the performance. Take away the announcement, the turn-taking and the audience and they'll usually join something that never called itself a game.

[curious] Last thing. How long do you run it?

[matter-of-fact] Less time than people want. That's the answer and nobody follows it. A party game has one job — convert a room of separate conversations into one room. Once that's happened, it's spent, and every minute after is a subtraction. Stop while it's still working and the energy stays in the room as conversation. Run it until it fades and you have to restart the whole party from lower than where you began.

[confident] It's the same edit instinct you use on stage. You leave on the laugh. [short pause] Ten minutes is a long party game. The good ones are ninety seconds.
