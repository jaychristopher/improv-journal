[curious] I want to talk about the thing where somebody at a party pulls out a list of questions, and within about four minutes everybody wants to die.

[teaching] And the strange part is the questions are usually fine. Nothing wrong with them. They're just doing a completely different job than the person asking thinks they're doing.

[curious] Which is?

[matter-of-fact] Most lists confuse funny with fun. Funny is a property of the question. Fun is a property of the answering. A funny question gets its laugh on the way in and then leaves the person holding it with nothing to do. A fun question might not raise a smile at all when you ask it, and then produces four minutes of everyone talking over each other.

[curious] So the laugh at the ask is almost a bad sign.

[teaching] It's neutral. It's just not the measure. And depth isn't the measure either — people sort these lists by how deep the question sounds, which gets you nowhere. "What's your favourite colour" is shallow and no fun whatsoever. "What would you do with a year you weren't allowed to work" is enormous and also enormously fun. Depth and fun are basically unrelated.

[curious] Then what's the actual line?

[confident] A boring question asks you to look something up. A fun question asks you to make something up.

[short pause]

[teaching] "What's your favourite film?" — that answer already exists. Finished. Sitting in their memory. The only job is to fetch it and read it out loud. There's nothing to enjoy in retrieval, which is why the room goes quiet and somebody changes the subject.

[curious] And the invention version of that same question?

[teaching] "What film would you make everyone watch before they were allowed to have an opinion about anything?" That answer does not exist yet. They have to build it, in front of you, right now. And building something in front of people is — genuinely, mechanically — the part that is fun.

[emphatic] Which is the entire premise of what we do. Spontaneity is a skill, and the reason it's worth training is that it feels good to use. A question that gives somebody a reason to use it is doing them a favour, and they'll feel that even if they couldn't tell you why.

[curious] So the test is portable. If you could answer it accurately off a form you once filled in —

[matter-of-fact] It's retrieval. Change it until it couldn't be.

[curious] Okay, but there's a failure mode on the other side, right? Because "describe yourself" is technically an invention task and it's also a wall.

[warm] It's a wall. And this is the half people miss. A question is an offer. Something handed over for the other person to build on. An offer that's impossible to build on isn't generous, it's a demand.

[curious] Which is the same thing that happens on stage when somebody hands you nothing.

[teaching] Exactly the same. Accepting an offer gets much easier when the thing you're given has edges. And this is the counterintuitive bit — constraints make invention easier, not harder. "You may bring three objects" beats "what would you bring," every time. The number gives your mind something to push against.

[curious] Give me some that have the constraint welded in.

[conversational] You have to teach a one-hour class tomorrow on any subject — what are you teaching? A stranger has to guess your job from your kitchen; what do they guess? You get a shop on your street, rent-free, for one year — what's in it? Somebody's making a documentary about you but they only have the last week of footage; what's it about?

[curious] And you can rescue a favourites question the same way.

[teaching] Attach a job to it. Not "what's your favourite song" — what song would you use to wake an entire building? Not "favourite book" — what book would you hand a sixteen-year-old who says they hate reading? Same subject. One is a lookup, one is a design problem.

[curious] Here's my worry though. Invention is the pleasure, but invention is also the cost.

[serious] It is. And that's worth saying out loud rather than letting somebody discover it at their own expense. Ask a table of five to make something up and one of them will be visibly slower. Not less imaginative — slower. Invention speed varies enormously, and the quick ones set the pace without meaning to.

[curious] So that person now picks between saying something they're not happy with and holding everyone up.

[matter-of-fact] Neither of which is fun, which is the only thing the format promised to deliver.

[curious] Fixable?

[warm] Four moves, and none of them is a different question. First — pass, yourself, early. A pass nobody has modelled reads as a confession. A pass the asker took two minutes ago reads as a rule.

[short pause]

[teaching] Second, answer your own first when you can see somebody stuck. It buys them ten seconds and it shows the level, which is usually the real problem — people don't freeze on the question, they freeze on not knowing how big an answer is wanted.

[curious] Third?

[emphatic] Take the flat answer without rescuing it. "Oh come on, you can do better than that" is meant warmly and lands as a mark. Accept the short version. Respond to it as though it were the full one. The next round costs them less.

[curious] And fourth.

[matter-of-fact] Change the format, not the question. If someone's struggling, stop asking for invention. Would-you-rather is a middle setting — a choice to defend rather than a thing to conceive. And if you want the quiet one talking, ask them something they already know the answer to. Retrieval is free.

[contemplative] The principle underneath all of it: a fun question is only fun while answering it is optional. The moment invention becomes compulsory, you're running a performance review with snacks.

[curious] What kills a good run outright?

[teaching] Three things. Questions with a correct answer — the group can be graded, so it stops being play and becomes a test. Questions that require a confession — a fine conversation, but not this one, and dropping it in flattens a light evening in a single move. And questions you haven't answered yourself. If you'd stall on it, they'll stall on it. You're hoping for discovery, the answer neither of you saw coming, and that doesn't arrive while one person is conducting an interview.

[curious] So what actually decides whether the evening works?

[warm] The ten seconds after somebody answers. Yes-and is the whole difference between a list of questions and a conversation. The failure mode is the round-robin — everyone answers in turn, nobody responds to anybody, and it quietly becomes admin.

[emphatic] So follow the interesting part, not the list. If an answer contains something better than what you asked, abandon the list on the spot. It's already done its job.

[curious] And if you're the one who freezes on your own turn?

[confident] That's not a personality trait. That's the planning habit, and it's trainable. One-word story is the shortest way in — you contribute a single word at a time to a story nobody is steering. Planning becomes impossible, so invention becomes automatic.

[contemplative] Which is the same thing a good question does to you, just with better snacks.
