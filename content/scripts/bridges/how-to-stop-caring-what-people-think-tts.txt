[curious] I want to start with the paradox, because it sounds like nonsense until it doesn't. Improv performers care enormously about the audience and don't care at all what the audience thinks of them. How are both true?

[teaching] Because those are two different kinds of caring wearing the same word. Caring about the audience means tracking what's landing — is this scene alive for them, are they with us. Caring what they think of me means running a private evaluation of my own standing while the scene happens without me. One points outward. One points at a mirror.

[curious] And the second one is the thing everyone's trying to fix when they say they want to stop caring what people think.

[matter-of-fact] Right. And the standard advice for it is useless in two flavours. "Just don't care" — which is telling an insomniac to just sleep. Or "their opinion doesn't matter" — which is straightforwardly false. Social feedback is how we navigate reality. Neither one touches the mechanism.

[curious] So what's the mechanism?

[teaching] You're performing for an internal audience instead of engaging with the real one. Improv has a name for the symptom — performing cleverness. You're playing to an imaginary critic in your head rather than responding to what's actually in front of you. And the critic gets a look at every choice before it's allowed out. Is this funny enough. Will they judge me. Is this good.

[emphatic] And the result is identical on stage and in a Tuesday meeting. Everything you say is pre-filtered, sanitised, and late. The authentic response gets swapped for the calculated one.

[contemplative] Here's the cruel part. The calculated one is almost always less interesting, less connecting, and less likeable than what you filtered out.

[curious] Which people can feel.

[matter-of-fact] They can feel it, they usually can't name it. Performing for someone versus being with them. The polished version reads as fake even when the content is flawless. The stumbling version reads as real.

[curious] Okay, but before we go further — why do we care this much in the first place? It feels disproportionate.

[teaching] Because for almost all of human history being cast out of the group killed you. The machinery that tracks your standing in it runs continuously, cheaply, and without asking your permission. Caring what people think isn't a defect. It's a working sense doing the job it was built for.

[serious] Which is why "stop caring" is such bad instruction. You're being asked to disable a system that isn't broken. Nobody's ever managed it. The attempt mostly just adds a second layer of self-criticism about still caring.

[curious] So if the caring isn't the miscalibrated part — what is?

[teaching] The estimate. People wildly overrate how much others notice them and how long it stays noticed. There's a study where participants were made to wear an embarrassing t-shirt and then guess how many people had clocked it. They guessed roughly double the real number.

[emphatic] That's the useful finding, because an estimate you can correct. An instinct you can't switch off. You are not the main character in anybody else's evening. And the people you're worried about are, almost without exception, running the identical process about themselves — which is also why the room feels so much more attentive than it is.

[curious] So the goal isn't caring less. It's carrying a more accurate number.

[matter-of-fact] And performers arrive at that number by experiment, not by reassurance. Three moves.

[teaching] First — replace the internal audience with the real one. The imagined opinion is always worse, because your threat system is built to overestimate danger. In a scene, that means tracking what your partner is actually giving you. Their words, their emotion, what their body's doing. In life it means listening to what someone's saying instead of monitoring how they might be judging you. Both are caring. Only one produces information.

[curious] Second?

[teaching] Commit before you evaluate. The fear lives in the gap between impulse and expression — you have a thought, you assess it, and then you either water it down or bin it. Performers shrink that gap to nothing. Choose before your evaluator has time to intervene.

[curious] That sounds like "don't think before you speak," which seems like terrible advice.

[emphatic] It's not that. It's: stop thinking about what they'll think, start thinking about what's true. There's a difference between checking whether something is honest and checking whether it's safe. The first one is wisdom. The second one is fear in a good coat.

[curious] And third.

[teaching] Accumulate evidence that you survive being seen. Underneath all of this is a catastrophe prediction — if they see the real me, something terrible happens. The only thing that weakens a prediction is contradicting data. Improv delivers it in concentrated doses. Every class, every show, you're unscripted and exposed. And every time you survive it — which is every time — the prediction loses a little grip.

[curious] What does a rep look like without a stage?

[matter-of-fact] Small, and deliberate. Say something before you've vetted it — start a sentence before you know where it's going. Two seconds of exposure, repeated, beats one large act of courage. Then remove the option to walk it back, because most of what reads as caring what people think isn't the silence, it's the hedge afterwards. And the hardest one — send the real emotional signal instead of the performed version. That one goes last.

[teaching] Then one real rep a day. One unhedged sentence. One opinion without the disclaimer attached. Size doesn't matter. Frequency does, because what you're updating was built by repetition.

[curious] I want to push on one thing, because "stop caring what anyone thinks" produces people who are impossible to work with. Where's the line?

[emphatic] Improv draws it in a genuinely useful place. The audience isn't a judge, it's a co-creator — the third player in the scene, whose response is information about what's landing. A performer who ignored the room entirely wouldn't be brave. They'd be bad at the job. What performers learn to ignore isn't the room. It's the imagined room — the internal committee assembled from every past humiliation, which isn't present, has no information, and never updates.

[curious] How do I tell them apart in the moment?

[matter-of-fact] Three questions. Is this person actually in the conversation — real feedback comes from someone who saw the thing, the internal audience never has. Is it about the work or about my standing — "that explanation lost me" is signal, "they must think I'm an idiot" is a prediction with no source. And: would I act differently if it were true? If yes, it's information. If no, it's threat-response wearing the costume of feedback.

[curious] Last one. Doesn't confidence fix all of this eventually?

[serious] Backwards, and waiting for it is how people lose years. Confidence is a description of how it feels after the caring loosens. It's not the lever. The lever is evidence — do the exposing thing, nothing bad follows, and the part of you predicting disaster gets a data point it can't argue with. Enough of those and what arrives is the feeling people call confidence. In that order. Never the other one.

[contemplative] And you'll never stop caring. You're a social animal, other people's perceptions are real information. The move is redirecting it — from "do they approve of me" to "am I being real with them."

[warm] The first question has no good answer. That's why it eats you. The second one always does.
