[curious] I want to talk about prompts. Because every list I've ever found is the same list — a dentist who's afraid of teeth, a wedding where nobody knows the bride. And they read really well.

[teaching] They read well and they play terribly. And there's a reason that isn't just taste. A premise like that is a joke that's already happened. You hand it to two players and the only thing left for them to do is illustrate it for three minutes. That's not improvising. That's a recital.

[curious] So what should a prompt actually give you?

[matter-of-fact] Room. A prompt's job is to give two people the same starting image at the same instant, and then get out of the way. Which means specific beats general — "a place" is nothing, "laundromat" is something, "your grandmother's attic" is better than both because it arrives with a smell and light and a dead person's belongings in it.

[curious] Attic beats laundromat because it's got the emotional freight already loaded.

[teaching] Right. And that's the second criterion — personal beats clever. If you're taking suggestions from a room, "give me a word" invites people to be funny in public, which is exactly the trap you're trying to avoid onstage. Ask for the thing instead. "What did you do on your last day off." "What's in your kitchen drawer." "What's something you're avoiding this week."

[curious] Those get answers instantly.

[matter-of-fact] Because they require memory, not invention. Nobody has to be clever to answer them.

[curious] Okay but here's the one I want to fight about. Relationship prompts. Nobody lists them.

[emphatic] Nobody lists them and they're the most useful kind by a mile. Base reality needs four things — character, relationship, objective, where. Relationship is the one that generates the other three for free. Give two players a relationship with something already in it and the scene starts itself.

[curious] Give me one.

[teaching] Two siblings clearing out their mother's house. [short pause] You already know where you are. You already know what you're doing with your hands. And there is something underneath it that neither of you has said yet.

[curious] Another.

[teaching] Best friends, one of whom just got the job the other applied for. A driving instructor and a student who's forty. A coach and the player they're dropping. Someone and their ex's new partner. Every one of those has a scene in it before anybody opens their mouth.

[contemplative] The thing they share is that there's tension you don't have to invent. It's structural. It's already there.

[curious] What about first lines? People say "just start with a line" and I never know what that means.

[matter-of-fact] It means a line to say, not a situation to build toward. "She has your eyes." "You said you'd stopped." "I found the receipts." "Nobody's used this room since." You say it and then you find out what it meant.

[curious] That's the part that scares people. You don't know yet.

[warm] You don't, and that's the whole job. And the obvious choice rule matters more here than anywhere. The first line that occurs to you is connected to the scene. The second one is connected to your ego.

[curious] Say more about that.

[teaching] The second line is the one where you've noticed you're being watched. You've had a beat to think "is this good enough," and the answer to that question is never a scene, it's a bit. The first thought came from the same place the scene is going to come from. Trust it.

[curious] Let's go back to the absurd ones, because I want to know why they fail structurally, not just why you don't like them.

[serious] Base reality shouldn't be funny, or weird, or absurd. When it is, the grounding step got skipped — and now there's nothing for the strange thing to be strange against. No contrast means nothing can escalate. Every heightening move lands at exactly the same volume as the one before it.

[curious] So the scene is flat from the top.

[matter-of-fact] Flat from the top. And there's a bandwidth cost on top of that. Every offer costs your partner something to process. "Happy birthday" costs nothing. "I'm a cyborg from the future here to steal your toaster" demands five minutes of exposition before anybody can play. You've spent all the shared attention on comprehension instead of on each other.

[curious] Is there a version that works?

[warm] Play the people straight. A talking fridge is a bad scene. Two flatmates finally having the argument they've been avoiding for a year, while a fridge listens — that's a scene. And the fridge is funnier for being ignored.

[curious] [pause] Okay. Last thing. Once I've got a prompt, how do I not become its prisoner?

[teaching] Free-associate rather than depict. "Dentist" goes to cavity, to hollow, to empty, to loneliness. Your scene can be about somebody who's stopped answering their friends — sparked by "dentist," never mentioning teeth once.

[curious] Take the second thing it makes you feel, not the first thing it makes you think.

[emphatic] Exactly that. The first thought is a job or a place. The feeling underneath it is what the scene is actually made of.

[curious] And if my partner opens with something completely unrelated?

[matter-of-fact] Take the offer. Offers outrank suggestions, always. The prompt came from a stranger who's now sitting in the dark. Your partner is in the scene with you.

[curious] The failure I see most is people checking back. Mid-scene, they'll swerve to re-mention the suggestion.

[serious] Scenes die there. And they die for nothing, because nobody in that room is auditing you. The suggestion existed to prove the show wasn't written. Thirty seconds in, its whole job is done.

[contemplative] Which is the thing that carries off stage, honestly. The prompt is whatever you walked in with — the plan, the agenda, the thing you rehearsed in the car. It got you through the door. And then the person in front of you says something you didn't expect, and you have a choice.

[warm] Take the offer or check your notes.

[contemplative] Everyone can tell which one you're doing.
