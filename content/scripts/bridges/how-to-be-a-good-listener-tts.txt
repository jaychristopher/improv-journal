[curious] I want to start with the advice everyone gets. Eye contact. Nod. Paraphrase back. Put the phone away. What's wrong with it?

[teaching] Nothing, and that's the trap. Every one of those is producible while you're writing your next line. You can nod on schedule and say "so what I'm hearing is" and be, underneath, entirely somewhere else. It's a listening costume.

[curious] And people can tell.

[matter-of-fact] They can always tell. They usually can't say how. But they leave the conversation knowing something didn't land.

[curious] So what is it actually? If it's not the behaviours.

[teaching] It's where your attention is. That's the whole skill. Spolin called it receiving — taking in the complete communication, not just the verbal part. Words are maybe twenty percent of the signal. The rest is tone, breath, posture, pace, the pause before the sentence, the thing they almost said and didn't.

[emphatic] You know this from scenes. Your partner says "I'm fine" and their shoulders drop two inches and the pitch goes down. A player listening to words hears "I'm fine." A player listening hears the whole thing.

[curious] Okay, but I want to know what's stopping me. Because I do this on stage and then I go to dinner and I'm terrible at it.

[teaching] Three things, and they're all things you're doing on purpose. First one's the big one — you're composing your reply while they're still talking. Improv name for it is internal computation. The machinery running in the background eating the bandwidth you need to receive.

[curious] The fix is just... don't?

[confident] The fix is trust that a response will be there when they stop. It will. It always is. And it'll be better than the one you drafted, because it's aimed at what they actually said instead of what you guessed by sentence three.

[curious] Second one?

[teaching] Filtering for what's relevant to you. They mention a restaurant you've been to and your brain lights up — oh, I've been there — and now you're queuing. You're waiting for a gap. The listening went self-referential.

[emphatic] In scene terms: you're not tracking their offers anymore, you're tracking yours. And the restaurant probably wasn't the offer. The way their voice changed when they mentioned it — that was.

[curious] Third.

[contemplative] Evaluating. And this one's sneaky, because it isn't the obvious judgement. It's the low hum. Agree with that, not that. This bit's interesting. I'd have done it differently. Every one of those is a moment you stepped out of receiving and into assessing.

[warm] Spolin's version: suspend your own agenda. Not forever — you're going to respond, you're going to contribute. But during reception, your agenda is noise.

[curious] What about distraction? That one isn't on purpose.

[matter-of-fact] Right, and it splits into two things people call by one name. A phone isn't a small distraction, it's a specific one. Wickens found tasks interfere in proportion to what they share — same stage, same sense, same code. Doodling is motor and spatial, costs a conversation almost nothing. A screen full of text is verbal comprehension, which is exactly what listening is made of. It's not competing for your attention in general. It's competing for the precise part you were using.

[curious] So half-listening while I glance at something —

[emphatic] Doesn't exist. Cherry put different speech in each ear back in fifty-three and asked people to repeat one back. Afterwards they couldn't say what the other message was about. Not the content, not even the language. The unattended channel isn't running quietly. It's binned. You were switching, and losing the seam every time.

[curious] And mind-wandering?

[teaching] Opposite problem. Nothing's competing — there's slack. Speech comes in around a hundred and fifty words a minute and you can process faster than that, so the spare capacity goes looking for work. Attention doesn't idle.

[confident] Which means "concentrate harder" is the wrong instruction. Give it a job. Track what they're giving you, what's underneath it, what changed mid-sentence. There's no room to drift because there's no slack.

[curious] Give me something I can do tomorrow.

[teaching] Make the first word of your response the last word of their sentence. Not as a rule — as a focusing device. They say "and I just don't know what to do about my sister." You start with "sister."

[warm] It's silent. Nobody knows you're doing it. And it forces you to still be there at the end of their sentence instead of having left at the middle. Ten minutes of it will tell you honestly how much of your listening was queue management.

[curious] Is there a version of this that's too much? Where listening is the wrong move?

[serious] Three, at least. When somebody wants a decision, pure reception reads as evasion — they asked about the job and you gave them reflective questions, and they've concluded you're dodging. When you actually disagree, silence isn't neutral, it's read as agreement and it detonates later. And when someone's rehearsing rather than thinking — running a loop they've run forty times — attentive listening reinforces the loop.

[emphatic] The improv frame handles this cleanly, because listening and contributing were never opposites. The whole point of receiving an offer is to build on it. A scene where one player only receives is as dead as one where nobody does.

[curious] Last thing. How do you listen to someone who talks for twenty minutes?

[matter-of-fact] Stop trying to retain it. That's what makes long talkers exhausting — you're holding detail in working memory, you're going to lose all of it, and the effort shows up on your face as glaze.

[teaching] Track the shape instead. What do they keep coming back to. Where does the energy rise. Which part have they now said three times. The repeated thing is nearly always the point, and it's almost never the thing they announced at the start.

[contemplative] That's what two players do on stage every night. Fully tracking each other, responding to the complete signal, building something neither of them planned. From outside it looks like telepathy.

[warm] From inside it's just listening with nothing in the way.
