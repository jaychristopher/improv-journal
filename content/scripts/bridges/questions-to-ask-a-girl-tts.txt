[curious] Okay, I want to start with something that annoys me. There's a whole genre of content out there — "questions to ask a girl." Lists of three hundred. And I think it's the same failure I see in scene work.

[teaching] It's exactly the same failure. It's a script. Somebody walks in with a plan and the plan is about them, not about the person in front of them.

[curious] So what's actually going wrong? Because on the surface it looks like preparation. Preparation is good, right?

[matter-of-fact] Preparation is fine. The problem is the framing underneath it. A list like that says: this person is a category, and the category has a correct approach. Which is the same thing as saying the other person is a lock and you're looking for the key.

[curious] And people can feel that.

[emphatic] Within two questions. [short pause] You know when you're in a scene with someone who's already decided where it's going? You can't name it in the moment but you feel the door close. It's that.

[curious] Okay, so if the list isn't the fix, what is?

[teaching] Two things, and they're both about you, not about them. First one: you're asking and not answering.

[curious] Meaning what, exactly?

[matter-of-fact] Meaning you fire off ten questions and offer nothing. That's not a conversation, that's a survey. And people answer surveys accurately and reveal nothing, because there's no reason to. Nothing's been put in, so nothing's owed.

[teaching] Trust is an accumulated estimate. It's built out of small exchanges that go unpunished. But an exchange needs two people putting something in. One-directional questioning doesn't build anything, because there's no second party to the transaction.

[curious] What's the second failure?

[matter-of-fact] You're asking about them rather than about now. "What are you passionate about" — that requires a self-assessment before it can be answered. And self-assessments come out rehearsed. Everybody's got a canned one.

[curious] Versus?

[confident] "What did you nearly do instead of coming tonight." The answer's already sitting there. They don't have to construct anything.

[contemplative] That's an offer, isn't it. That's the whole thing.

[warm] That's exactly the thing. Offers — everything you say is something the other person can build with. So judge your questions by what they can do with them, not by how they sound when you say them. Which is a completely different criterion than most people use.

[curious] Because most people are optimizing for how the question makes them look.

[teaching] Right. And there's a name for the trap there. Obvious choice. Your first, plainest question is connected to the situation. Your second one is connected to how you want to appear. The plain one works better. Every time. Same as in a scene — the first thing you thought of was true, and then you got clever.

[curious] So there's one rule. What is it?

[emphatic] Answer everything you ask, at the same depth, without being asked to.

[short pause]

[teaching] And that's not a nicety, that's the mechanism. Every answer you give at slightly greater depth than the last one gives the other person permission to match it. The conversation escalates without anybody deciding to escalate.

[curious] There's actual research on this, isn't there? The thirty-six questions thing.

[matter-of-fact] Aron, 1997. Strangers escalated through thirty-six increasingly personal questions, reported substantially more closeness than a matched small-talk group. And two things get dropped every time it gets cited.

[curious] Which are?

[confident] Both people answered everything. It was mutual disclosure, not one person interviewing another. And the escalation did the work, not the questions — each answer made the next question askable. A list used one-directionally has neither property. So people cite the study as proof that questions make somebody like you, when the study actually showed that mutual escalation does.

[curious] Okay. Here's the thing I want to get to, because I think this is the part nobody covers. What about when it's your turn? When they ask you something?

[emphatic] That's the half everybody skips, and it's the only half that matters. Anybody can read a good question off a page. How you answer is the part that cannot be borrowed.

[curious] So what's the failure mode there?

[teaching] Hearing a question as a prompt to perform. She asked where you grew up. The answer is where you grew up — not a thesis about how it shaped you. [short pause] Answer the question that was asked, not the adjacent one you'd rather discuss.

[curious] Which is a support move. That's just supporting your scene partner.

[warm] It is exactly that. And the same rule about specificity applies. "I like travelling" is a category — there's nothing to do with it. "I went for four days and got stuck for eleven" is a thing somebody can pick up.

[curious] What's the one move that changes an answer most?

[confident] Include one thing you didn't have to include. The small unrequested detail — the reason, the embarrassing part, what you actually thought. That's what makes an answer sound like a person rather than a form. And it's the escalation, in one move. You've gone slightly further than required, and they can match it or not, at no cost.

[curious] And what about the "ha, what about you" reflex?

[matter-of-fact] Declining the offer. However friendly it sounds. Answer first, then ask.

[contemplative] There's a thing that happens when people run out of stuff to say. They panic and reach for a new topic.

[teaching] You haven't run out. You've run out of new topics, which is a much smaller problem — and the reflex to find another one is what creates the silence in the first place. Almost everything already said has more in it. They mentioned a job, a sister, a place they lived, a thing they nearly did instead. Any of those can be gone back into.

[curious] And going back into something signals you were listening.

[emphatic] Which a new topic never does. [pause] That's the whole skill, and it isn't asking. It's what you do with the answer while it's still arriving.

[teaching] Last word response trains it directly. Every reply has to be built from the other person's last words. Which makes drafting impossible — you physically cannot compose underneath them, because you don't know what the material is until it lands.

[contemplative] Most people trying it discover how much of their listening was preparation.

[warm] All of it. Usually all of it. [short pause] And that's the discovery, on stage and off — you weren't listening, you were waiting. The list is just a formalized version of waiting.
