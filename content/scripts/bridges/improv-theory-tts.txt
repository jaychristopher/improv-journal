[curious] Okay, so most of us learned improv the same way. Someone stood at the front of a room and said "yes, and," and it landed like it came down from a mountain.

[teaching] Right. And then years later you take a workshop somewhere else and a teacher says something that flatly contradicts it, and you assume one of you is wrong. Neither of you is wrong. You're just standing in different traditions.

[curious] How many are there?

[matter-of-fact] Five that matter. And they were built by people arguing with each other, mostly across generations, mostly without saying so out loud.

[curious] Start me somewhere.

[teaching] Spolin. Chicago, nineteen-forties. She wasn't training performers — she was working with kids and immigrants learning English. So she built games. Structured exercises with a physical focus, a point of concentration. Her bet was that the body knows before the mind does, and if you give the conscious brain a job — count the objects, keep the space object consistent — it stops interfering with everything else.

[curious] So the game isn't the point. The game is a distraction from your own brain.

[emphatic] That's exactly it. Side-coaching is hers too. The teacher talking to you while you're still in it, without stopping the scene. Every school in America does that now. Most of them don't know whose idea it was.

[curious] And Del Close comes after.

[teaching] Del Close came after and was furious. Because Spolin's method got carried into Second City by her son, and there it became a way of generating sketches. You improvise, you find the good bits, you script them, you perform the script. The improvisation was the rough draft.

[short pause]

[teaching] Close's whole position is a reaction to that. His claim was that the improvising *is* the performance. Not the rehearsal for one. And once you believe that, everything else follows — ensemble over star, form over bit, truth over joke. "Play to the top of your intelligence" isn't a motivational poster. It's an argument with a room full of people treating improv as a punchline delivery system.

[curious] And group mind.

[warm] Group mind, which sounds like mysticism until you watch it happen. He didn't treat it as magic. He treated it as a state with conditions — trust, presence, shared vocabulary, and the willingness to follow the group's intelligence instead of your own good idea.

[curious] Then UCB.

[matter-of-fact] Then UCB does to Close exactly what Close did to Second City. They trained in his program, they saw what worked, and they wrote it down. Game becomes the organizing principle. Find the first unusual thing, heighten it, explore it — if this is true, what else is true?

[curious] Which is why it spread.

[teaching] Which is why it spread. You can teach it on a syllabus. You can assess it. You can apply it under pressure at eleven p.m. when you're dying. That's not a small thing. And the cost is that it's drier than what it came from, and scenes start converging on a recognizable shape.

[curious] And then someone reacts to *that*.

[emphatic] The Annoyance. Napier's argument isn't with Close, it's with what happens when a set of observations becomes a checklist a beginner is running while trying to have a conversation. His method is three steps. Do something. Be affected by what you did. Do something else. That's it.

[contemplative] And TJ and Dave take it further — full hour, two people, no format, no game, nothing. Their line is that the scene already exists. You're not building it. You're finding it.

[curious] Where does Johnstone fit in that sequence?

[teaching] He doesn't. That's the useful part. He was in Britain at roughly the same time, arguing with drama school orthodoxy instead of with Chicago, and the two bodies of work didn't properly meet for decades. Which is why his vocabulary sits at an angle to everyone else's — status, blocking, the seesaw. Different fight.

[curious] And his version of yes-and?

[emphatic] He doesn't say yes-and. He says *be changed by what happens.* And that's not a synonym. You can yes-and someone all night and concede nothing. Agree out loud, stay exactly who you were. It looks like compliance and it functions like a block.

[short pause]

[contemplative] Being changed means you end up somewhere you didn't start. That's a much more expensive instruction.

[curious] Okay, here's what I actually want. I get notes all the time. How do I tell which tradition a note came from?

[confident] By what the note is *about*. This is the practical payoff of the whole thing.

[teaching] "What's the game here, what's the unusual thing?" — that's UCB. It assumes the scene has an organizing pattern you can identify and heighten.

[teaching] "Who's higher status, what are you doing with your hands?" — Johnstone. Physical, observable, about the transaction between two people rather than the shape of the piece.

[teaching] "What are you actually doing? Where are you?" — Spolin. Point of concentration. Attention pushed outward into the environment.

[teaching] "What did the group establish, what came back?" — Close. The unit being judged is the whole show, not your scene.

[teaching] And "stop worrying about it and just do something" — Napier. Aimed at somebody with enough technique to be paralyzed by it.

[curious] Why does the source matter if the note is good?

[emphatic] Because knowing where a note came from tells you where it stops applying. And nothing else tells you that.

[serious] A game note given to somebody who can't yet sustain a two-person scene is premature — you're asking for pattern recognition from someone who hasn't got listening yet. A "just commit, stop thinking" note given to a beginner with no discipline to loosen makes them worse. Both of those notes are correct inside the tradition that produced them. Neither is correct everywhere.

[contemplative] A note that sounds universal is almost always a note that was true somewhere specific.

[curious] Do they actually disagree, though? Or is it vocabulary?

[matter-of-fact] Both, and separating them is most of the value. A lot of the conflict dissolves when you look at it — several lineages describing the same behavior with different words and then arguing about the words.

[teaching] Underneath that there are real incompatibilities. Where comedy comes from. How much structure a performer should be holding. Those aren't terminological. Two teachers can watch the same scene, agree completely on what happened, and give you opposite notes about whether it worked.

[curious] What do they all agree on?

[warm] Four things, and they're unanimous. Listening is the foundation — real listening, not waiting. The planning mind is the enemy; they just attack it with different weapons. Commitment beats cleverness, every time. And the ensemble is bigger than you are.

[contemplative] Which means the disagreements are downstream of a shared question. How do people make meaning together, in real time, without a plan.

[teaching] Johnstone answers with power and vulnerability. Spolin with the body. Close with collective intelligence. UCB with pattern. TJ and Dave with patience.

[short pause]

[emphatic] Each of them found something real. None of them found all of it. And the improvisers who get very good almost always end up borrowing from traditions they never trained in.
