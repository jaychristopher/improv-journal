[curious] So there's this whole genre of advice about people skills. Make eye contact. Use "I" statements. Paraphrase what you heard. And none of it is wrong, exactly. But it never seems to make anyone better.

[teaching] Because it's a parts catalog. You can name every component of an engine and still have no idea how the thing runs. And communication is a running system. It's happening in real time, with mechanics that operate whether or not you can name them.

[curious] And improv is what — the discipline that reverse-engineered those mechanics?

[teaching] Sixty years of it. Not improv as entertainment. Improv as the practice of building meaning between two people with no script. Which means improvisers ended up with names for the parts everyone else can only feel.

[curious] Start with the biggest one.

[matter-of-fact] Offers. Everything is an offer. Any signal one person sends that another person could build on. Words, obviously. But also a gesture. A silence. A shift in posture. A sigh. A glance at the door.

[curious] Okay, but everybody in a class learns "everything is an offer" in week two. What's the part that actually changes something?

[teaching] The frame shift underneath it. If you think communication is messaging, your question is always: did I say that clearly enough? Am I being understood? All the attention sits on the sender. On you.

[short pause]

[teaching] If you think communication is offers, the question moves. Did I notice what they gave me? Did I build on it? Now the attention sits on the receiver. On what you caught.

[contemplative] So clarity is a property of what you sent, and connection is a property of what you caught.

[emphatic] And those are graded at opposite ends of the same channel. Which is why most communication breakdowns aren't failures of clarity at all. The signal went out fine. Nobody caught it — because the other person was listening for content and the offer was somewhere else entirely.

[curious] Give me the off-stage version.

[warm] You tell a friend about something at work. In the messaging frame, the question is whether they followed the facts. In the offers frame, the question is whether they caught the thing underneath the facts — the worry, the frustration, the ask for solidarity — and responded to *that*. You've had both conversations. You know which one you walked away from feeling met.

[curious] Second mechanic.

[serious] You cannot stop signaling. Even silence is a signal. Even stillness is a choice. On stage this is brutally obvious — you check out for two seconds, your eyes drift, your body says "I'm building my next line," and your partner sees it. The audience feels it. The scene goes flat.

[matter-of-fact] Watzlawick put it as an axiom: one cannot not communicate. Improvisers just get it beaten into them, because they're doing it under lights.

[teaching] The same thing happens in every conversation you have off stage. You're just not lit, so the signal loss is invisible. Nobody names it. They just feel slightly less met and don't know why.

[curious] Which brings us to listening. Which is the most-repeated and least-useful advice in the genre.

[confident] "Listen actively." Right. Improv actually breaks it into parts. Three of them.

[teaching] One — receive the *whole* offer. Not just words. Tone, status, physical energy. Partner walks on slouching and sighing and says "great, another Monday." The offer is not in the sentence. It's in the slouch. Responding to the words and ignoring the body is like reading the lyrics of a song and thinking you heard it.

[teaching] Two — stop computing. While someone talks, your brain wants to build your reply. And building it costs the exact same resource you need to process what they're saying. That's not a metaphor. It's the same bandwidth. So you get two people alternating prepared statements. We call it being in your head.

[teaching] Three — respond to what was actually offered. Not what you expected. Not the version that supports the point you were already going to make. Johnstone's observation: most scenes die because the improviser responded to the scene they wanted instead of the scene they got.

[curious] And that's a daily-life failure too.

[matter-of-fact] Every day. You ask how their day was. They say "fine" in a tone that means the opposite. You answer the word.

[curious] Say more about status, because that's the one people get squeamish about.

[teaching] Every conversation runs two channels at once. Content carries the information. Status carries the relationship — who's leading, who's deferring, who has permission to say what, how much distance is in the room.

[matter-of-fact] It's not dominance. It's behavioral. Stillness, direct eye contact, finished sentences, comfortable with a pause — that's high. Fidgeting, breaking eye contact, "this might be a stupid idea, but," rushing to fill silence — that's low.

[emphatic] And here's the useful bit: most communication problems are status problems wearing a content costume. Somebody says "I disagree with this approach" and the room tightens — not because the disagreement is hard, but because a status transaction just happened. A couple argues about the dishes and it is never about dishes.

[contemplative] Reading it doesn't mean working it.

[warm] Right. Seeing the status channel just means you finally see what the conversation is actually about. Fights that looked irrational turn out to be perfectly logical. They were just about something else.

[curious] Last one. Honesty. Which sounds like a moral instruction.

[teaching] It's mechanical. On stage, "be honest" means don't perform what you think the room wants — respond from your real reaction to what just happened. And it works for a boring engineering reason. A fabricated response has to be built, maintained, and monitored against what you're actually feeling. That's three jobs. An honest one is already there.

[emphatic] More importantly, honest responses are congruent. Words match body matches tone matches timing. Your partner is processing all four channels simultaneously. Aligned, it feels effortless. Misaligned — mouth says "I'm fine," shoulders say "I'm drowning" — they receive a contradiction. They won't name it. They'll experience it as distrust.

[matter-of-fact] That's the actual Mehrabian finding, by the way. Not "ninety-three percent of communication is nonverbal." It's: when the channels conflict, people believe the body.

[curious] So if someone changes one thing this week.

[serious] Stop drafting your reply while the other person is still talking.

[short pause]

[emphatic] It's the most expensive habit in this entire domain and nearly everyone does it. It guarantees you're working from what they said thirty seconds ago instead of what they're saying now. It makes your responses land a half-degree wrong in a way people feel and never mention. And it eats the exact capacity every other technique needs.

[curious] And the cost of dropping it?

[warm] Tolerating about two seconds of not knowing what you're going to say. That's it. That's the whole price.

[contemplative] Which reframes the thing people ask most — why does this feel harder for me than for everyone else. It's usually not aptitude. It's load. If you're tracking what you said, how it landed, what's next, and how you're coming across, you're running four processes while they're running one. They're not better at conversation. They're spending less on it.

[confident] And that's good news, because capacity is reclaimable. You drop processes. You get the bandwidth back. And you spend it on the only thing that ever mattered — noticing what the person in front of you just offered.
