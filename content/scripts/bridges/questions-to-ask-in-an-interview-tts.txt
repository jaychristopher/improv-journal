[curious] I want to start with something you said that I haven't stopped thinking about. That in an interview, your questions get read more carefully than your answers.

[teaching] Because answers are prepared. Everyone in the room knows they're prepared. You rehearsed the story about the difficult stakeholder, they've heard four versions of it today. But your questions? Nobody scripts those well enough to hide behind. Your questions show what you noticed, what you think matters, and whether you understood the job well enough to ask something specific about it.

[curious] Okay, but here's where I get stuck. There's this power gap in an interview. They're deciding. I'm being decided about. How do questions change that?

[teaching] They're the only move you've got that does. Think about status the way we think about it on stage — it's complementary. One person's position goes up, the other's comes down. And an interview opens with that gap wide open by structure, not by mood. Everybody can be lovely to each other and the gap is still there.

[matter-of-fact] So the question is what your line does to it.

[curious] Say more.

[teaching] Some questions confirm the gap. "Do you think I'd be a good fit?" "How did I do?" "Is there anything about my background that concerns you?" [short pause] Every one of those is asking them to evaluate you a second time, out loud, while you sit there. That's the lowered position doing exactly what the lowered position does. And it invites an answer neither of you wants said in the room.

[emphatic] Other questions close the gap. Not by being aggressive — by repositioning you as someone deciding whether this is worth your time. Which is the actual truth. A hire is a mutual bet. Asking as though that's true is what makes it true.

[contemplative] It's the same thing as playing status on stage. You don't get high status by announcing you have it. You get it by behaving as though the thing you're claiming is already settled.

[curious] There's a second mechanic in here too, isn't there. Something about how the question is built.

[teaching] Initiation. Making a choice that implies the world rather than asking someone else to supply it. And the test is dead simple — does your line assert a reality, or defer to theirs?

[matter-of-fact] "What's the culture like?" defers. You've handed them a blank stage and asked them to build it. So you get the brochure. Of course you do — you gave them nothing to be specific about.

[teaching] "What do people find hardest about their first six months here?" asserts. Something is hard. That's obviously true everywhere, so it costs nothing to claim it, and now you've given them permission to say what it is.

[curious] It's an offer instead of a question.

[teaching] It's an offer. And people accept offers. That's the whole mechanic.

[curious] So if I get three questions, what are they?

[matter-of-fact] "What is the problem you're hoping this hire solves?" The job description describes a role. This asks for the brief. The answer is always more specific than anything written down, and it's what you'd actually be judged on.

[teaching] "Was this role created, or is it a replacement — and what happened to the person before?" Highest information per word available to a candidate. New role means the expectations aren't defined yet. Replacement means there's a story. [short pause] And how readily they tell you that story is itself the answer.

[emphatic] "What would make you say, a year from now, that this hire went unusually well?" Success criteria, spoken aloud, by the person who'll apply them. If they can't answer, nobody's decided, and you'd be walking into a job whose definition is still open.

[curious] And you said something about asking the same one to multiple people.

[emphatic] That's the move almost nobody makes. Ask the recruiter, the hiring manager, and a future peer what the hardest part of the job is. Consistent answers mean the organisation knows itself. Contradictory answers tell you more than any single answer could.

[contemplative] It's triangulation. Same as watching a scene from three seats.

[curious] What about the end? "Do you have any questions for us."

[teaching] That's the only part of an interview you can prepare exactly. And it's the last thing in the room before they start comparing you to somebody else — recency is doing real work. Whatever you ask is disproportionately what they remember when they talk about you afterwards.

[serious] So "no, I think you've covered everything" is not efficiency. It reads as low investment. Against a candidate who asked something specific, that's the difference in a close call.

[matter-of-fact] Two is right. Four is too many. One signals politeness, two signals interest, past three you've moved from interested to auditing them.

[curious] And if it genuinely has all been covered?

[teaching] Say so and ask anyway. "You've answered most of what I had — can I ask the one thing I'd still want to know?" You acknowledge the conversation and you buy the question. Much stronger than inventing something or bowing out.

[curious] Is there one at the end that's worth the nerve?

[contemplative] "What's your biggest hesitation about me, and can I address it now?"

[curious] Wait — that sounds like exactly what you told me not to ask.

[teaching] It's the one exception, and it works because it isn't asking for reassurance. It's asking for an objection so you can answer it. Completely different move. [pause] But it takes nerve, which means the relevant thing isn't technique, it's being brave. Only ask it if you can hear the answer without flinching.

[curious] What's the failure I should watch for?

[matter-of-fact] The good question evaporating. Forty minutes in, under pressure, it's gone — and you ask about next steps because it's the only thing left in your head.

[teaching] So write them down. Take the paper out where they can see it. Candidates think it looks unprepared; it reads as the opposite. Interviewers take notes and nobody finds that strange. Ending that asymmetry is worth doing.

[curious] Last thing. What do I listen for in their answers?

[serious] What they avoid. A fast, general, cheerful answer to a specific question has usually been dodged, and that's data. Safety in the room cuts both ways — if an interviewer can't say one difficult thing about their own team, you've just learned what that team is like to be inside.

[contemplative] And don't perform interest you don't have. Every interviewer has sat opposite someone reading from a list. [short pause] These only work if you actually want the answer.
