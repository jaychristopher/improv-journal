[curious] Okay, I want to start with the thing nobody says out loud. Why does networking feel so specifically bad? Not hard — bad. Like you need a shower after.

[teaching] Because everyone in the room is there to extract something, and everyone can tell. That's it. That's the whole thing. You're scanning for what you can get, they're scanning for what they can get, and the actual subject of the conversation is what each of you wants out of the other. No opening line fixes that. You can have the smoothest handshake in the building and it still feels like two people negotiating.

[curious] So all that advice about how to remember names, how to exit gracefully —

[teaching] Treating it as a skills problem. It isn't. It's a frame problem.

[conversational] And here's where you already know the fix, because you do it every Thursday night in a black box theatre.

[curious] Go on.

[teaching] An offer. Anything you hand your partner that they can build with. That's the unit improv runs on. And it turns out it's a shockingly exact description of what makes some people effortless in a room and other people exhausting.

[curious] Say more about exhausting.

[matter-of-fact] The exhausting person is the one who's taking. Every time. Not necessarily asking for a job — just taking attention, taking airtime, waiting for their turn to pitch. You leave that conversation with less than you brought.

[warm] Whereas the person everyone's glad to see is the one who hands you something. An introduction. A name of a tool. An answer to the problem you mentioned two minutes ago. The article that's exactly your situation. Most of it costs nothing. That's the reframe — not "be more confident," not "have a better pitch." Arrive with something to give, and give it without invoicing.

[curious] Without invoicing. Meaning don't attach the ask.

[emphatic] Meaning don't attach the ask. Because trust is an accumulated thing — it's an estimate built from lots of small exchanges. One event is one exchange. Whatever you were hoping to extract that night was never arriving that night anyway. So you might as well make a deposit and leave.

[contemplative] There's something almost freeing in that. You've already lost the thing you were anxious about losing.

[conversational] Right. Nobody closes the distance from stranger to would-refer-you in one evening. Nobody. So stop trying and the evening gets much easier.

[curious] Okay, practical. The part I hate most is the standing around. Everyone's already in a group and I'm just — orbiting.

[teaching] Look for an open triangle. Two people angled slightly apart have left a gap. Two squared off haven't. That's reading the room at its most literal — it's the same scan you do before you edit a scene, is there space for me here or am I stepping on something.

[curious] And if there's space?

[matter-of-fact] Approach from the side, not the back — enter where somebody can see you arriving. Wait a beat. Say the obvious thing. "Mind if I join" works fine. Then listen for thirty seconds before you contribute.

[emphatic] Thirty seconds. Joining a conversation by redirecting it is the fastest way to be resented. Ask about what they were already discussing, not a fresh topic. They chose that one.

[curious] That's just yes-and.

[warm] It's exactly yes-and. You accept the reality that already exists in that circle instead of importing your own.

[curious] What about a closed pair? Two people really in it?

[matter-of-fact] Leave them. Two people in a real conversation are the last people on earth who want a third.

[curious] Openers. Give me ones that aren't dead on arrival.

[teaching] "What's the most interesting problem on your desk right now." "How did you end up doing this." "What does your job involve that the title doesn't suggest." "Who here would you want me to meet." Easy to answer, and they go somewhere.

[matter-of-fact] Two to avoid. "What do you do" requests one noun and has nowhere to go. And "so what are you looking for tonight" — that one names the transaction out loud, which is the thing everybody was politely pretending wasn't the point.

[curious] Let's talk about status, because I think that's the real machinery here.

[teaching] Status is complementary. One position rises, the other falls. So when you approach somebody as a supplicant — you want a job, an intro, a favour — you've taken the low position before either of you has spoken. The exchange is transactional from second zero.

[curious] And the fix isn't faking high status.

[emphatic] The fix is never faking. You can't play high status with an empty hand, everyone reads it. The fix is having something to offer, because then the exchange is genuinely level and you can both feel it.

[teaching] Three moves. Don't open with the ask — if there is one it comes at the end, or in the follow-up, never in the first two minutes. Don't apologise for existing: "sorry, I know you're busy, I'll be quick" lowers you and hands them your discomfort to manage on top of their own. And be willing to disagree, lightly. Total agreement reads as deference and it's boring. You're also deciding whether they're safe to be real around.

[curious] What about the exit? Leaving is the part that feels rudest.

[warm] "I'm going to go find a drink — it was really good to meet you." Complete. Kind. Nobody in history has been offended by it. Better version: introduce them to somebody before you go. Highest-value thing you can do all night, and it makes the exit gracious instead of abrupt.

[conversational] And leave while it's still good. Same rule as everything else.

[curious] Then the follow-up, which I'm guessing is where you're going to tell me the actual networking happens.

[emphatic] The event is not the thing. The event has never been the thing. Within two days or not at all — after that you're a stranger with a reminder. Reference the specific thing: not "great to meet you" but "you mentioned the hiring problem, here's the piece I was thinking of." Send what you promised. More relationships get built by the person who actually sent the link than by anyone's charisma.

[curious] And ask for nothing.

[matter-of-fact] Ask for nothing the first time. The estimate has one exchange in it. Withdrawing now empties the account. Then follow up in three months with something useful and still no ask. That's the entire technique. Almost nobody does it.

[curious] Does any of this actually work, though? Or is it folklore?

[teaching] It's measured. Granovetter, 1973 — people got jobs through weak ties, acquaintances, far more than through close friends, because your close friends know everything you already know. That sat on correlational data for fifty years. Then a randomised study across twenty million people on LinkedIn tested it causally, and weak ties do cause job mobility. But it's an inverted U.

[curious] Meaning the weakest ties aren't the best.

[emphatic] The weakest ties aren't the best. The most useful people are the moderately weak ones — roughly the people you share about ten connections with and rarely speak to.

[contemplative] Which is unflattering to how everyone networks. Collecting strangers is the far end of the curve where returns fall off. Reconnecting with people you already half-know is the productive end. And nobody does it, because it doesn't feel like work.

[curious] Last thing. The objection I always hit: what if I genuinely have nothing to offer?

[warm] You're reading "offer" as a job, a budget, an introduction to someone important. The actual currency in that room is much smaller. An answer to a question somebody just asked. The name of a tool that solves the thing they described. Introducing two people standing four feet apart who'd like each other. Attention paid properly to somebody who's been talked over all evening.

[emphatic] And if you're junior you have one thing senior people cannot buy — an accurate account of what it's actually like to do the job they're hiring for. That's genuinely valuable and no junior person believes it.

[teaching] If some night you truly have nothing, ask well and take a real interest. Making the other person's conversation go better than it would have gone without you — that's supporting your partner. That's the whole discipline. It's the same one that makes you good on stage.
