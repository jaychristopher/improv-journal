[curious] Can we talk about the plateau? Because I think everyone hits it and nobody names it properly.

[teaching] Name it however you want, the shape is always the same. You've got the vocabulary. You know what Yes And is, you can spot a game, you know when someone blocked you. And your scenes are still hit and miss, and the hits feel like luck.

[curious] So what do people do about it?

[matter-of-fact] More. More classes, more shows, more reps. Volume. Which is the instinct, and it's wrong, because volume without direction is just repetition. You get better at doing the thing you're already doing.

[curious] What's the alternative?

[teaching] Diagnosis before treatment. And the problem is improvisers describe their problems in a way that makes diagnosis impossible. "I'm in my head." That's not a diagnosis. That's telling a doctor you feel bad.

[curious] Okay but I say that. Everyone says that. What does it actually mean?

[teaching] It means at least three different things, and they have three different fixes. One: you're planning your next line instead of listening. That's an attention problem. Two: you're evaluating your choices as you make them instead of committing. That's a judgment problem. Three: you're trying to be clever instead of honest. That's a performance problem.

[short pause]

[teaching] Attention problems get fixed with listening drills. Judgment problems get fixed with commitment drills. Performance problems get fixed by lowering your standards and raising your honesty. Nothing about the word "clever" is a technique.

[curious] Do the same three-way splits work for the other complaints?

[confident] Every time. "I can't find the game" — either you're hunting for something unusual when the game was already in the first unusual thing that happened, or you found it and you're scared to heighten it, or you're trying to impose a game instead of noticing one. Recognition, commitment, control. Different fixes.

[curious] And connection?

[matter-of-fact] "I'm not connecting with my partner" is almost always: you're not listening, you're waiting for your turn. Sometimes it's that you're both initiating and neither of you is receiving — two people making offers into the air. Sometimes it's that you're playing a character *at* your partner instead of *with* them.

[curious] So once you've got the diagnosis — you drill it?

[teaching] In isolation. Like an athlete. A basketball player who misses free throws doesn't play more games, they shoot free throws. If your problem is listening, do Last Word Response for a week — every line you say starts with the last word your partner said. It's a focusing device. Most improvisers are composing their reply by the second word of their partner's sentence.

[emphatic] That drill is brutal, by the way. You find out how much of your listening was drafting.

[curious] And commitment?

[teaching] First Line Drill. Walk out, say the literal first thing in your head, build a character out of it. You're shrinking the gap between impulse and expression to zero. The first impulse is almost always better than the edited version, because the second thought isn't about the scene — it's about how you'll be perceived.

[curious] Here's my actual problem with all of this. Every drill you've named needs another person.

[contemplative] Yeah. And that's the real arithmetic of improv training. Three hours of class a week. A show if you're lucky. And then a hundred and sixty-five hours of your ordinary life. Any advice that only works inside those three hours is advice for a rounding error.

[curious] So what's left?

[teaching] Two things, and they're worth separating. There's a small set of genuinely solo drills. Space work is the good one — make a cup of tea with no kettle. It's not about mime. It's that an imaginary object requires continuous attention or it stops existing, so you cannot do it while thinking about yourself. Two minutes and you get a visible pass or fail.

[teaching] Or first thought out loud. Walking anywhere, name the first thing you notice, out loud, and keep going. No editing, no upgrading it to something more interesting.

[curious] And the second category?

[emphatic] This is the one people miss. The skills that matter most — listening, accepting, not planning ahead — don't need a stage. They need another person talking. And you're in conversations all day.

[short pause]

[teaching] Catch yourself planning. In any conversation, notice the exact moment you stop listening and start composing. That's the identical failure on stage and off, and noticing it is most of the fix, because you can't stop it by deciding to.

[teaching] Or take the last word — start your next sentence with the last word they said. Do that three times over coffee and you'll learn the same thing the class drill teaches, without needing the class.

[curious] Does any of that replace stage time?

[matter-of-fact] No. It changes what you bring to it. Which is the part three hours can't give you.

[curious] Last thing. What about the feeling that you're getting *worse*?

[warm] That one's real and it has an explanation. Recognition improves faster than execution. You learn to see a game arriving, spot a late edit, tell a strong scene from a busy one — and all of that gets sharper long before your responses get faster.

[emphatic] So the gap between what you can see and what you can do widens before it closes. The scenes aren't worse. The person watching them is better.

[serious] That is the single most common reason people quit at six months. Nothing is going down. Both lines are climbing. One is just climbing faster.

[curious] And how long does the rest of it take?

[matter-of-fact] Nobody can tell you in months, because it depends on reps, not on time elapsed. Anyone quoting you a number is describing their own life. What I can give you is markers. You stop composing your next line while someone else is talking. You can start a scene without having an idea first. You stop tracking the audience mid-scene.

[contemplative] And then the one that matters most — you can say, afterwards, what the scene actually needed. Because from there you stop hoping and start practising.

[pause]

[warm] Del Close told his students: you're not good enough yet, keep going. People hear that as cruelty. It isn't. It's the only honest thing you can say to someone about an art form worth being patient for.
