[curious] So a teacher asks you to run improv with a Year Five class. Thirty kids. What's the first thing you'd tell them?

[teaching] Don't say the word funny. Not once.

[curious] Really? That's the whole intervention?

[teaching] It's most of it. Think about what happens in your own class when somebody decides the assignment is be entertaining. They start reaching. They go up in their head, hunting for the biggest thing, and the scene dies while they shop. Now put that in a room of ten-year-olds who were told to stand up, not people who paid to be there.

[curious] The room splits.

[teaching] Instantly. Four kids decide they're the performers and start swinging for laughs. Four kids do the maths, work out they can't win that competition, and go quiet for the rest of term. And both halves have stopped doing improv. One's performing cleverness twenty years early, the other's just hiding.

[conversational] So what do you say instead?

[matter-of-fact] It's a task with a rule. Nobody has to be funny to pass a clap around a circle. You just have to look at one person and clap. That's it. That's the whole job.

[curious] And the laughs?

[warm] Arrive anyway. At things nobody was aiming at, which is the whole point, and you don't explain that bit. You just let it happen.

[curious] Okay, ages. What actually changes between a five-year-old and a fifteen-year-old?

[teaching] The constraint moves. With the little ones — five to eight — it's not confidence at all. Small children are frequently the least self-conscious people in the building. What they can't do is hold a three-part rule in their head while also inventing something.

[conversational] So one rule.

[matter-of-fact] One rule, physical, circular, nobody waiting. Pass the Clap. Zip Zap Zop — three words and a pointed hand. Sound Ball, where you throw an imaginary ball with a sound and catch it with the same sound.

[curious] Kids are better at Sound Ball than adults, aren't they.

[warm] Much better. Because they haven't learned yet to improve what they were given. You throw an adult a stupid sound and they hand you back a slightly better one. A six-year-old just gives you the sound.

[curious] What about scenes?

[matter-of-fact] Not before nine. And it's not that they can't — it's that a scene has no rules to fall back on. The child who doesn't know what to do has nowhere to stand. A game gives them somewhere to stand.

[curious] Nine to twelve?

[teaching] This is where it starts properly working, and it's also where self-consciousness shows up. Both at once. So the games need visible structure — a nervous kid has to be able to succeed by following the rule rather than by being interesting.

[conversational] Freeze Tag.

[teaching] Perfect for it. You always know what you're supposed to do next: take the shape you were given and justify it. The structure does the work. Same with One-Word Story — nobody's individually responsible for the story, so nobody can wreck it.

[curious] What about the wait? Thirty in a circle is a long time between turns.

[matter-of-fact] Split it. Past about nine, the wait is longer than their attention, and the far end starts a private conversation — which is a completely rational response, by the way. Two circles of fifteen beats one of thirty every time.

[curious] Teenagers. Here's where I'd expect the wheels off.

[serious] Teenagers can do every single thing on that list and most of them will refuse. For a reason that's genuinely correct: the social cost of looking stupid in front of these specific thirty people, at fifteen, is real. It's not a skill problem.

[curious] So how do you fix it?

[emphatic] You don't fix them. You remove the audience.

[curious] Meaning?

[teaching] Run everything simultaneously. Fifteen pairs mirroring at once means nobody's being watched — that's fifteen times the practice and none of the exposure. Then make passing cheap and public. Say at the start anybody can pass, and mean it. A group that's seen somebody pass with no consequence volunteers far more than a group that hasn't.

[contemplative] And that hesitation you see — that's not a note you give.

[serious] No. That's the thing. At fifteen it's a social calculation, not a skill gap, and coaching it as a skill gap tells them they're broken. It changes when the calculation changes. Not before.

[curious] What about when it just descends into chaos?

[matter-of-fact] Narrow the game. Don't raise your voice. Chaos in a room of children is almost never defiance — it's what happens when a game has more freedom in it than the group can hold, and they fill the gap with volume. So take freedom away. Fewer players, smaller space, thirty-second rounds.

[teaching] And a game that felt slightly too easy and stayed together is a better session than an ambitious one that fell apart. Because the one that fell apart taught the room that these games end in shouting.

[curious] The three kids who dominate?

[emphatic] Change the structure, not the children. The second you ask the loud ones to hold back and the quiet ones to speak up, you've handed the problem to the people least able to solve it and made everybody self-conscious. Use formats where turn-taking is built in. Or pairs — in a room of thirty in fifteen pairs, everyone's talking and nobody's performing for twenty-nine people.

[curious] Do you need drama training?

[matter-of-fact] No. It might help not to have any. These games were built for community workers and classroom teachers, not directors, and the instructions are the whole method. What you need is a willingness to play rather than supervise.

[warm] An adult standing at the edge with a clipboard produces performance-for-approval — which is exactly the reflex the games exist to dissolve. Join in badly and visibly. It's the most useful thing you can do all session.

[curious] Last thing. Head of department asks what this is actually teaching. What do you say?

[contemplative] Not comedy. It trains paying attention to another person under mild pressure, and responding to what they actually did instead of to what you had planned.

[teaching] Which is the same skill a child needs to join a conversation, work in a group, handle a moment they didn't rehearse. The comedy's a side effect.

[serious] And treating the side effect as the goal is the reliable way to lose everything else.
