[curious] Okay. Name one person who invented improv.

[teaching] Most people say Del Close. Some say Keith Johnstone. Almost nobody says the right answer, which is a woman who never set out to teach actors at all.

[curious] Viola Spolin.

[teaching] Viola Spolin. And here's the thing that reorganizes everything else about her — she didn't come from theatre. She came from social work.

[curious] Meaning what, exactly?

[teaching] Chicago, the 1920s. Hull House. She trained under a woman named Neva Boyd, in a program that used games and folk dance and storytelling as tools for social development. The question in that room was never how do we make better performers. It was how do we get thirty immigrant kids, some of whom don't share a language with the person next to them, doing something together.

[curious] So the games were never about performance.

[teaching] They were about participation. And then she spent years as drama supervisor for the WPA Recreation Project, inventing games to teach dramatics to exactly that population. Think about the constraint. Whatever she designed had to work on people who hadn't volunteered, had no training, and in many cases couldn't understand the instructions.

[contemplative] That's a brutal design brief.

[teaching] It's a brutal design brief and it's why the stuff still works. You know how people say improv games "translate well" to corporate away-days or classrooms or kids? They weren't translated. They were built for non-performers. The theatre application came second.

[curious] So what did she actually invent? Because "theater games" is vague.

[teaching] Four things, and they're still the entire method every improv class in the world runs on. First — the Point of Concentration.

[curious] Which is?

[teaching] Every game gives you exactly one thing to attend to. The ball. The space object. Your partner's exact words. And it's narrow enough that it occupies the part of your brain that would otherwise be watching you perform.

[emphatic] And then the behaviour the teacher wanted shows up as a side effect.

[curious] Say more about that, because that feels like the whole trick.

[teaching] It is the whole trick. Nobody ever gets better at being present by being told to be present. You've had that note. "Be more present." What are you supposed to do with that? Nothing. But give someone a task narrow enough that self-monitoring can't fit in the room alongside it, and presence just — arrives. Uninvited.

[contemplative] The skill is never the instruction.

[teaching] The skill is what solving a narrow problem leaves behind.

[curious] Okay, second thing.

[teaching] Side-coaching. Calling adjustments while the game is running instead of stopping to explain. "Slower." "Where's the weight." "Look at her."

[curious] Every teacher I've ever had does that.

[teaching] Every teacher you've ever had does that, and almost none of them know whose idea it was. And it is the single biggest practical difference between a class that improves and a class that just plays. Because the moment you stop the game to explain the game, you've pulled the player out of the experience and into their head, which is the exact place the game was designed to get them out of.

[curious] Third?

[teaching] Space work. Handling an object that isn't there. And her argument for it is sharper than the way it usually gets taught — it's not about mime skill. It's that deciding where the cup is, how heavy it is, what it does when you tip it, that decision cannot be made from inside your own head. It forces you outward. Physicality before psychology, decades before anyone else was saying that.

[curious] And the fourth.

[teaching] Evaluation. After the game, you don't ask "was that good." You ask the group: was the problem solved.

[contemplative] Which takes the teacher out of the judgment seat.

[teaching] Completely. The standard becomes something everybody in the room can see for themselves. Nobody's guessing what the coach liked.

[curious] So if she built all of it — why is Del Close a household name in improv circles and she isn't?

[teaching] Her son. Paul Sills. He's the mechanism of both facts at once.

[curious] How so?

[teaching] Sills co-founds the Compass Players in 1955 — first professional improvisational company in America. Then Second City in 1959. And throughout, he runs his mother's games as the training method. She came out to run workshops with Compass. From 1960 she's running improvisation workshops for the Second City cast directly.

[matter-of-fact] So the line from her games to the entire American comedy industry is short, direct, and familial.

[curious] Then what got lost?

[serious] What travelled was the exercises. What didn't travel was her reason for them. Second City needed material for revues. So a method built to draw participation out of non-performers became a method for generating sketches. Which is a much narrower use than she intended — and honestly, it's the source of most of what improv teachers have been arguing about ever since.

[curious] Does all of it hold up?

[teaching] The mechanics, essentially all of it. The Point of Concentration is a better account of how attention works under pressure than most of what's been written since. She anticipated the whole cognitive-load argument by about twenty-five years.

[curious] And what doesn't?

[matter-of-fact] The book. Improvisation for the Theater is organised for a teacher to pull exercises out of, not for a person to read front to back. If you've bounced off it, you weren't missing something — you were reading a handbook like it was a narrative.

[curious] So how should someone start?

[teaching] Not at page one. Pick one Point of Concentration. Run the game attached to it. Watch what it does to a room. Then go read the surrounding material, and it'll be obvious.

[curious] Last thing. Is there a way to run her games wrong?

[emphatic] Yes, and it's everywhere. Her games assume somebody outside the exercise, calling adjustments while it runs. Remove that person and the game flattens into recreation — a room having a perfectly pleasant time and transferring absolutely nothing into their scenes.

[contemplative] A great deal of bad corporate improv is Spolin games with the coach taken out.

[serious] The game was never the point. Somebody watching, and adjusting, while you're inside it — that was always the point.
