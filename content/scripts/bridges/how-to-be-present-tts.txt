[curious] Okay, presence. Everyone's got advice about this. Breathe. Notice five things. Feel your feet. And I've done all of it and still lost twenty minutes of a conversation staring at someone's face.

[teaching] Right, because those techniques are trying to quiet the mind using the mind. And the mind has opinions about being told to shut up. There's a more mechanical way in, and improv found it out of pure necessity.

[curious] Necessity how?

[matter-of-fact] Because a scene dies publicly if you're not there. Your partner says something, and you have to respond to what they actually said — not what you were expecting, not the line you had loaded. One second in your head and everyone watching can see it. So improv had to develop presence that works under pressure, on demand, in front of people. Not presence as a nice aspiration.

[curious] So what's the mechanism?

[teaching] Bandwidth. Your conscious processing capacity is small — you can hold something like four things at once. And at any given moment that capacity is split between two channels. External: what's actually happening. What they're saying, how the room feels, what your body's doing. Internal: simulations. What you'll say next, whether that thing an hour ago was stupid, whether they're judging you.

[emphatic] Presence is just when the allocation tips external. Absence is when it tips internal. That's the whole thing.

[curious] And you're saying that's literal, not a metaphor.

[matter-of-fact] Completely literal. It's zero-sum. You cannot plan your response and hear what someone is saying, because both draw from the same pool. One displaces the other. Every cycle spent simulating is a cycle not spent perceiving.

[contemplative] Improv has a name for the failure mode — internal computation. And once you know the symptoms you can't unsee them.

[curious] Give me them.

[teaching] The eyes defocus slightly. Looking at the person, not seeing them. Responses land a half-beat late, because you're processing old input. Contributions go generic, because they're coming from your model of the scene instead of the scene. And you start missing details — small ones first, a shift in tone, a flicker across the face, and then bigger ones.

[curious] And that's not laziness.

[warm] Not at all. It's your brain doing its job — predicting, planning, protecting you. Planning is genuinely useful. The problem is timing. When it runs at the same time as something that needs your eyes and ears, both get a fraction of what they need and both degrade.

[curious] So how does improv fix it? More discipline?

[emphatic] No — and this is the part that reframed it for me. Almost all presence advice is supply-side. Expand your capacity. Meditate more. Build the concentration muscle. That works, slowly, with practice most people don't sustain.

[teaching] Improv goes demand-side. It doesn't grow your bandwidth. It builds conditions that eat all of it with external processing, so there's nothing left to be in your head with. You can't retreat inward because the exercise won't let you.

[curious] Example.

[matter-of-fact] Word-at-a-time story. You and a partner tell a story, one word each. "Once" — "there" — "was" — "a" — "lonely" — "astronaut." You can't plan ahead, you don't control enough of the output. You can't evaluate your word, the next one's already coming. Your entire capacity goes to two jobs: hear what they just said, produce a word that fits.

[warm] Total presence. Not because anyone decided to be present. Because the architecture left no room for anything else.

[contemplative] Same with repetition. You sit across from someone and say what you see — "you're wearing a blue shirt" — they say it back — and it only changes when something genuinely changes. Nothing to plan. Nothing to be clever about. Internal computation has no foothold.

[curious] Here's my question though. I meditate. Reasonably consistently. And it hasn't transferred — I still lose the thread thirty seconds into talking to someone. Why?

[teaching] Nothing's gone wrong and you're not bad at it. Look at the definition — attention, on purpose, in the present moment, non-judgmentally. The first three describe a performer mid-scene exactly. No daylight at all.

[short pause]

[emphatic] It's the fourth where they split. Non-judgmentally, in the meditative sense, means noticing without acting on what you notice. A thought comes, you see it, you let it pass. The noticing is trained precisely by not converting it into action.

[curious] And on stage you don't get that option.

[matter-of-fact] Everything you notice has to become a response, immediately, in a way that changes what happens next and can't be taken back. Same faculty, with an output requirement bolted on. Which makes improv a worse practice for equanimity and a better one for staying present while somebody is talking to you.

[warm] So you've trained the noticing half thoroughly and never trained the responding half under load — because sitting practice deliberately removes the load. That's it. That's the whole gap.

[curious] Okay, here's the one that actually bothers me. Why do I drift in conversations I care about? With people I love?

[teaching] Because caring isn't the input. Attention isn't allocated by how much something matters — if it were, nobody would ever drift with someone they love, and everybody does.

[emphatic] What allocates attention is demand. Whether something is asking you for a response you can't produce automatically. A conversation you care about but can predict makes almost no demand, and the spare capacity goes where spare capacity always goes. Inward.

[curious] Which explains why it's worse with people I know well, not better.

[contemplative] Sharpened, yeah. With a stranger, every sentence carries information you can't anticipate, so attending isn't optional — the demand does the work for you. With someone you've known for years, you can predict the shape of most of it. And prediction is exactly what frees attention to leave.

[warm] The unwelcome version: drifting with the people closest to you isn't evidence you care less. It's evidence you know them. Which means the demand has to be supplied on purpose where it used to arrive for free.

[curious] Doesn't that get exhausting? Attending that hard?

[matter-of-fact] Less tiring than the alternative, actually. Running a conversation while monitoring how you're doing in it is two jobs, and the monitoring is the expensive one. That's what leaves you flattened after an hour of small talk. Attention pointed outward is one job. People come out of genuinely absorbing conversations energized — not because they tried harder, because they were only doing one thing.

[curious] So off stage, practically —

[teaching] Stop composing while they're talking. Instead, notice one specific thing about how they're saying it. Their pace, their energy, the odd word they picked. That consumes the bandwidth that would've gone to rehearsal. And your responses get more relevant, not less, because they're based on what happened instead of what you predicted.

[warm] And when you catch yourself gone — don't force it back with willpower. Increase the external demand. Engage a sense you weren't using. Give the bandwidth something to chew on and the chatter loses its grip.

[contemplative] The first thing that changes isn't the drifting. It's that you catch it. Which feels like failure and is the opposite — you can't notice from inside the drift. Later the drift lasts a sentence instead of a minute, and you're back before they've registered anything.

[emphatic] You've been completely present thousands of times already. An intense argument. A close game. A first kiss. The narrator went quiet not because you told it to — because there was no room for it.

[matter-of-fact] So the question was never how to become a more present person. It's how to build conditions where presence is the only thing left.
