[teaching] I usually say something like "unscripted theatre." And then I watch their face do the thing.

[curious] The Whose Line face.

[teaching] The Whose Line face. Comedians, a suggestion, jokes on the spot. And it's not wrong, exactly. It's just — describing surgery as people in masks using knives. Technically accurate. Completely missing what's happening.

[curious] So what is happening?

[teaching] Two people walk out with nothing. One says "I can't believe you ate the last of Mom's pie." And in one sentence a world exists. There's a mom. There was a pie. It mattered. They're siblings and one of them is furious. [short pause] That's an offer. And offers are the whole substrate. Everything on that stage is built from them.

[curious] And Yes And is what you do with them.

[teaching] Yes And is the most famous thing in improv and the most misunderstood. It doesn't mean agree with everything. It doesn't mean be positive. The yes is acknowledgment — I accept the reality you established. The and is contribution — here's something new inside it. She made it for Dad's birthday and you know that. Now there's a father, a birthday, a betrayal.

[curious] Which is easy to say and impossible to do.

[teaching] Because of the timing. Think about what you're actually doing in those two seconds. Listening precisely — not just words, tone, body, implication. Integrating that with everything already established. Generating something that accepts and extends. And delivering it like you believe it.

[emphatic] All four. Same two seconds. Not in sequence.

[curious] Which is why the moment you start writing your next line —

[teaching] You're gone. And the reality keeps moving without you. That's why presence in improv isn't a philosophy thing. It's structural. You physically cannot build a shared world with someone while your attention is elsewhere. The scene shows it immediately.

[conversational] Can we talk about the two shapes? Because I think knowing which show you're watching explains most of it.

[matter-of-fact] Short form is a run of separate games, each with a rule the audience gets told up front. Do this scene as a Western. Do it backwards. One of you can only ask questions. That's the Whose Line version, and Johnstone formalised it into Theatresports in the seventies. The constraint is the engine — the rule gives you something to push against.

[curious] And long form.

[matter-of-fact] One unbroken piece. Twenty-five, forty-five minutes, one suggestion. Scenes give way to scenes, things come back, and the pleasure is watching unplanned material turn out to connect.

[emphatic] And neither one is the beginner version of the other. They train different muscles. Short form rewards speed and clarity and playing a rule cleanly. Long form rewards patience and letting something develop.

[curious] Here's what surprised me when I learned it — how short the history is.

[teaching] Sixty years. That's the whole vocabulary. Viola Spolin in Chicago in the thirties, working with immigrant kids through a WPA programme, inventing theatre games because instruction didn't work and play did. Her son Paul Sills takes the games into professional theatre — Compass in fifty-five, Second City in fifty-nine. Johnstone arrives at the same territory independently in London, caring about status and spontaneity rather than comedy. Del Close and Charna Halpern make the turn to long form in the eighties. UCB carries it to New York in the nineties.

[contemplative] Everything you were taught in class was invented inside one human lifetime.

[curious] And here's the part I think trips people up. Spolin wasn't making entertainment.

[teaching] No. She was teaching. The performance tradition grew out of the teaching method, not the other way round — which is the reverse of what everyone assumes.

[conversational] And it's why the word does three different jobs now. Improv as a performance form — an audience pays, watches something that will never exist again. Improv as training — a skill, make the performer responsive. And improv as applied practice — people wanting to be quicker in meetings, less frightened of speaking, better at working with what they're given.

[emphatic] Now rank those by how many people take part. Applied is biggest by a mile. Performance is smallest. Then rank them by visibility, and the order flips exactly.

[curious] So almost everyone arguing about what improv is has only ever seen the smallest one.

[matter-of-fact] Right. And it goes wrong at the door. Somebody joins a class to get funnier and spends six weeks on exercises that aren't about jokes. Somebody gets sent by their employer and resents being asked to perform. A teacher who doesn't say which of the three the class serves loses half the room in the first hour. Not to difficulty. To a mismatch nobody named.

[curious] There's a related split that took me embarrassingly long to notice. Improv comedy and improv acting are not the same class.

[teaching] They're not, and they get advertised in the same words. Comedy descends from the Chicago institutions — game, premise, heightening, the mechanics of a laugh. Acting descends from Spolin and Johnstone — attention, impulse, being changed by your partner. Drama schools use it on people who'll spend their careers doing scripted work.

[curious] Same mechanics though.

[teaching] Identical mechanics. Both build reality out of offers. Both collapse when somebody stops listening. What differs is what gets corrected. A comedy teacher watching a truthful, unfunny scene says nothing got heightened. An acting teacher watching a funny, unconnected one says neither of you was actually looking at the other.

[contemplative] They don't disagree about how it works. They disagree about what to fix.

[curious] Last thing. The bit about it not being a metaphor for life.

[emphatic] It isn't a metaphor. Every conversation you have is unscripted. Every meeting, every first date, every exchange with a stranger — no script, building a shared reality through offers and acceptance and contribution. You're just doing it unconsciously.

[teaching] Blocking isn't a stage concept. It's what happens every time you dismiss or redirect what someone just handed you. Discovery is what happens when a conversation goes somewhere neither of you planned. Improv didn't invent any of it. It built a room where those dynamics run at speed with instant feedback, and then wrote down what it saw.

[contemplative] Which means the question was never whether you improvise. You do, constantly, all day.

[confident] The question is whether you're any good at it.
