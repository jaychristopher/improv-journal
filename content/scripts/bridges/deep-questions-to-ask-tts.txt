[curious] Here's a thing I keep running into. You ask somebody a real question — a big one, a good one — and what comes back sounds true and tells you absolutely nothing.

[teaching] And the reflex is to blame the person answering. They were guarded, they weren't in the mood, they don't open up. Usually it's not them. The question was built wrong.

[curious] Built wrong how?

[teaching] It asked for a conclusion. Take "what's your greatest fear." Think about what that actually requires somebody to do. Review their entire life, sort it, pick a winner, hand you the summary. And a summary is a performance. It arrives pre-edited, because editing was the work. [short pause] Now try: when were you last properly frightened? They're not summarising anymore. They're remembering. And memories come with detail, and detail is where the actual thing lives.

[conversational] That's a scene note. You just gave a scene note.

[warm] It is exactly a scene note. A character who tells you their philosophy is a character we know nothing about. "I've never trusted institutions." Fine. Sounds like a person. Isn't one. But a character who tells you they still won't drive past the old church on Handley Road — now there's someone on stage. Specifics are what make a person real, and it doesn't matter whether you're in a scene or in a car with a friend.

[curious] Okay, but I've asked good questions and still gotten the flat version back. So the question isn't the whole story.

[teaching] No. Depth isn't stored in the question. The same sentence lands as intimacy with one person and as an intrusion with another, and nothing about the wording changed. What changed was the conditions. [pause] And there are two of them. Trust, which is cumulative — it's an accumulated estimate of whether it's safe to be exposed around you specifically. Every honest answer that goes unpunished ticks it up slightly. Every one that gets judged, or fixed, or repeated to somebody else, drops it. It's not a switch you flip with a clever question.

[curious] And the second one is the one nobody wants to hear.

[emphatic] Reciprocity, and it has to start with you. You cannot ask another person to supply the openness on your behalf. A question that asks for more than you've offered reads as an interview, and people answer interviews carefully. Answer your own question — first, or immediately after, at the depth you're hoping for. Five questions asked in a row, unanswered by the asker, is not a conversation. That's a form being filled in.

[matter-of-fact] Which is why "tell me something nobody knows about you" fails every single time.

[serious] Every time. It demands the deepest thing available, first, with no ladder underneath it and nothing offered in return. It's a request for a confession from somebody who isn't confessing. [short pause] That's blocking wearing intimacy's costume. It looks like an invitation and it functions as a wall, because there's no answer that's safe to give.

[curious] You said ladder. Give me the rungs.

[teaching] Six. Specifics — what's on your desk, what you had for lunch. Preferences with reasons — not what you like, why, which is the first point where an answer reveals something. Memories, anything from before they were twenty, and that's cheap depth: everybody has one and nobody has to be clever. Then present difficulty — what's hard right now, and that's the first rung that costs something. Then self-assessment. Then the unresolved. Grief, regret, the things without a tidy ending. [pause] You can't jump to the top. What you can do is climb faster than most people, because most people never start climbing at all. And every rung gets easier if you've just answered it yourself.

[conversational] Where do hypotheticals sit on that? Because most lists of deep questions are really just would-you-rathers with a serious face on.

[teaching] They're a different tool, and they're good at what they do. But notice what a question that makes you think actually produces. A considered answer. And considered is the problem — thinking is the step where the answer gets shaped for its audience. Worse, the hypothetical asks about a person who doesn't exist. The you who'd face that situation. That's a lovely safe distance to speak from, and it reveals about as much as any other piece of fiction. [short pause] Excellent openers. Terrible closers. Use them to warm the room, then stop, or you get an evening where two people were interesting at each other for three hours and learned nothing.

[curious] So say it works. Say somebody actually tells you the real thing. What then?

[teaching] That's the half nobody practises, and it's the half that matters. A deep answer is a small risk somebody just took in front of you, and the next thirty seconds decide whether they take another one. [pause] First move: ask the follow-up. The first answer is almost always the version they've told before. The unrehearsed one is sitting right behind it and needs nearly nothing to reach. "What was the worst part of that?" Usually enough.

[curious] And the ways you can blow it?

[serious] Don't respond with more feeling than they showed — if you're visibly upset, their disclosure becomes something they now have to look after. Steadiness reads as capacity, and capacity is what makes somebody tell you the next thing. Don't escalate straight to a bigger question; that's extraction, not interest. Don't answer past their depth — going further than they did looks generous and works as pressure, because it quietly turns the thing into a competition about who can be more exposed. [short pause] And never, ever repeat it in company. One instance of something private resurfacing in front of other people undoes an enormous amount of accumulated evidence, and no apology puts it back.

[contemplative] Let the silence sit, too. The pause after a hard answer is usually doing more work than anything you'd put in it.

[warm] There's a two-minute drill for all of this, and improvisers already have it. Last word response — you can only start your line from the final word of theirs. It removes changing the subject as an available move, rather than just discouraging it. That's the entire skill. [pause] Because the failure mode isn't running out of good questions. It's reaching for the next one. If you find yourself doing that, the answer you just got almost certainly had more in it, and you left.
