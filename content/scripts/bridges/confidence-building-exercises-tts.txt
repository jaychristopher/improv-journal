[curious] I want to start with the thing that annoys me about most confidence advice. It's all about arranging your feelings first. Affirmations, visualisation, stand like a superhero in the bathroom for two minutes. Generate the state, then do the thing.

[teaching] And improv can't do that. That's what makes it interesting. The scene starts whether you're ready or not. Someone claps, the lights are on, and there's no window in which to become the kind of person who's ready. So the whole sequence gets reversed out of necessity.

[curious] Which produces what, exactly?

[matter-of-fact] It produces people who look completely assured and will tell you, if you ask, that they're still scared. Every time. They just stopped reading the fear as an instruction to wait.

[contemplative] That's the working definition worth holding onto. Confidence isn't the absence of fear, and it isn't a state you generate in advance. It's what accumulates after you've acted anyway, enough times, and nothing catastrophic happened.

[curious] So the problem isn't the fear. It's the waiting.

[teaching] It's the waiting. And here's the part people miss — they picture low confidence as one big frozen moment. The thing they didn't say at the meeting. The scene they didn't initiate.

[emphatic] But hesitation isn't one moment. It's hundreds of tiny withheld contributions, each of which felt completely reasonable at the time. That's what makes it invisible. Nobody notices themselves not doing something.

[curious] Okay. So what do you actually practise?

[teaching] Start with the most literal version. First line drill. A scene begins and you speak before you know where it's going. No planning window. Clap, talk.

[conversational] And it's genuinely uncomfortable, which is the point. The discovery is that the line doesn't need to be good. It needs to exist. Everything after it has something to work with. Nothing after it exists without it.

[matter-of-fact] Watch for the long inhale before people speak. That's the planning window sneaking back in.

[curious] What's the next one?

[teaching] No backspace scene. Nothing can be retracted, qualified or restarted. Whatever got said is now true and you build on it.

[emphatic] And this one reframes something. Most visible under-confidence isn't silence — it's hedging. The immediate qualification. The nervous laugh. The "sorry, that came out weird." Remove the option entirely and people find it a relief, not a pressure.

[curious] Because the hedging was doing work they didn't want to do anyway.

[warm] Exactly. Then status transfer. Play the same scene high status, then low, then shift deliberately mid-scene. Eye contact, stillness, pace, how much room you take up.

[teaching] This is the exercise that changes the whole subject. Status is social position expressed through behaviour, not title. And what people read as confidence turns out to be a set of observable behaviours — which are practisable in a way that feelings simply are not.

[emphatic] You do not have to feel high status to hold still and finish your sentence.

[curious] I notice people play high status as loud.

[matter-of-fact] Almost everyone does at first. It's mostly stillness and unhurriedness. Volume is usually the tell that someone's playing at it.

[short pause]

[teaching] Then there's blind offer — respond to an undefined gesture by deciding what it meant and playing it like you're certain. That isolates the actual skill. Because when people say they lack confidence, they usually don't mean they can't act. They mean they can't act without knowing it'll work.

[conversational] And one word at a time. Build a story with a partner, one word each. You literally cannot make it good on your own, so the standard you're holding yourself to becomes obviously absurd. A few sessions of that does more for perfectionism than arguing with it ever has.

[curious] What about public speaking? Everyone's advice there is calm down.

[emphatic] And everyone's advice there is wrong. The calibration performers actually aim for is nervous arousal *and* relaxed openness, together. Not the absence of activation.

[teaching] Fear and excitement have basically the same physical signature — raised heart rate, shallow breath, sharpened attention. The difference is substantially what you call it. Performers don't eliminate the arousal because the arousal is the fuel. They stop labelling it as a problem.

[curious] What about power posing? That was everywhere for a decade.

[matter-of-fact] It's a cautionary tale, not a technique. The original 2010 finding claimed hormonal and behavioural effects. A much larger replication in 2015 found nothing. [contemplative] What survives is the unglamorous version, which is the argument of this whole thing. Standing differently in a lift for two minutes changes very little. Speaking first in the meeting changes the next meeting.

[curious] Here's the practical problem though. Every exercise you've named needs another person.

[teaching] Real limitation. But the mechanism doesn't need a partner. What the mechanism needs is a small action taken before you feel ready, and a chance to notice nothing collapsed.

[conversational] So — act inside the hesitation. That pause where the decision to act gets quietly reconsidered into not acting? The window's about a second wide. Pick one thing a day you catch yourself about to skip and move while it's still open.

[matter-of-fact] Send it on the second read, not the fifth. Rereading past twice isn't improving the message, it's rehearsing the reaction to it. And the fifth-read version is always more hedged and less clear.

[teaching] Take the obvious option on purpose. When you catch yourself reaching past the first workable answer for a cleverer one — use the first. Obvious choice holds off stage too, and it's the cheapest available rep of choosing before you're certain.

[warm] And keep a list of the times nothing happened. This is the one everybody skips and it's the one that does the work. Three words a day. "Asked, fine."

[curious] Why does that matter so much?

[emphatic] Because confidence here is an accumulated estimate, and an estimate only updates on evidence you actually registered. You've been doing brave things for years and filing none of them.

[curious] Last thing. How long until it feels different?

[matter-of-fact] Sooner than people expect for the specific thing. Never in general. A few dozen reps of one behaviour — speaking first, making the offer — usually shifts the prediction about that behaviour inside a month. But it doesn't travel. The person who's become completely comfortable initiating scenes is often still avoiding the phone call.

[curious] And that feels like proof the confidence was fake.

[contemplative] That's the usual conclusion and it's wrong. It wasn't fake. It was specific. Specific is what confidence is.

[teaching] Which is also why the scoring system is only ever one question. Did you act before you were ready? Whether the line was any good is a separate question, and mostly irrelevant.

[warm] The feeling lags. You'll act more readily for a long time before you feel any different. That order isn't a sign it's failing.

[emphatic] That order is the mechanism.
