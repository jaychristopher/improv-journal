[curious] I want to talk about a thing that's been ruining my Tuesdays. I'll be in a meeting, or a conversation with my partner, or trying to talk a friend out of a bad idea. I have the facts. I lay them out clearly. And the person on the other side just... doesn't move. Or worse — they dig in harder.

[warm] That's the room you walk into every time you try to change someone's mind.

[curious] So why doesn't being right work?

[teaching] Because you can't see into someone else's model of the world. You only have your model of their model. And it's almost always wrong. That's the first thing to get honest about.

[short pause]

[contemplative] So I'm not actually arguing with them. I'm arguing with the version of them I built in my head.

[matter-of-fact] Always. Every conversation. The actual person is opaque. You're working from signals and inferences, sculpted by your priors. The sculpture is yours. Acting like the sculpture is the territory is how most conversations quietly fail.

[curious] Okay so even before I open my mouth, I'm at a disadvantage. What else am I missing?

[teaching] People defend their belief systems by default. Not as preferences — as architecture. The brain minimizes surprise; a coherent self-story is load-bearing for the identity sitting on top of it. Touch a structural belief head-on and the whole shape wobbles. The defense reflex is the body protecting the building, not the opinion.

[curious] So when I tell someone they're wrong about something they actually care about...

[warm] You're not arguing with a position. You're trying to remove a beam. They feel it as demolition because structurally, it is.

[short pause]

[contemplative] And it gets worse with smarter people, right? Like — they should be more reasonable, not less.

[teaching] Dan Kahan's identity-protective cognition work. People reason more skillfully against evidence when it threatens their group identity. The defense gets better with intelligence, because the intelligence is in service of the architecture. The smartest person in the room may be the one most able to construct a clever-sounding reason not to update.

[curious] That's brutal.

[matter-of-fact] It's the system functioning exactly as evolved. Coherent priors are what let you act fast in an ambiguous world. The same machinery that makes you reliable also makes you defensive.

[long pause]

[curious] Okay so the news is bad. But defense isn't the whole picture. Right?

[warm] Right. Beliefs are rigid at the core and malleable at the edges. Which one you're touching determines everything.

[teaching] The core is identity-anchored — what kind of person I am, what I believe in, what side I'm on. Touch it directly, rejected on contact. The edges are the implications, the adjacent cases, the way the belief gets applied. People will reason their way across the edges without noticing the ground move under them.

[curious] So most failed persuasion is core-aimed when an edge would carry the same content.

[matter-of-fact] Most successful persuasion is edge-aimed and only reaches the core through the person's own re-equilibration.

[short pause]

[curious] Three governing factors then — what makes a signal land?

[teaching] Framing. Stakes. Role.

[teaching] Framing first. A challenge to a core belief gets rejected on contact. The same idea, reached through the person's own reasoning, or arriving through a reframed context, passes through cleanly. You're not changing the content — you're changing the angle of approach.

[curious] Tversky and Kahneman proved that empirically in '81.

[matter-of-fact] Identical statistical outcomes — lives saved versus lives lost. Subjects' preferences reversed. Same numbers, different angle of arrival. The framing effect isn't a quirk of human irrationality. It's a measurement of how meaning gets constituted.

[curious] So framing isn't manipulation — it's recognition that meaning lives in reception.

[warm] Lakoff put it most clearly. You can't argue against a frame from inside the frame. You have to provide a different frame, and let the new web of inferences do the work.

[long pause]

[curious] Stakes — the second factor.

[matter-of-fact] The signal has to matter to them, in their terms, or it's noise. This requires modeling them — which you'll do imperfectly — but imperfect modeling beats none.

[curious] So I can't just say what's important to me and expect it to land?

[teaching] You can't even just say what's important to them — you have to say what's important to them in language they recognize as important. If you're using your in-group vocabulary, you're signaling you're outside their architecture. The boundary defense fires before any reasoning happens. You're now arguing against a frame they didn't even articulate.

[short pause]

[curious] Use their words.

[warm] Use their words. Their words carry their frames. Stay inside the conversation instead of pulling them outside it.

[long pause]

[curious] Third factor. Role.

[teaching] People improvise from their position to keep their story intact. If you occupy a role with conviction, others tend to bend around it. Not always — entrenched power, hard evidence, and pre-assigned roles all push back — but more often than you'd expect in ambiguous or status-fluid situations.

[curious] This is Johnstone's status work applied off stage.

[matter-of-fact] Right. Status isn't who you are. It's what you do. And it operates as a complementary system — when one person raises, the other feels pressure to lower. Pick the role you actually want to occupy, play it with commitment, and the room often bends to fit.

[contemplative] So conviction is a structural force, not just an emotional one.

[teaching] Commitment shapes the substrate. The same content delivered hesitantly bounces. Delivered with conviction it sticks — not because it's more correct but because the role you're playing has structural weight.

[long pause]

[curious] The deeper point is that social reality is co-constructed.

[warm] Not invented from nothing. Propped up by the players together. The hard lines people defend are usually narrower than they appear, and most apparent blockers yield to a shift in framing rather than a stronger push.

[teaching] Which is why this matters off stage. You're not breaking through their resistance. You're shaping the substrate you're both standing on. The room has more give than direct argument suggests.

[short pause]

[contemplative] So the practical move is...

[matter-of-fact] Model humbly. Frame carefully. Play the role you actually want to occupy.

[emphatic] The room has more give than it looks.
